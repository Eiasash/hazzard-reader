# Chapter 33 Study: Low Vision: Assessment and Rehabilitation

## Summary

**Recognize functional vision loss**
- Normal aging can reduce accommodation, contrast sensitivity and light/dark adaptation while increasing glare sensitivity (pp471–472). Under high contrast, visual acuity in older people without visual impairment shows only a **modest decline**; low light, lower contrast and glare produce much greater losses (p471, Table 33-1).
- Macular disease commonly impairs central vision; glaucoma can cause peripheral field loss, and cataract affects acuity, glare and contrast (p473, Table 33-2).
- Assess task performance, not acuity alone: field loss, contrast, glare, visual perception and lighting all matter (p475, p478).
- Rehabilitation may help with better-eye acuity below **20/50**, field loss despite intact acuity, reduced contrast or adaptation difficulties (p475).

**Assess and refer**
- Obtain the person's goals for reading, mobility, medication use, household activities and social participation (p475-476).
- Arrange low-vision eye assessment and task-specific device prescription, plus certified rehabilitation professionals for training and environmental assessment (p474-475).
- Evaluate acuity and contrast sensitivity; the book recommends the Pelli-Robson chart for practical contrast testing (p475).
- Ask about emotional adjustment, depression and family strain; offer counseling or support-group referral when needed (p475).
- Functional assessment should occur in relevant settings and include real daily tasks and safety (p477-478).

**Rehabilitation and environment**
- Match optical, electronic and nonoptical aids to the desired task; demonstration, instruction and practice are part of treatment (p478-481).
- Train fixation, scanning and use of remaining vision; a device alone may not restore useful reading (p479-482).
- Adjust lighting and control glare individually; use contrast, color, organization and consistent object locations (p482-484).
- Teach safe mobility and alternative sensory strategies; involve caregivers in learning the person's abilities and devices (p479, p484-485).
- Discuss available rehabilitation funding and services; the chapter describes U.S. rehabilitation, Veterans Health Administration and Medicare routes (p485-486).
- In palliative care, keep corrected glasses, call bell and useful devices within reach; identify yourself and explain before touching or moving objects (p486-487).
- Preserve autonomy by addressing the person directly and asking what assistance is wanted (p486-487).

### Why the examination and the patient's experience can differ

- The older exam on visual aging asks which change should prompt a search for disease. Reduced accommodation, difficulty distinguishing dark colors and slow adaptation to darkness can accompany aging. A substantial loss of high-contrast acuity calls for evaluation. Healthy aging can still produce a modest decline on that chart (p471–472).
- In one sample of **900** older observers, median acuity at age **82** was **20/30** with high contrast. Low contrast yielded **20/55** in bright conditions and **20/120** at low luminance; with glare, low-contrast acuity was **20/160**. Ask about signs, steps or faces in the lighting where the person actually struggles (p471).
- A central scotoma can impair face recognition and reading. A person may adopt a preferred retinal locus away from the fovea. Training in eccentric viewing and appropriate use of magnification can improve the task even though the scotoma persists (p473, p482).
- With peripheral field loss, a person may identify a small object straight ahead yet miss an obstacle to the side. Teach scanning and movement through the person's own environment; ask relatives which conditions trigger hesitation (p473–474, p479, p485).

### Establish the impairment and the task that it interrupts

Low vision has consequences that do not appear on a distance chart. The person who cannot distinguish one medicine bottle from another may still read large numbers on the wall clock. The same person may manage well at home, where objects have familiar locations, yet be unable to locate a meal or a call bell in hospital. The chapter describes rising prevalence of impairment with age across studies, especially after age 75. Estimates differ according to whether investigators count examination findings or reported functional problems. In one set of U.S. estimates for 2020, vision loss affected about **4.8%** of the population overall and **10%** of those aged 50 and older. These figures describe a defined population and period; neither is an estimate of the proportion who would benefit from a particular device (p469–470).

Consider the indirect consequences when taking the history. Difficulty recognizing other people can shrink the patient's social world. Trouble judging a step or reading a food label can appear in the record as a fall or a nutrition problem rather than an eye complaint. Dual sensory loss makes it harder to rely on spoken instructions in place of print. Arthritis or weakness can prevent use of a magnifier even when the optical prescription is correct. The geriatrician's contribution is to identify these interactions and make the rehabilitation referral describe the task, setting and relevant limitations, not merely the eye diagnosis (p469–470, p474–478).

### Normal aging alters more than the ability to resolve a letter

Table 33-1 connects each change to the kind of difficulty a patient reports. Loss of lens flexibility and ciliary function reduces accommodation, often evident from around age **45** as the need to move near material farther away or use a plus lens. This is an optical focusing problem. A high-contrast line on a chart can remain legible while a pale object on a similarly colored surface disappears. Reduced ocular transmission and smaller pupils make that low-contrast problem worse as illumination falls. A patient who finds a dark stair edge easily in the examination room may miss it at dusk or when there is no contrast between tread and riser (p470–472).

Increased scattering of light alters visual quality. A bright window, headlights or a glossy table can obscure a target despite ample total light. During a transition between outdoor and indoor illumination, slower adaptation can briefly make an otherwise navigable space unsafe. Ask when hesitancy begins and whether it improves after waiting. Difficulty discriminating dark colors or pastels also has an immediate practical consequence: two shirts that are distinct in daylight can look identical when selected in a dim cupboard. The book discusses directed lighting, filters or a brimmed hat and allowing time to adapt. These address distinct problems; additional overhead light alone may make the glare component worse (p471–472).

The attentional visual field describes how far someone can detect and process rapidly presented information while attention is divided. It is not identical to the sensory field measured using stationary test lights. A crowded intersection, signs emerging at the edge of view and simultaneous traffic demands stress that attentional field more than looking for a single light. Reading also requires attention, coordinated eye movements and finding the next line. Older adults can retain accuracy while reading more slowly; print size, spacing and contrast can improve the task. The older exam about a pathologic change uses the contrast between these changes of normal aging and a marked acuity deficit. The cited age-82 measurements above show exactly why illumination and contrast must be specified before comparing acuities (p471–472).

### Predict the rehabilitation problem from the disease pattern

Use Table 33-2 to distinguish where visual information has been lost. Macular degeneration removes detail at fixation. The patient may not describe a black spot, but a face straight ahead can lose its distinguishing features and letters may disappear from a word. Moving the eyes slightly off the target can place it on functioning retina. A preferred retinal locus is a substitute fixation site, and training may help the person use it deliberately. A central scotoma can affect mobility too: poor detail, contrast and depth cues matter when judging a curb, even with peripheral vision remaining (p473, p479–482).

Diabetic retinopathy can produce several patterns in one eye. Macular edema can blur the foveal image, while hemorrhage, retinal injury or treatment can create scattered central or more peripheral scotomas. The patient may report fluctuating function rather than a steady decline. Glaucoma can produce the reverse of the classic macular pattern: a person can recognize a face straight ahead or identify a small object but may miss someone approaching from outside a restricted field. For cataract, scattered light, glare, altered color, image distortion and loss of contrast may be as disabling as the acuity loss. Lens extraction with implantation often restores function, while coexisting macular or optic nerve disease can leave a continuing rehabilitation need (p473–475).

Stroke or traumatic brain injury requires a further distinction. Loss of a hemifield or quadrant removes a region of visual input. Neglect, agnosia and visuospatial processing difficulty can cause important errors even beyond what the sensory field defect explains. Ask the person to find items across a tray, locate the sleeve on the affected side or scan a line of text. A patient can fail at rehabilitation exercises for another impairment because visually presented material or demonstrations are inaccessible. Identify whether the limiting factor is resolution, field, failure to attend to information or failure to recognize what has been seen; the compensatory strategy differs (p473–475, p478–479).

### Turn a symptom questionnaire into a referral and a measurable goal

Table 33-3 contains the VA low-vision functional questionnaire's **20 tasks**. It spans sustained reading, handling money and medicines, navigating familiar places, responding to bright light, finding items on a crowded shelf and managing meals or personal care. Ask about performance *with* the aid or technique the person normally uses. The response for someone who does not perform a task for nonvisual reasons is unscored; treating it as impossible because of vision would misdirect rehabilitation. A useful goal is concrete enough to repeat later, such as finding the correct medicine and reading its label in the patient's usual kitchen lighting (p475).

Table 33-4 lays out the clinical examination. Assess distance and near acuity, refraction, internal and external eye health, pressure, central and peripheral fields, color and contrast. The Pelli-Robson chart tests contrast sensitivity in a way that supplements high-contrast acuity. Observe the patient with candidate optical, nonoptical or electronic aids. An ophthalmologist or optometrist with low-vision expertise can prescribe devices, while a rehabilitation professional can teach their use, assess the environment and address independent travel. Referral may be appropriate with better-eye acuity below **20/50**, but can also be appropriate with preserved central acuity and disabling field loss, glare or contrast difficulty (p474–476).

Table 33-5 takes assessment from eye measurements to performance. Observe fixation, tracking, scanning and use of eye and head movement. Determine whether the person can separate an object from its background, recognize a critical feature and make sense of a whole scene. Test near and distant objects under the lighting needed for each task. Record speed, effort, stress, safety and independence: completing something slowly with a reproducible technique is different from succeeding once by guessing. The same person may benefit from reading training and from a separate plan for obstacles outside the remaining visual field (p477–479).

Setting changes what can be learned. In long-term care, ask staff what the person can find in their own room and whether they can still participate in activities when the environment is arranged consistently. In hospital, identify which needed aids or spectacles remained at home, and choose a time when acute illness allows meaningful participation. A community agency may offer ready access to rehabilitation specialists and devices; a home visit can reveal glare on a worktop, poor contrast at the bathroom door or an inaccessible position of the reading lamp. A clinic demonstration of a device remains incomplete until the person has tried a corresponding task (p476–478).

### Match the aid to the distance, duration and size of the visual field

Table 33-6 organizes devices by what they make possible. A handheld or stand magnifier is well suited to a short task such as checking a price or a recipe. Spectacle-mounted magnification frees the hands and may be better for sustained reading; it also imposes a working distance that the user must learn to maintain. A stand, chair with appropriate support or reading surface can help a person who fatigues while holding the head or material at the focal point. Before prescribing more magnification, ask whether poor hand stability, posture or positioning is what ended the previous attempt (p478–481).

A distant spot task such as finding a street sign has different optical demands. A handheld monocular or binocular can enlarge the target after the person finds and aligns it. A spectacle-mounted telescope may be used for television or a spectator activity; selected short-focus devices help at intermediate distances. A device's presence on the list does not establish driving fitness. Telescopic magnification narrows the field of view, creating a need for scanning training. Someone with severe peripheral loss and near-normal central acuity might instead benefit from minification, field-expanding prisms or mirrors to detect an obstacle while walking. Enlarging a scene can actively worsen that particular task (p479–482).

Electronic magnification allows the person to change size and contrast for reading and writing. A video system or accessible tablet may provide a larger adjustable view for the same task that was exhausting through a handheld lens. Speech output, audiobooks or accessible applications allow long passages to be heard when visual reading becomes slow or tiring. Bold-lined paper, a felt-tip pen and a signature or check-writing guide help position writing. These are choices according to purpose: a person may want to read a greeting card visually for pleasure, use speech for a long report and use a writing guide to sign a form (p480–482).

Demonstrate and then watch the person use the aid. For a magnifier, teach focal distance, positioning of the light and how to navigate along and between lines. For a central scotoma, practice maintaining the preferred retinal locus while moving material through the available field. Loss of visual span means fewer characters are available at once; comprehension can remain good even when the reading rate is slow. In the Swedish study cited, **71%** of participants could read the newspaper after rehabilitation and **48%** could at follow-up three years later, yet the number of fluent readers improved. Ask about the relevant outcome: the ability to read any newsprint, sustained fluency and reading for pleasure can change differently over time (p479–482).

### Change the environment so a task stays possible when conditions change

Table 33-7 groupings prevent a common error: treating every difficulty as a request for larger print. For poor detail, increase actual size, move the target closer or use magnification. For restricted peripheral fields with usable acuity, moving back or reducing apparent image size can bring more of the object into view. Control lighting and observation distance together. Move a reading lamp to the target rather than flooding the entire room. Examine the view from the patient's seated or standing position; a light that looks even to the examiner can reflect directly into the person's eyes (p482–483).

Use contrast at the relevant boundary. Place utensils on a background of a different tone, mark the front edge of a step or select a contrasting switch plate. Busy patterns can hide a small target despite good overall lighting. Shadows may help when they reveal the shape of a step but can obscure a curb if they cross it in the wrong direction. Color helps when it covers a sufficiently large area and contrasts with its background. Add texture or a raised marker to a dial so identification works by touch as well as sight. Store similar cans or clothing in a consistent order rather than requiring repeated identification through a lens (p482–484).

Clear predictable pathways are as important as individual markers. Keep doors fully open or closed and return chairs under the table. Avoid relocating familiar furniture without telling the person. Table 33-8 shows how to introduce an unfamiliar room: start at a recognizable point such as the doorway, describe where important surfaces and objects lie, and guide the person around the space and back to the same reference point. A clock-face or compass description works only if the person finds it intuitive. An audible machine, familiar smell or bright window can supplement tactile and visual landmarks. Confirm that the person can independently find the chair, exit, toilet and route back (p482–485).

### Support adaptation, access and autonomy at the end of life

Changing visual performance can appear inconsistent to family. A person may pick up a dropped object on a bare floor but fail to recognize a relative's face, or walk confidently at home and hesitate outside a dim restaurant. Explain how remaining field, contrast and time to adapt account for the difference. Teaching relatives how to offer help without moving items unpredictably supports confidence and continued device use. Ask about isolation, fear of falling and withdrawal from activities. The chapter describes peer support and short-term counseling alongside task practice; mood and adjustment to loss can affect the willingness to try an unfamiliar method (p484–485).

The U.S. services described in the chapter include state rehabilitation agencies, Veterans Health Administration programs and medically prescribed rehabilitation services under Medicare. Distinguish evaluation and training from purchase of a particular device: eligibility for a therapist visit does not mean every magnifier or electronic aid is funded. The VA example integrates ophthalmic assessment, orientation and mobility specialists, low-vision therapists, rehabilitation teachers and provision of selected devices. Confirm local eligibility and payment arrangements when making an actual referral rather than promising the same service in every setting (p485–486).

In palliative care, choose the daily function that matters to the patient today. Keeping the correct spectacles, magnifier, call bell and water in a predictable location may do more for autonomy than introducing a demanding new device. Knock, identify yourself, face the person, say who else is present and announce what you are about to do. Describe where a tray has been placed and ask before leading the person or moving a familiar item. A patient who cannot identify a staff member's face may still make decisions and understand explanations perfectly well. Direct the conversation to the person, establish what help is desired and tell them before leaving the room (p486–487).

For a medication-management goal, observe the entire sequence in the usual environment. Can the person find the bottle on a crowded shelf, distinguish it from a similar container, read the label at a comfortable distance and return it to a consistent place? If the failure is finding the bottle, rearrangement, a contrasting background or a tactile mark may help. If the failure is reading the dose, test task lighting, a magnifier or speech output. Reassess the same sequence with the chosen aid and ask whether the patient can repeat it when tired (p475, p478–480, p482–484).

## Exam traps

- Impairment and dependency are different observations. When rating medication identification, check the usual aid, label format and lighting before assigning inability to vision alone (p475–478).
- Magnification trades field for detail. A telescope for locating a street sign serves a different purpose from a reverse telescope or prism for detecting hazards (p480–482).
- Reading speed, accuracy, comprehension and endurance are separate outcomes; practice can improve one even when the acuity measurement does not change (p472, p482).
- A patient who cannot visually recognize the clinician may still understand and choose among treatment options. Introduce yourself and obtain their preferences directly (p486–487).

- High-contrast acuity in a well-lit room can conceal disabling loss of contrast sensitivity (p471, p475).
- Preserved central acuity does not exclude field loss or remove the indication for rehabilitation (p475).
- More illumination may increase glare; test light direction, reflection and the task together (p482–483).

## Recall drill

**1. Which everyday visual changes commonly accompany healthy aging?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Reduced accommodation, poorer dark adaptation, greater glare sensitivity and difficulty discriminating some colors (p471–472).</details>

**2. What qualification belongs with the older exam answer about acuity loss?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>High-contrast acuity usually declines only modestly in healthy aging; marked loss merits investigation, and low contrast or glare can worsen measured performance substantially (p471).</details>

**3. How did lighting change measured acuity in the cited age-82 sample?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Median high-contrast acuity was <strong>20/30</strong>, compared with <strong>20/120</strong> under low contrast and low luminance and <strong>20/160</strong> under low contrast with glare (p471).</details>

**4. What explains slow reading and poor face recognition with macular disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A central scotoma removes detailed foveal vision and impairs contrast. A preferred retinal locus may develop outside it (p473).</details>

**5. Which pattern can diabetic retinopathy produce?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Blurring with macular edema, scattered field loss and fluctuating vision with swelling or hemorrhage (p473).</details>

**6. Why may someone with preserved central acuity still miss a doorway or obstacle?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Peripheral field loss can impair detection away from fixation (p473–475).</details>

**7. Which chart does the chapter suggest for contrast sensitivity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Pelli-Robson chart, which the chapter describes as practical and reliable (p475).</details>

**8. Which findings can justify low-vision referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Better-eye acuity below <strong>20/50</strong>, field loss despite intact acuity, reduced contrast sensitivity, glare or light-adaptation difficulty (p475).</details>

**9. What should the first functional interview establish?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Which activities the person wants to recover and in which settings they break down, such as reading medication labels or walking outdoors (p475–478).</details>

**10. What does eccentric viewing train?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use of a retinal area outside the damaged fovea, including coordination with an appropriate low-vision device (p482).</details>

**11. Why can fluent comprehension coexist with very slow reading?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Central loss reduces visual span and slows word acquisition; identification accuracy and comprehension can remain near normal (p482).</details>

**12. How can kitchen controls be easier to locate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Place high-contrast, tactile marks on frequently used dials and keep items in predictable locations (p484).</details>

**13. What lighting change can make mobility harder?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Light reflecting from a glossy surface or an abrupt change between bright and dim areas can cause glare or a period of poor adaptation (p472, p482–483).</details>

**14. What can family members learn from a low-vision evaluation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>How light, glare, adaptation and field loss affect performance and which environmental changes or aids actually help (p484–485).</details>

**15. How can staff preserve autonomy when entering a room?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Identify themselves, speak to the person directly, explain before touching or moving objects, and keep glasses and aids within reach in known locations (p486–487).</details>
