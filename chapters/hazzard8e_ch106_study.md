## Summary

## Definition & epidemiology
- ASB is bacteriuria, with or without pyuria, without localizing genitourinary symptoms; bacteriuria in older adults is usually asymptomatic colonization. (p1699)
<a id="p1700"></a>

<span data-exam-page="1700"></span>

- Simple cystitis stays within the bladder; complicated UTI extends beyond it and includes pyelonephritis. Recurrent UTI: **≥3 in 12 months** or **≥2 in 6 months**, after clinical resolution of each treated episode. (p1700)
- CAUTI requires compatible symptoms with an indwelling urethral, suprapubic or intermittent catheter, or genitourinary symptoms within 48 hours of removal with no other infection source. In the comparison extending onto p1708, **IDSA clinical criteria use ≥10³ cfu/mL** of at least one bacterial species in a catheter specimen or a midstream specimen after removal. **AMDA uses ≥10⁵ cfu/mL**, and **SHEA surveillance uses ≥10⁵ cfu/mL** in a catheter specimen. The overview on p1700 gives ≥10⁵ and notes counts as low as ≥10² in some symptomatic patients. Bacteriuria and pyuria are common with chronic catheters (p1700, p1707–1708).
- UTI may be hematogenous rather than ascending, secondary to bacteremia from a nonurinary source; this is particularly a consideration when *Staphylococcus aureus* is isolated in a patient without an indwelling catheter. An indwelling catheter is consistently a strong risk factor for symptomatic UTI; the most common risk factor is a history of UTI (p1702)

## Diagnosis & management
<a id="p1703"></a>

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-526291ef04bcef123eab45db" data-exam-read-page="1703"></span>

- Do not screen/treat ASB routinely; the exception discussed for older adults is endoscopic urologic procedures associated with mucosal trauma. (p1703)
<a id="p1705"></a>

<span data-exam-page="1705"></span>

- In PALTC residents without specific urinary signs or symptoms, behavioral change (exclusive of delirium), functional decline, falls or anorexia should NOT prompt evaluation for UTI; a change in the character of the urine may reflect mild dehydration, diet or medication (p1705)
- Urine-character changes alone do not establish UTI. McGeer criteria serve surveillance; Loeb criteria guide antibiotic decisions. (p1705)
- With nonspecific symptoms but no UTI criteria or warning signs, use active monitoring, hydration and alternative-diagnosis assessment. Fever, rigors, unstable vitals or clear-cut delirium require evaluation. (p1712)

## Microbiology

<span data-exam-page="1707"></span>

- *E coli* causes **75% to 95%** of infections in ambulatory older women, asymptomatic or symptomatic; other Enterobacteriaceae and *Enterococcus* are occasional, *Pseudomonas* and group B and D streptococci rare. In uncomplicated UTI, over **95%** are due to a single organism; skin flora usually signify contamination (p1707)
- In LTCF residents *E coli* is still most frequent, followed by *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococcus*, *Staphylococcus* and *Pseudomonas*; *Acinetobacter*, *Candida* and MRSA are less common. With MRSA in the urine, consider staphylococcal bacteremia as a potential source. Polymicrobial bacteriuria is more likely in LTC residents, especially with a chronic catheter: more than one organism in **10% to 25%** of bacteriuric subjects (p1707)
- Risk factors for non-*E coli* UTI in community-dwelling older adults: male gender, severe presentation (symptoms more than 7 days, fever, nausea or vomiting, flank pain), UTI in the past 12 months, diabetes, previous TMP/SMX failure, functional or anatomical abnormalities (p1707)

## Drugs — acute simple cystitis
- Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals **100 mg twice daily for 5 days**; avoid with CrCl **<30 mL/min** or long-term suppression. (p1708)
- TMP/SMX **160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days**; avoid empiric use when local uropathogen resistance exceeds **20%**. (p1708)
<a id="p1709"></a>
- TMP/SMX interacts with warfarin and sulfonylureas; monitor increased INR/hypoglycemia risk. (p1709)
- Fosfomycin **3 g once orally**: generally reserved for resistant pathogens or inability to use other first-line drugs; not for complicated UTI. (p1709)
<a id="p1711"></a>
- Most older men with simple cystitis: **7 days** if response is prompt, within **72 hours**; assess for extension beyond the bladder. (p1711)


### Deciding when a positive culture should change care

- In someone with no compatible symptoms, diabetes, residence in long-term care, chronic catheterization and planned elective nonurologic surgery are among the settings in which Table 106-2 advises against screening or treating bacteriuria. Endoscopic urologic procedures that injure mucosa are the important exception. The book advises a preprocedure culture, targeted treatment for one or two doses, and starting **30 to 60 minutes** before the procedure (p1703, p1707–1708).
- A positive dipstick in a resident who has fallen does not locate the cause of the fall. Look for a new urinary complaint or a systemic warning sign, assess hydration, review other explanations and arrange reassessment. Acute delirium warrants evaluation for infection and noninfectious causes. Chronic colonization makes the urine a poor discriminator by itself (p1704–1705, p1712).
- Ask whether the catheter remains necessary. If it has been in place for **over 2 weeks**, discontinue it if possible or replace it before collecting the specimen. A recently removed catheter still matters when a patient develops urinary symptoms within **48 hours**. In the catheterized patient, pelvic discomfort, fever or rigors without another source can support infection (p1700, p1704, p1707–1708).
- Distinguish drugs for bladder infection from therapy for a patient with systemic illness. Vomiting may preclude oral treatment. Pyelonephritis requires an agent that reaches renal tissue, and the book includes parenteral ceftriaxone among initial options. A stable patient with isolated dysuria and a patient with vomiting and fever therefore need different assessments (p1709–1711).

## Exam traps & prevention
- Male sex alone does not make every UTI complicated. (p1710)

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-60d358f39722f9df5a3990cc" data-exam-read-page="1711"></span>

- Nitrofurantoin/fosfomycin lack adequate renal penetration for complicated UTI. (p1711)
- Complicated UTI with severe symptoms (eg, persistent vomiting), suspected sepsis, uncertain diagnosis, obstruction or significant frailty should be hospitalized. The book limits empiric oral therapy for complicated UTI to fluoroquinolones, but not empirically where local resistance exceeds **10%**; then give an initial dose of a long-acting parenteral agent (eg, ceftriaxone, ertapenem) first (p1711)
> Current practice (not the book): Empiric therapy depends on illness severity, prior cultures and antibiotic exposure, and patient-specific risks. In sepsis, consult a relevant local antibiogram. Selected patients can receive other oral agents, and definitive therapy should follow susceptibility results. - [IDSA complicated UTI guideline, 2025](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/complicated-urinary-tract-infections/).

- Avoid unnecessary catheters and remove promptly when no longer indicated; routine fixed-interval replacement is not recommended. (p1714)

## Recall

**1. Does pyuria convert asymptomatic bacteriuria into symptomatic UTI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. ASB can occur with or without pyuria in the absence of localizing genitourinary symptoms. (p1699)</details>

**2. What distinguishes simple cystitis from complicated UTI in this chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Simple cystitis is confined to the bladder; complicated infection extends beyond it, including renal-parenchymal infection in pyelonephritis. (p1700)</details>

**3. How is recurrent UTI defined?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>At least <strong>3 symptomatic UTIs in 12 months</strong> or <strong>2 in 6 months</strong>, following clinical resolution of each episode with treatment. (p1700)</details>

**4. Does a high postvoid residual consistently predict symptomatic UTI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Study findings conflict; high residuals were not consistently associated with symptomatic infection in ambulatory women or long-term-care residents. (p1702)</details>

**5. What ASB-treatment exception is specified for older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Screening and treatment before endoscopic urologic procedures associated with mucosal trauma. (p1703)</details>

**6. Should a urine color or odor change alone establish UTI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. A change in urine character is insufficient and may reflect mild dehydration, diet or medications. (p1705)</details>

**7. What are the different purposes of McGeer and Loeb criteria?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Original/revised McGeer criteria support nursing-home surveillance; Loeb minimum criteria support clinical antibiotic decisions. (p1705)</details>

**8. What belongs in active monitoring for nonspecific symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Frequent vital-sign, hydration and symptom checks; hydration support; alternative-diagnosis assessment; and explicit criteria for notifying the physician about deterioration or persistence. (p1705)</details>

**9. What nitrofurantoin regimen and renal limit does the chapter give for simple cystitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Monohydrate/macrocrystals <strong>100 mg twice daily for 5 days</strong>; avoid if CrCl is <strong>&lt;30 mL/min</strong>, and avoid long-term suppression. (p1708)</details>

**10. What TMP/SMX regimen and local-resistance cutoff are given?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary><strong>160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days</strong> for simple cystitis; avoid empiric use if local uropathogen resistance exceeds <strong>20%</strong>. (p1708)</details>

**11. Which important TMP/SMX interactions should be recognized?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Warfarin, with increased INR, and sulfonylureas, with increased hypoglycemia risk. (p1709)</details>

**12. What is the fosfomycin dose and its usual place in simple-cystitis treatment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary><strong>3 g as a single oral dose</strong>; generally reserved for highly resistant pathogens or inability to use other first-line agents. It is not recommended for complicated UTI. (p1709)</details>

**13. Can an older man have simple cystitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Men should not automatically be classified as having complicated UTI, but require careful assessment for infection extending beyond the bladder. (p1710)</details>

**14. When may a week of antibiotics suffice for an older man with simple cystitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>For most, <strong>7 days</strong> is adequate if the response is prompt, within <strong>72 hours</strong>. (p1711)</details>

**15. What warning signs prevent a nonspecific presentation from being treated as routine observation alone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fever, rigors/chills, unstable vital signs or new clear-cut delirium warrant evaluation for infection and noninfectious causes; assess whether the presentation is consistent with UTI. (p1712)</details>
