# Chapter 90 Study: Prostate Cancer

## Summary

**Risk & diagnosis**
- Established risk factors include older age, African-American race and family history. Most newly detected cancers are asymptomatic (p1409, p1411).
- Consider life expectancy and comorbidities before screening; no drug or food supplement is approved for prostate-cancer prevention in this chapter (p1409–1410).
- PSA is sensitive but nonspecific: benign enlargement, inflammation and infection also raise it. A value below **4 ng/mL** does not exclude cancer (p1412).
- Abnormal PSA or digital rectal examination prompts urologic evaluation and biopsy; multiparametric MRI can guide targeted sampling (p1412).

**Localized disease**
- Choose treatment using tumor risk, life expectancy, comorbidity, baseline quality of life and preferences (p1412).
- Active surveillance monitors for progression with potential delayed curative treatment; watchful waiting anticipates palliative treatment if needed (p1414).
- The chapter describes surveillance PSA every **3 months**, rectal examination every **6 months**, and repeat biopsy as warranted (p1414).
- Radical prostatectomy is an option for confined disease, life expectancy over **10 years** and no surgical contraindication; radiation and brachytherapy are other local options (p1414). Relative contraindications to **external-beam radiotherapy** include active collagen vascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease and microvascular damage from hypertension or diabetes.

**Advanced disease & treatment**
- Androgen-deprivation therapy is the initial hormonal approach for advanced disease or relapse after local treatment; timing for isolated biochemical recurrence is controversial (p1416). Medical castration can use an LHRH agonist, with an antiandrogen started beforehand in metastatic disease to prevent testosterone flare; orchiectomy is a surgical alternative.
- ADT can reduce bone density by **2–3% per year** initially and increases fracture risk; assess bone health and consider indicated protective therapy (p1417).
- Docetaxel has demonstrated survival benefit in castration-resistant disease. Mitoxantrone offers palliation but lacks demonstrated survival benefit (p1418).
- Among the **bisphosphonates** tested in advanced prostate cancer, only zoledronic acid has demonstrated benefit for bone metastases; assess renal function and dental health because renal toxicity and osteonecrosis of the jaw are concerns (p1418).

### Who is at risk, who benefits from finding a tumor, and why those groups differ

Age raises the probability of finding prostate cancer, yet it also increases the chance that another illness will determine survival. In the United States figures cited by the book, the median age at presentation was **66 years**, and about **90%** of prostate cancer deaths occurred in men over 65. These observations describe a population. They do not show that PSA screening at every advanced age improves survival. Before a screening test, ask what the patient hopes to gain, how competing illness affects the time available for benefit and whether he would accept further testing or treatment following an abnormal result (p1409, p1411–1412).

Family history was the answer in the older examination. Approximately **10%** of cases are described as hereditary and they tend to present earlier. Some families carry germline variants such as BRCA2, HOXB13 or ATM. In the prospective study described, BRCA2 carriers had a higher incidence of clinically significant tumors at a younger age. The book also describes higher U.S. incidence and mortality among African-American men and cautions that differences in disease risk and outcomes have several contributors. A positive family history belongs in the initial discussion even when a man feels well and has no urinary symptoms (p1409–1411).

Distinguish associations from tested prevention strategies. Dietary studies of fish and tomato products suggested possible protective associations. Neither establishes that prescribing a supplement prevents cancer. Finasteride and dutasteride inhibit conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone. Prevention trials led to concern about detection of more serious cancers, and the book reports that these drugs were not approved for chemoprevention. In SELECT, selenium and vitamin E did not reduce prostate cancer risk in healthy men. Later follow-up found a significant increase in diagnoses with vitamin E alone. A biologically plausible intervention and a positive result in a different population or for a secondary outcome are insufficient grounds for prescribing it (p1409–1411).

### Comparing screening trials without confusing detection with mortality

A screening program advances the date of diagnosis and can detect cancers that would never become clinically important during a man's lifetime. More localized tumors and more survivors measured from diagnosis therefore do not by themselves prove that fewer men die from prostate cancer. In the U.S. PLCO study, annual testing increased diagnoses by **22%** relative to the comparison group. The chapter reports no prostate-cancer mortality difference at seven or ten years. By contrast, ERSPC found a **20% relative reduction** in that cause-specific endpoint after a median of **13 years**, with **742 men** needing an invitation to screening to avert one prostate cancer death. Invitation is the denominator for that estimate; it is not the number of positive tests or treatments (p1411).

Read Table 90-1 as a comparison of the organizations and dates printed in the book. The USPSTF 2018 row calls for an individual informed decision at ages **55–69** and advises against screening in the older group. The AUA 2018 row discusses shared decision making at the same younger ages and an interval of **at least 2 years** if screening is chosen. Its older-age recommendation incorporates whether life expectancy is less than **10–15 years**. The NCCN row reserves screening beyond **75** for selected very healthy men with little or no comorbidity. The table notes that it does not apply to men at higher risk. These are screening decisions for someone without suspicious symptoms. New bone pain, neurologic signs or a palpable abnormality requires assessment on its own merits (p1411–1412).

### What PSA, biopsy and imaging each establish

PSA is prostate-specific rather than cancer-specific. Benign enlargement, inflammation and infection can produce a high value, and the conventional **4 ng/mL** cutoff misses some cancers. In the population cited, **15%** of men with a normal PSA had cancer and **2%** had high-grade disease. For a total PSA of **4–10 ng/mL**, the free-to-total PSA ratio can refine risk. The aim is to estimate the probability of clinically important disease before biopsy rather than treating a cutoff as a histologic diagnosis. Include the DRE, trajectory, baseline function and willingness to act on the result (p1412).

The book describes ultrasound-guided transrectal or transperineal sampling and generally **6–12 cores**. Multiparametric MRI can localize an abnormal area for targeted sampling. A needle specimen supplies histology: the dominant and secondary Gleason patterns describe architecture, so their order matters. Grade group 1 corresponds to Gleason score at most 6; groups 2 and 3 divide score 7 into **3 + 4** and **4 + 3**, respectively. Group 4 corresponds to score 8 and group 5 to scores 9–10. Two men with identical PSA and Gleason totals can therefore occupy different risk groups if the dominant pattern differs (p1412–1413).

Table 90-2 combines grade, clinical T category, PSA and the extent of biopsy involvement. In the very-low group all the listed criteria apply: T1c, grade group 1, PSA **below 10 ng/mL**, fewer than three positive cores, **at most 50%** cancer in each and PSA density **below 0.15**. Low risk allows T1–T2a with grade group 1 and PSA below 10. Neither group ordinarily requires staging imaging in the printed table. This imaging decision follows the biopsy. It is distinct from MRI used earlier to select an area to sample (p1412–1413).

The intermediate group has at least one of T2b–T2c, grade group 2–3 or PSA **10–20 ng/mL**, in the absence of high-risk features. Favorable intermediate disease has one intermediate factor, grade group 1 or 2 and fewer than half the cores positive. Two or three intermediate factors, grade group 3, or at least half the cores positive make intermediate disease unfavorable. The table indicates pelvic or abdominal imaging in these groups. It omits bone scan for favorable intermediate disease and recommends it in the unfavorable group when T2 and PSA greater than 10 coexist. High-risk disease includes T3a, grade group 4–5 or PSA above 20. Very-high-risk features include T3b–T4, a primary Gleason pattern 5 or more than four cores with grade group 4 or 5. Both high categories lead to bone and pelvic/abdominal staging in this book algorithm (p1413).

### Reading Figure 90-1 with the patient's priorities in view

The flowchart starts with a positive screening result and separates a negative biopsy from a confirmed tumor. For very-low or low-risk disease, surveillance can precede radical prostatectomy or radiation if the tumor progresses. Intermediate risk more often moves directly toward local treatment. High and very high risk can call for local treatment with androgen deprivation according to modality and risk. Disease confined at diagnosis can still recur biochemically. Selected local recurrences may be amenable to salvage radiation with or without ADT; established metastatic disease shifts the goal toward sustained control and symptom relief. The branches represent possible trajectories and alternatives, not stages every patient must pass through (p1413–1416).

Observation has two distinct intents. In active surveillance the person remains a candidate for curative treatment if later findings indicate progression. Besides the surveillance intervals above, the book cites a PSA doubling time shorter than **3 years**, a repeat biopsy with primary Gleason pattern 4 or 5, and increasing extent or number of involved cores as concerning findings. In watchful waiting, the decision is to treat symptoms if and when they develop; repeated invasive sampling for a curative intervention the patient would decline makes little sense. Both the initial risk of cancer death and the impact of biopsy, urinary adverse effects or the demands of treatment matter to this choice (p1412–1414).

Radical prostatectomy removes the prostate and seminal vesicles, allowing pathologic assessment of organ confinement and margins. A favorable surgical candidate has sufficient life expectancy to realize benefit and can tolerate the operation and recovery. Discuss continence and sexual function in the context of current symptoms and preferences. The book reports comparable results for open and less invasive approaches in experienced hands and no demonstrated benefit of neoadjuvant hormone therapy before prostatectomy. A visually localized lesion and a favorable biopsy are estimates: the resection specimen may reveal more extensive or higher-grade disease (p1414).

For external-beam radiation the field and dose depend on risk, and treatment commonly involves multiple visits. The chapter describes low-risk regimens of **70–75 Gy in 35–41 fractions** and **75–80 Gy** for higher-risk features. Its regimen for intermediate-risk disease includes **4–6 months** of ADT around radiation and, for high-risk disease, **2–3 years** in total. These are the conventional schedules discussed in the book. Baseline urinary, bowel and sexual symptoms matter when weighing the harms of a local treatment. In the older question about a patient with Crohn disease, inflammatory bowel disease is a relative contraindication to external-beam radiation: the vulnerable adjacent bowel and potential injury influence the comparison with other local choices (p1414).

Brachytherapy places radiation sources within the gland and may suit selected organ-confined disease. In intermediate-risk cases the book describes combining it with external-beam radiation; high-risk patients generally make poor candidates for brachytherapy alone. A patient weighing these local options should compare the recovery from anesthesia and surgery, urinary or bowel effects, the need to travel repeatedly and the likelihood of additional treatment. Observational comparisons across modalities reflect differences in tumor biology and treatment selection. Similar biochemical control in a cohort does not mean that every modality imposes the same burden on the individual (p1414–1415).

### The meaning of a rising PSA depends on the preceding treatment

After prostatectomy there should be very little PSA-producing tissue left. The chapter defines biochemical recurrence as PSA **at least 0.2 ng/mL** and rising on two or more determinations. After radiation some prostate tissue remains, and the criterion is a rise of **2 ng/mL above the nadir**. A single reading that has increased slightly from the previous result therefore cannot be interpreted without knowing the treatment, the lowest post-treatment level and the trend. Surveillance intensity also depends on whether recurrence could lead to an intervention the patient would accept (p1415).

A slow rise one to three years after definitive therapy or a new finding on DRE may indicate local recurrence and prompt consideration of local salvage. A relapse within **6 months** of local therapy is more suggestive of previously occult metastatic disease and less likely to respond to local salvage alone. A very short doubling time carries prognostic significance even when the absolute PSA has only recently risen. For isolated biochemical relapse, the optimal timing of hormonal therapy remains controversial. In contrast, new neurologic symptoms, obstructive disease or painful bone metastases pose immediate treatment questions that cannot wait for serial PSA measurements (p1415–1416).

### Metastatic hormone-sensitive disease and the painful-bone exam case

Table 90-3 separates four ways of reducing androgen signaling. Orchiectomy removes the major source of testosterone permanently. LHRH-directed therapy suppresses testicular production medically, with depot schedules described at **3–4 months** in the table. Examples include goserelin, leuprolide and triptorelin. First-generation antiandrogens such as bicalutamide, flutamide and nilutamide block the androgen receptor. Abiraterone inhibits CYP17-dependent synthesis, whereas enzalutamide, apalutamide and darolutamide act at the receptor. The oral agents have different roles and adverse effects despite appearing together as daily tablets in the table (p1416–1417).

June 2023 Q60 describes disabling bone pain with metastatic prostate cancer in a very old man. Systemic suppression of androgen signaling addresses cancer throughout the skeleton; definitive removal or irradiation of the gland alone cannot do that. An LHRH agonist is the appropriate option offered. In metastatic disease the initial testosterone surge with an agonist can briefly worsen symptoms; the book advises starting an antiandrogen **7–10 days before** agonist administration and continuing antiandrogen treatment for at least **30 days**. Orchiectomy offers another route to castration. An agonist should not divert attention from a mechanically unstable bone or a compressive neurologic emergency (p1416).

> Current practice (not the book): EAU recommends discussing treatment intensification for men presenting with metastatic hormone-sensitive disease who are fit for combination therapy, have sufficient life expectancy to benefit and accept its toxicity. For impending cord compression or bladder outlet obstruction, it recommends an LHRH antagonist or orchiectomy at initiation to avoid an agonist flare. - [European Association of Urology prostate cancer guideline, 2026](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/prostate-cancer/chapter/treatment).

Ask specifically about new severe back pain, weakness, gait changes, sensory symptoms and bladder or bowel dysfunction. In cord compression, pain often precedes a neurological deficit. Suspected compression warrants urgent imaging, preferably MRI or CT myelogram as discussed in the chapter, and corticosteroids while compression is being excluded. Focal pain in a weight-bearing bone poses a separate risk of fracture; assess stability urgently and involve the appropriate surgical and radiation teams. Analgesia can begin while this assessment is under way. Relief of pain by an opioid does not establish that it is safe to bear weight (p1416, p1418–1419).

### Progression during castration and choosing another mechanism

Castration-resistant disease means progression by PSA or imaging despite castrate serum testosterone, defined in the book as **below 50 ng/dL**. Confirm the treatment history and the testosterone level before labeling a rising marker resistance. For someone already receiving a first-generation antiandrogen, withdrawal may produce a short-lived response. Testicular suppression is commonly continued during later therapies. With antiandrogen treatment the book discusses liver function monitoring after the initial month and every **3 months** thereafter. Different hormonal drugs cannot be substituted simply because each is taken orally (p1416–1417).

Abiraterone is administered with prednisone. Its inhibition of androgen synthesis can cause mineralocorticoid excess, so new edema, rising blood pressure and a falling potassium concentration are clinically related toxicities. Enzalutamide also improved survival in the studies summarized, but seizure risk is a relevant caution. For either strategy, change in pain, functional capacity and time until a skeletal event are meaningful outcomes alongside PSA. Long-term androgen deprivation itself can accelerate bone loss, cause vasomotor or sexual symptoms and change body composition. The chapter reports hip and spine density losses of about **2%–3% annually** early in treatment and describes daily calcium **500 mg** and vitamin D **400 IU** supplementation along with consideration of bone-density testing. These supplement amounts are the book's quoted regimen; baseline intake, bone density and fracture risk still require assessment (p1417).

Docetaxel improved survival in castration-resistant disease, whereas mitoxantrone with a corticosteroid historically provided palliation without a demonstrated survival gain. Cabazitaxel provides an additional taxane option after prior therapy; marrow reserve and other illness affect tolerability. For homologous recombination repair gene-mutated metastatic castration-resistant disease after specified prior androgen-directed treatment, the book discusses olaparib. Sipuleucel-T was studied for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic metastatic castration-resistant disease. One trial improved survival by **4.1 months** without a PSA decline or progression-free survival advantage. This is a reminder to match the endpoint to the drug's demonstrated benefit rather than discard a survival result because PSA did not improve (p1417–1418).

### Preventing skeletal events and relieving existing pain

May 2024 Q70 asks which supportive treatment has evidence in prostate cancer with bony metastases. Zoledronic acid is the bisphosphonate with demonstrated benefit among the agents compared in the chapter. The practical endpoint is prevention or delay of tumor-related skeletal events. This is distinct from increasing bone density during ADT, from treating the cancer itself and from rapid relief of an existing painful metastasis. Renal monitoring and dental evaluation before zoledronic acid matter in older adults; ask about dental symptoms because of jaw osteonecrosis risk. Denosumab blocks RANK ligand, providing another mechanism in metastatic castration-resistant disease. The renal caveat for denosumab is hypocalcemia risk, even when drug clearance is not the obstacle (p1417–1418).

Radiation can target an especially painful metastasis while systemic therapy continues. The chapter describes **1–10 fractions**, with meaningful lasting relief in **80%–90%** of patients treated for painful bony disease. Onset can occur within **48 hours**, although maximal benefit may take up to two weeks after treatment ends. Strontium-89 and samarium-153 are beta emitters used for palliation. Radium-223 delivers alpha particles and has survival as well as pain data in selected metastatic castration-resistant disease; one trial improved median survival by **3.6 months**. Its addition to abiraterone plus a corticosteroid in another trial did not improve symptomatic skeletal-event-free survival and increased fractures. Different bone-directed interventions answer different questions, even when they are all described as treatment for bone metastases (p1418–1419).

**Exam traps & urgent complications**

- PSA after prostatectomy and PSA after external-beam radiation have different recurrence definitions; do not transfer the surgical cutoff to an irradiated gland (p1415).
- The order of Gleason patterns distinguishes grade group 2 from grade group 3 even when both totals are seven (p1412–1413).
- ADT-related bone loss, skeletal-event prevention in metastatic disease, focal radiation for analgesia and immediate stabilization of a threatened fracture have different indications (p1416–1419).
- New back pain may precede neurologic signs of cord compression; a normal neurologic examination must not delay definitive imaging (p1416).
- Suspected cord compression warrants steroids while being evaluated. Pain at a weight-bearing metastasis needs fracture assessment and urgent surgical/radiation consultation (p1416).
- A survival benefit need not produce a PSA decline: the sipuleucel-T trial improved survival without PSA reduction or better progression-free survival (p1418).


### Painful metastases and the choice of treatment

- Bone pain in a patient with newly diagnosed metastatic prostate cancer prompts two parallel decisions. Androgen deprivation treats hormone-sensitive cancer, while focal pain in a weight-bearing bone or new back pain may require urgent imaging and local treatment to avert fracture or cord compression. A normal initial neurologic examination cannot exclude epidural disease (p1416).
- An LHRH agonist initially raises testosterone. For known metastases the chapter describes antiandrogen coverage starting **7–10 days** before the agonist and continuing for **at least 30 days** in total. Orchiectomy is an alternative way to reduce testosterone. Timing after an isolated PSA rise is a different question from treating painful metastatic disease (p1416).

> Current practice (not the book): For newly presenting metastatic hormone-sensitive disease, offer ADT with treatment intensification to patients fit for combination therapy who have sufficient life expectancy and accept the additional adverse effects. With impending cord compression or outlet obstruction, an LHRH antagonist or orchiectomy avoids an agonist flare. - [European Association of Urology guideline, 2026](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/prostate-cancer/chapter/treatment).

- Separate ADT-related loss of bone mineral density from tumor-related skeletal events. The first calls for fracture-risk and bone-density assessment. For the second, the book singles out zoledronic acid among bisphosphonates tested in advanced prostate cancer. It also describes denosumab, a RANK ligand antibody, as noninferior for preventing skeletal events in metastatic castration-resistant disease. That comparison does not establish a survival advantage (p1417–1418).
- Before IV zoledronic acid, assess renal function and obtain a dental examination. Ask about dental symptoms during treatment because osteonecrosis of the jaw can occur. The chapter describes denosumab as usable in renal insufficiency (p1418).

> Current practice (not the book): Correct pre-existing hypocalcemia and monitor calcium during denosumab treatment, particularly early on. Severe renal dysfunction or dialysis increases the risk of hypocalcemia. Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D. - [Xgeva US prescribing information, 2026](https://www.pi.amgen.com/united_states/xgeva/xgeva_pi.pdf).

- Palliative radiotherapy can relieve painful bone metastases even when systemic treatment continues. The chapter reports significant and durable relief in **80%–90%** of treated patients. Suspected impending fracture warrants urgent structural assessment as well as analgesia (p1416, p1419).
