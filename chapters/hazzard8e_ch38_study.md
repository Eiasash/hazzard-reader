# Chapter 38 Study: Benign Prostate Disorders

## What the exams asked

- In a man with neurologic disease who is considering prostate surgery, when should overactivity during filling and poor emptying be investigated together? ## Before prostate surgery in a man with neurologic disease

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e2d127b4b2b341b7154a9425" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Why urgency and retention can coexist.** A detrusor can contract involuntarily while the bladder fills, causing urgency or leakage, yet generate too little sustained pressure to empty the bladder during voiding. A weak stream, residual urine and an enlarged prostate fit more than one mechanism. Resection relieves resistance at the outlet. If impaired contraction also contributes, emptying may improve after obstruction is relieved, but recovery of contractility is uncertain. This distinction changes what benefit a man can expect from surgery (pp. 562, 568, and ch. 47, p. 701).

**The decision in May 2024 Q68.** Consider urodynamics in a frail older man, particularly with Parkinson disease or a spinal cord injury, when prostate surgery is being considered and the mechanism matters. Filling cystometry identifies involuntary contractions and assesses compliance. During voiding, pressure measured together with flow distinguishes high pressure with low flow (outlet obstruction) from low flow with inadequate contraction. A residual measures incomplete emptying but cannot identify its cause. Isolated stress leakage or infrequent large voids alone do not present the same preoperative question (p. 562). [Read the paragraph](?chapter=38#exam-p562-mcq-e2d127b4b2b341b7154a9425).

- Urodynamics is not routine initially. High bladder pressure with low flow supports obstruction; consider testing after inadequate response or before surgery in selected frail patients (p562).

## Summary

**Definitions**

- BPH is histologic hyperplasia; benign enlargement, bladder outlet obstruction and lower urinary tract symptoms overlap only partly (p555).
- LUTS include storage and voiding symptoms and can reflect comorbidity, medications or functional impairment rather than prostate disease alone (p555).

**What changes with age.** Histologic benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is nearly universal by the ninth decade. Only about half of men with BPH have benign prostatic enlargement, and only some enlarged glands obstruct. Hyperplasia usually arises in the transition zone around the urethra. Local conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone supports stromal and epithelial growth. Most prostate cancers arise more peripherally. BPH itself is not a prostate cancer risk factor (pp. 555–558).

**Course over time.** Among men followed for moderate symptoms, some improved without surgery and others worsened. A larger gland, prior retention, an elevated baseline residual and failure to respond to an alpha blocker raise concern for retention. Higher baseline PSA was associated with subsequent retention or surgery. Symptom burden, medication exposure and the patient's priorities matter when deciding whom to follow more closely (pp. 555–558).

## Symptoms, bother and the initial workup

**Assessment**

- IPSS/AUASI scores **0–7** are mild, **8–19** moderate and **20–35** severe; symptom bother and quality of life also guide decisions (p559).
- Examine cognition, mobility, neurologic function, prostate/rectal findings and stool impaction; rectal examination is inaccurate for prostate volume (p561).
- Urinalysis is routine; PSA testing follows appropriate cancer-screening decisions rather than automatically accompanying LUTS (p561).

Ask which symptom limits sleep, leaving home, sexual activity or other daily activities. The separate IPSS quality-of-life question is not added to the seven-symptom score. The BPH Impact Index measures the effect on daily life on a scale of 0 to 13. People with similar symptom scores may want different treatment. A slow stream can reflect obstruction or a weak bladder. Review all drugs, including nonprescription cold remedies, and ask about fluid intake, bowel function, sleep and access to a toilet (pp. 558–561, Table 38-2 and Table 38-3).

Consider a residual after unsuccessful empiric therapy, with complex neurologic disease, unexplained renal impairment or a drug that compromises emptying. Inspect the bladder with cystoscopy for hematuria, especially in a smoker, or for positive cytology or pelvic pain. Pressure-flow testing answers a different question about how the outlet functions during voiding (pp. 561–563).

## Nocturia and the frequency-volume chart

First ask whether the patient woke to void or voided because pain, insomnia or another sleep disorder woke him. Frequent small voids can arise from involuntary contractions or a reduced usable bladder capacity due to retained urine. Large overnight volumes point toward nocturnal polyuria. In older adults the chapter uses at least one-third of 24-hour urine output during normal sleeping hours. Assess late fluid intake, leg edema, diabetes and obstructive sleep apnea. Leg fluid can return to the circulation on reclining and increase nighttime diuresis. Gabapentin and pregabalin are among the drugs listed as causes of edema (pp. 561–562, Table 38-4).

Record voiding times and volumes across the full day and night for about three days. The first morning void counts toward nighttime production. In the worked diary the man passes 1,020 mL overnight on one night and his largest single void is 400 mL. Even at that capacity he would have to empty about three times to pass the overnight volume. Treat the contributor to urine production or disrupted sleep before escalating prostate treatment for nocturia (pp. 562–563, Table 38-5).

## Watchful waiting and indications for referral

**Management & drugs**

- Address reversible contributors and use shared decisions; many patients can begin with education, behavioral measures or watchful waiting (p563, p566).

Active observation suits men with uncomplicated mild to moderate symptoms who prefer it and can return for follow-up. Discuss fluid timing, voiding habits, drugs that can precipitate retention and how to recognize a full bladder that fails to empty. In one trial of moderate symptoms, improvement at three years averaged 38% with watchful waiting and 66% after TURP. The choice depends partly on how much the symptoms bother the patient today (pp. 563, 565–566).

Retention, recurrent urinary infection, bladder stones or hematuria warrant prompt urologic assessment. The chapter gives **over 200 mL** as an example of a large residual warranting immediate referral (p563), flags **over 300 mL** in Figure 38-5 (p559), and lists retention with a residual **over 250–300 mL** for prompt referral (p565). New renal impairment or hydronephrosis increases urgency. An incontinent man with a distended bladder can have overflow leakage. A storage drug can worsen the residual (pp. 562–565).

> Current practice (not the book): NICE advises immediate catheterization for acute urinary retention and catheterization for chronic retention with impaired renal function or hydronephrosis. [NICE CG97, section 1.7, treating urinary retention](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg97/chapter/recommendations#treating-urinary-retention).

## Choosing medication by the patient's aim

- Tamsulosin **0.4–0.8 mg daily** or alfuzosin **10 mg daily** can improve LUTS; dizziness and hypotension still occur despite greater selectivity (p566).
- Tamsulosin can cause ejaculatory effects and floppy iris syndrome. Alert the cataract surgeon; stopping it does not reliably eliminate the risk (p566).

Alpha blockade relaxes smooth muscle in the outlet and usually begins to improve symptoms in two to four weeks. Available agents have broadly similar symptom efficacy. Ask about postural symptoms and falls, especially with terazosin or doxazosin. Even the more selective agents can cause hypotension. Review antihypertensive treatment and tell the ophthalmologist about current or previous tamsulosin before cataract surgery (pp. 564–566).

- Finasteride/dutasteride shrink the prostate and reduce progression risk, but symptom improvement can take **6–10 months**; sexual adverse effects are possible (p566–567).
- These reductase inhibitors lower PSA by **40–60%** during the first year, complicating interpretation where screening is appropriate (p567).

Finasteride and dutasteride reduce conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone. The longer-term aim in a man with an enlarged gland is to lower the risk of progression, retention and surgery. Sexual adverse effects include reduced libido and erectile difficulty. In trials of combined alpha blockade and reductase inhibition, early relief was attributable chiefly to the alpha blocker. The combination reduced longer-term progression more than either drug alone in selected men, particularly those with larger glands. In CombAT, retention or surgery occurred by four years in 4.2% with combination therapy, 5.2% with dutasteride and 12% with tamsulosin (pp. 566–568).

- Tadalafil **5 mg daily** is approved for male LUTS in the chapter; combining PDE inhibitors with alpha blockers can cause dangerous hypotension (p566–567).
- Add-on antimuscarinic or mirabegron benefit is modest; weigh antimuscarinic harms, blood-pressure effects and limited evidence in patients with large residuals (p567).

Daily tadalafil can treat urinary symptoms and erectile dysfunction in the same patient. In the comparison reviewed in Chapter 38, adding a PDE-5 inhibitor to an alpha blocker produced an average additional IPSS improvement of about 1.7 points, below the three-point difference considered clinically meaningful. In short trials of drugs that relax the bladder, patients generally had residuals below 200 mL. Modest benefits, largely for frequency, must be weighed against anticholinergic cognitive effects or a rise in blood pressure with mirabegron (pp. 564–568).

Plant extracts such as saw palmetto vary in composition. The later randomized saw palmetto trial found no advantage over placebo for moderate to severe symptoms. Few adverse effects in short trials do not settle long-term safety. The chapter also describes investigational intraprostatic drugs and other experimental approaches (p. 568).

## Surgical options and their trade-offs

TURP removes obstructing adenoma through the urethra and leaves the prostate capsule in place. Consider surgery for persistent bothersome symptoms despite appropriate medical treatment, or for complications such as recurrent retention, stones or renal insufficiency attributable to obstruction. Demonstrated outlet obstruction with preserved detrusor function predicts a better symptom response. Discuss anesthesia, bleeding, retrograde ejaculation, postoperative retention and the frail patient's risk of functional or cognitive decline (pp. 565, 568–569).

- **Bipolar TURP.** The circuit allows isotonic saline irrigation. This reduces the dilutional hyponatremia of classic TURP syndrome compared with monopolar resection using hypotonic irrigant. Relief from TURP can persist for years but may diminish. Average reoperation rates in the chapter are 1% to 2% per year (p. 568).
- **Laser vaporization or enucleation.** GreenLight vaporizes tissue while coagulating blood vessels, lowers the transfusion risk and leaves no specimen for pathology. In a multicenter trial, catheterization and hospital stay were shorter than with TURP and the overall need for reoperation was similar at two years. Laser enucleation removes adenoma for morcellation and can treat even large glands. Simple prostatectomy remains an option for very large glands (p. 569).
- **Prostatic urethral lift.** Suture implants retract obstructing tissue. In a trial TURP gave greater symptom and flow improvement at two years, while the lift better preserved ejaculation. Transurethral incision can suit a small gland, generally below 30 mL, with less bleeding and a shorter procedure than TURP (pp. 569–570).
- **Other procedures and prevention.** Needle ablation generally gave less durable relief than TURP. The chapter advises against microwave thermotherapy because of limited efficacy and against urethral stents because of their complications, including migration, infection or encrustation. Physical activity, a diet rich in vegetables and attention to obesity and diabetes have observational links to lower risk. Intervention trials had not established prevention of BPH (p. 570).

**Exam traps**

- Alpha blockers treat symptoms rapidly relative to reductase inhibitors, but the latter have evidence for reducing retention/surgery risk (p566–567).
- Prostate size, obstruction and symptom severity correlate weakly; a high symptom score does not itself diagnose obstruction (p555, p562).
