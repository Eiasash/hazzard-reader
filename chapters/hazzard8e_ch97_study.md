## Cortisol deficiency at the bedside

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-76624b03e2c62f9093ab0142" data-exam-read-page="1531" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Recognize the pattern before ordering the test.** Loss of appetite, abdominal discomfort, weight loss, weakness and an orthostatic fall can be the entire presentation. Primary adrenal destruction can impair both cortisol and aldosterone production, leading to salt loss and hyperkalemia. In central or steroid-induced deficiency, renin and angiotensin usually preserve aldosterone. June 2023 Q21 gives no unequivocal mineralocorticoid deficit. A subnormal stimulated cortisol alone cannot decide whether fludrocortisone is needed (pp. 1531–1532).

**Interpret the units.** With a standard 250-microgram corticotropin test, the traditional stimulated cortisol cutoff at 30 or 60 minutes is 18 **micrograms** per deciliter, approximately 500 nmol/L. Use the local assay’s validated threshold. Both p. 1531 and June 2023 Q21 print mg/dL. Read the vignette’s 10 versus 18 as µg/dL. [Endocrine Society, recommendation 2.1](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-76624b03e2c62f9093ab0142" data-exam-read-page="1532" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Replace a basal need, then plan for stress.** The chapter commonly uses hydrocortisone 15–20 mg daily in older patients, with the larger dose on waking and any smaller dose at lunchtime. Late dosing may cause insomnia. Its plan for fever of 101.5°F (about 38.6°C) or higher is three times the maintenance dose for three days. If vomiting prevents oral dosing, use the emergency hydrocortisone injection and seek urgent medical attention. Severe illness or major surgery requires parenteral stress coverage. Teach the patient and caregiver, arrange a steroid alert and check that the emergency kit is usable (pp. 1531–1532, Table 97-3).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0dd3353be9d7e981544cb1cf" data-exam-read-page="1532" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Five milligrams of prednisone is clinically relevant after eight months.** A small oral dose can meet resting needs while suppressing the ability to mount a stress response. Under current guidance, a stable outpatient taking less than 10 mg/day who develops a minor febrile illness and can absorb tablets may temporarily need 10 mg prednisone daily, generally for two to five days until well. Use an individualized plan. Hemodynamic instability or repeated vomiting changes the route and urgency. In the rheumatoid arthritis vignette, assess fracture risk separately and maintain adequate calcium intake (pp. 769, 1532). [ESE and Endocrine Society, glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency, Table 8](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae250).

**A fracture can precipitate crisis.** In June 2022 Q9, shock after a hip fracture, prednisone in the patient’s belongings and cushingoid findings make an inadequate cortisol response dangerous enough to treat immediately. Give IV hydrocortisone and isotonic fluid, assess for bleeding and other causes of traumatic shock, and continue stress coverage. A glucose of 160 mg/dL does not explain the shock. In a stable outpatient with persistent weakness and orthostatic hypotension, there is time to test adrenal reserve. The June 2022 question cites the Geriatrics Review Syllabus. Chapter 97 supplies related physiology and stress-dose teaching (pp. 1531–1532). [Endocrine Society, adrenal crisis, recommendation 4.1](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-755606bb446afd8eb85ee844" data-exam-read-page="1530" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Exposure to excess steroid can coexist with poor endogenous reserve.** Prednisone, dexamethasone and other corticosteroids can cause thin skin, bruising, proximal wasting, osteoporosis and low-trauma fractures. These signs overlap with common changes in later life. Broad violaceous striae or easy bruising outside sun-exposed areas strengthens concern. Iatrogenic excess is the first cause to look for (p. 1530).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-755606bb446afd8eb85ee844" data-exam-read-page="1531" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Take a route-by-route exposure history.** Ask about oral, injected, inhaled and topical treatments and nonprescription products. Review whether the dose can be reduced while keeping the underlying illness controlled. Abrupt withdrawal may expose ACTH suppression. An endogenous cortisol-secreting adrenal tumor or ACTH-producing pituitary tumor is much less common and warrants endocrine assessment when exposure does not explain the phenotype (pp. 1530–1532).

## Hypercalcemia, PTH and the decision for surgery

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-cbefe6f6ea86d7a584e4cf49" data-exam-read-page="1537" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Chronic versus rapid change.** Most primary hyperparathyroidism is discovered on a chemistry panel. In an older adult, fatigue, proximal leg weakness, constipation, depressed mood or declining cognition can accompany even modest chronic hypercalcemia. Kidney stones, cortical bone loss at the distal radius and low-trauma fractures give more specific clues. Severe hypercalcemia that rises quickly can cloud consciousness, especially with volume depletion or an additional cause (p. 1537).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-cbefe6f6ea86d7a584e4cf49" data-exam-read-page="1538" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Confirm the abnormality before attributing cognitive decline to it.** Check albumin and repeat calcium, or measure properly handled ionized calcium. When using the conventional adjustment in mg/dL, albumin must be in g/dL. The printed equation on p. 1538 labels albumin g/L. Review prior calcium values for chronicity, medications and supplement exposure, then measure intact PTH. Urinary calcium and creatinine can help distinguish familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D detects coexisting deficiency (p. 1538, Table 97-8). [Fifth International Workshop, biochemistry](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.4677).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d0fbd7b7469793665d39657d" data-exam-read-page="1538" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**A low PTH changes the differential.** At calcium 11.5 mg/dL and PTH below the reference interval, the normal feedback response is intact. Myeloma can cause osteolysis and kidney injury, making it the best fit in the confused inpatient with creatinine 3. Thiazides can also cause PTH-independent hypercalcemia, but no exposure is given. Malignancy can act through bone destruction, PTH-related peptide or calcitriol production in lymphoma (p. 1538, Table 97-9).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-dda6ac411f9ab187b0ed6d34" data-exam-read-page="1538" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**A normal PTH can be inappropriately high during hypercalcemia.** Autonomous secretion by a single adenoma is the usual primary disorder. Secondary hyperparathyroidism is a compensatory response to impaired calcium balance, often in chronic kidney disease or vitamin D deficiency. Following severe, prolonged secondary disease, autonomous parathyroid hyperplasia can produce high calcium with high PTH. That sequence supports tertiary disease. Milk-alkali syndrome and thiazides usually suppress PTH unless another process coexists (pp. 1536–1538).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-22e94436a9815853eed097a3" data-exam-read-page="1539" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Read the skeletal and preference options separately.** Table 97-10 lists serum calcium more than 1 mg/dL above the upper limit, marked DXA loss or vertebral compression fracture, and renal involvement. A T-score of minus 2.1 alone is osteopenia. A fragility vertebral fracture can qualify independently of the T-score. Informed patient preference may also favor surgery after a discussion of risks, potential benefits and alternatives (pp. 1538–1539).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-474c586de9e8dcfbda4ad496" data-exam-read-page="1539" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Renal indications in the book.** Table 97-10 lists creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min, stones or nephrocalcinosis on imaging, or urinary calcium above 400 mg in 24 hours (p1539).

> Current practice (not the book): For asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism, 24-hour urine calcium >250 mg in women or >300 mg in men is a renal surgical indication. Other indications include eGFR or creatinine clearance <60, nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis, calcium >1 mg/dL above the upper limit, vertebral fracture, a T-score ≤−2.5, or age <50. [Fifth International Workshop, 2022, recommendation 5.1](https://doi.org/10.1002/jbmr.4677).

**If observing.** Maintain hydration and normal recommended calcium intake, avoid excess calcium or vitamin D, minimize immobilization and review thiazides. Follow renal function and serum calcium annually and assess bone density at one- to two-year intervals. Cinacalcet can lower serum calcium and PTH. Bisphosphonates can improve bone density. Neither effect by itself proves fracture prevention in this disorder (pp. 1538–1539).

## Testosterone, prolactin and the pituitary

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f4c9254576a742f22bfe7d34" data-exam-read-page="1533" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Confirm both parts of the diagnosis.** Weakness, depressed mood or reduced libido alone are nonspecific. Look for compatible symptoms or signs plus low testosterone on at least two separate mornings, preferably fasting. Concurrent illness and medications can lower testosterone transiently. With altered SHBG or a borderline total result, obtain an accurate free testosterone measurement or validated calculation. High gonadotropins favor testicular failure, whereas low or inappropriately normal levels suggest central or functional suppression (pp. 1533–1534).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f4c9254576a742f22bfe7d34" data-exam-read-page="1534" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**An active androgen-sensitive tumor changes the decision.** Metastatic prostate cancer precludes testosterone even when levels are low. Active breast cancer is another contraindication. Anemia, depression and osteoporosis warrant evaluation and may accompany hypogonadism. Testosterone can improve libido and hemoglobin in selected symptomatic men. Assess hematocrit before treatment, at three to six months, then yearly. A value above 54% requires reducing or withholding treatment and looking for hypoxia or sleep apnea. Revisit symptoms and stop an ineffective trial. Discuss prostate screening according to age and life expectancy (pp. 1534–1536).

> Current practice (not the book): In TRAVERSE, transdermal testosterone was noninferior to placebo for major cardiovascular events in men with hypogonadism and high cardiovascular risk. Atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism occurred more often with testosterone. Clinical fractures also increased (HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.04–1.97). In 2025 the FDA removed boxed-warning language concerning cardiovascular events and required warnings about increased blood pressure. [TRAVERSE, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37326322/) · [Fracture substudy, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38231621/). [FDA testosterone labeling changes, 2025](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-issues-class-wide-labeling-changes-testosterone-products).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d12eab8258f56c4efbb24244" data-exam-read-page="1528" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Review medicines for prolactin elevation.** Dopamine normally suppresses prolactin secretion. Risperidone, haloperidol, metoclopramide and domperidone can remove that inhibition. Verapamil is the named calcium-channel blocker. For a mild rise, repeat after quiet rest, check TSH and review medicines and kidney function. Levels of 50–250 ng/mL may have multiple causes or indicate a prolactinoma. Above 250, a large prolactinoma is especially likely. Refer for endocrine assessment when the elevation is moderate or severe, or when there are mass effects or hypogonadism (pp. 1527–1529, Tables 97-1 and 97-2).

**Incidental sellar masses.** A solid lesion merits a history for hormone excess and measurement of prolactin. For a solid or cystic lesion at least 1 cm, also assess the cortisol, thyroid and gonadal axes and its relation to nearby structures. A normal-looking pituitary hormone can be inappropriately low for its target gland’s output. Central cortisol deficiency is the most dangerous missed deficit. The chapter recommends serial sellar MRI for macroincidentalomas, usually every one to two years (p. 1529).

## Water balance, potassium and adrenal masses

**Distinguish hormone secretion from renal response.** Aging can increase basal and osmotically stimulated vasopressin while impairing renal concentrating capacity. Aldosterone and renin may fall, and thirst or access to water may be limited. An immobile older person can become hypernatremic during water deprivation. Conversely, lower GFR and reduced ability to excrete water favor hyponatremia, especially with pulmonary or CNS disease or drugs such as SSRIs and carbamazepine. Even mild chronic hyponatremia is associated with falls and gait disturbance. Blunting of the nighttime ADH rise contributes to nocturnal polyuria. If using desmopressin, watch for hyponatremia (pp. 1529–1530; related renal physiology on pp. 586, 1270).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f7f1da57f641c281b8dd1d40" data-exam-read-page="1533" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Resistant hypertension plus hypokalemia suggests aldosterone excess.** Increased distal sodium retention favors potassium loss. Screen with paired morning aldosterone and renin, correcting low potassium and accounting for interfering medicines and age-related low renin. By contrast, an incidental serum potassium of 6.4, as in May 2024 Q55, requires timely confirmation and assessment. Marked thrombocytosis can release potassium during clotting. Plasma potassium can reveal the discrepancy (pp. 1270–1271, 1532–1533).

**An incidentally detected adrenal mass raises two questions.** Assess hormone secretion and the likelihood of malignancy separately. The book says lipid-rich attenuation **<10 HU** on CT excludes malignancy and pheochromocytoma (p1532). Paroxysmal hypertension with headache, palpitations and sweating supports testing with plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines. Consider autonomous cortisol secretion and screen for primary aldosteronism when appropriate (pp1532–1533).

> Current practice (not the book): <span id="current-adrenal-hu" data-reader-metadata="navigation"></span>A homogeneous lesion measuring **≤10 HU on unenhanced CT** needs no further imaging. Hormonal assessment is a separate decision. [ESE/ENSAT 2023, recommendations 2.3 and 3.2–3.10](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvad066). ESE recommends metanephrines for adrenal lesions without typical benign adenoma imaging features. Evaluate for cortisol autonomy with a 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test when appropriate. In frail patients with limited life expectancy the test may be omitted. [ESE/ENSAT adrenal incidentaloma guideline, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37318239/).

## Focal bone pain, vitamin D and ovarian aging

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f37f1403dad31e7fe082843a" data-exam-read-page="1541" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Night pain in pagetic bone.** Focal overactive resorption is followed by haphazard new bone formation. The bone enlarges but loses mechanical strength. An ache in the pelvis or proximal femur can worsen at night or with loading. Deformity can cause regional osteoarthritis, skull involvement can impair hearing and a vertebral lesion can compress neural structures. Plain films show mixed lytic and sclerotic changes, coarse trabeculae and expansion. Alkaline phosphatase often rises but can be normal when disease is confined to one bone. Bone scintigraphy helps define extent (pp. 1541–1542).

**Treat pain and preserve function.** Consider zoledronic acid for active, symptomatic Paget disease. Correct hypocalcemia or vitamin D deficiency, check renal function and use the labeled 5 mg IV dose over at least 15 minutes only if appropriate. The Paget formulation is contraindicated below creatinine clearance 35 mL/min or with acute renal impairment. Provide gait aids, physical therapy, hearing evaluation or orthopedic referral according to the site and functional effects (p. 1542). [Zoledronic acid prescribing information, sections 2.6–2.9 and 4](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5a9b3737-9ce6-4a89-b76e-6aab79eba9cf).

**Use 25-hydroxyvitamin D to assess stores.** PTH can maintain calcitriol despite depleted stores. Limited sun exposure, malabsorption, impaired kidney function and enzyme-inducing drugs all affect vitamin D balance. Severe deficiency can cause osteomalacia, proximal weakness, falls and fractures. For established severe deficiency the chapter offers daily replacement or a limited high-dose weekly course followed by maintenance. Hypercalciuria can precede overt toxicity. Hazzard describes the historical AGS high-risk target as above 30 ng/mL. The [original AGS consensus](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.12631) calls 30 ng/mL the minimum goal. The IOM general-population threshold answers a different question. Contemporary prevention guidance does not set a universal screening threshold for healthy adults (pp. 1539–1540). [Endocrine Society, 2024 guideline communication](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae322).

**After menopause.** Ovarian estrogen production falls. Bone density declines most rapidly in the first three to five years, then more slowly. Ask about low-trauma fractures, height loss and family history, and assess fracture risk. Across endocrine disorders, choose replacement doses with declining clearance, multimorbidity, functional goals and treatment burden in mind (pp. 1525–1527, 1536).

## Summary

### General Principles
- Most clinical studies define endocrine dysfunction against young-adult reference ranges, not age-specific ones; hypogonadism in older men is defined by testosterone **below young-male norms**, while the upper limit of normal **serum thyrotropin rises with age** (~**7 to 8 mIU/L** in those 80+) (p1526).
- Hormone levels can be transiently altered by acute illness (eg, slight TSH elevation in recovery) or drugs (eg, opioids suppressing gonadotropin/testosterone) — repeat testing once the patient's baseline health is restored before acting on an abnormal result (p1526).
- Hormone replacement doses in older adults may be **lower than young-adult doses** because metabolic clearance of thyroid hormone, hydrocortisone, and testosterone falls with age (p1526).

### Pituitary
- Hyperprolactinemia tiers: **mild 20–50 ng/mL** (rarely needs treatment, review medicines, renal function and TSH and repeat a mild elevation after rest), **moderate 50–250 ng/mL** (refer — typically a prolactinoma or multiple contributing causes, including combined drug effects), **severe >250 ng/mL** (refer — almost always a large prolactinoma) (p1525, p1528).
- Pituitary incidentaloma prevalence **10–20%** on autopsy/imaging series; macroincidentaloma defined as **≥1 cm** and needs a fuller hormone-deficiency workup plus serial MRI every 1–2 years (p1528–1529).
- Chronic hypopituitarism incidence **<0.5 per 100,000/yr**; most common deficits are secondary hypogonadism and hypothyroidism, but **secondary hypoadrenalism, though less common, can be life-threatening** — these patients need an alert bracelet (p1529).

### Adrenal
- **Cushing syndrome: the most common cause in older patients is iatrogenic** (supraphysiologic corticosteroid dosing) (p1526, p1530).
- **Adrenal insufficiency: the most common cause is rapid discontinuation of chronic glucocorticoid therapy** (p1526, p1531).
- Adrenal insufficiency sick-day rule: **triple the daily hydrocortisone dose (~60 mg)** for 3 days with fever ≥**101.5°F**; give **100 mg IM hydrocortisone** if oral intake is impossible (p1526, p1531–1532).
- Usual glucocorticoid replacement: **15–20 mg hydrocortisone/day in divided doses**, avoiding late-afternoon/evening dosing (insomnia risk) (p1531).
- The standard cosyntropin test measures peak serum cortisol at 30 or 60 minutes; the traditional, assay-dependent threshold is **18 μg/dL** (500 nmol/L), and p1531 misprints the unit as mg/dL ([Endocrine Society, recommendation 2.1](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency)).
  > Current practice (not the book): peak cortisol below 500 nmol/L (18 μg/dL, assay dependent) at 30 or 60 min indicates adrenal insufficiency — Endocrine Society primary adrenal insufficiency guideline, 2016.
- The chapter reports incidental adrenal masses in **about 4%** of high-resolution abdominal CT scans and greater malignancy risk above **4 cm**. Its printed low-attenuation cutoff for excluding malignancy and pheochromocytoma is **<10 HU** (p1532). [Current practice note](#current-adrenal-hu).
- Primary hyperaldosteronism affects **5–10%** of hypertensive patients; workup = plasma aldosterone:renin ratio, withdraw **spironolactone for 4 weeks** before testing when safe and feasible; primary hyperaldosteronism suggested by aldosterone **>10 ng/dL** with renin activity **<1 ng/mL/hr** (p1532–1533).

> Current practice (not the book): The 2025 Endocrine Society guideline conditionally suggests screening people with hypertension for primary aldosteronism using aldosterone and renin. Interpret results in the context of potassium, medications and pretest probability. [Endocrine Society primary aldosteronism guideline, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40658480/).
- Pheochromocytoma incidence **<5 per million/year** but becomes more common after age 50 (p1533).

### Gonadal / Testes
- Male hypogonadism diagnosis needs symptoms/signs **plus** a **consistently low morning testosterone on ≥2 occasions** (p1533).
- Testosterone Trials (T Trials): **790 men**, average age **72**, baseline testosterone **<275 ng/dL**, treated to target **500–800 ng/dL** — improved sexual function and hemoglobin/anemia, no cognitive benefit, and increased erythrocytosis and PSA/urologic referrals (p1534–1535).
- Monitor hematocrit at baseline, 3–6 months, then yearly; **hematocrit >54%** → reduce or stop testosterone (p1535–1536).
- PSA/DRE screening on testosterone therapy is **not recommended in men ≥70 years** or those with life expectancy <10 years (p1536).
- Contraindications to testosterone: active prostate or breast cancer; use caution with PSA **>4.0 ng/mL**, hematocrit **>50**, severe LUTS (IPSS **>19**), or recent MI/stroke (p1534).
- Reversible causes of functional hypogonadism to screen for before treating: obesity; medications (opioids, glucocorticoids, megestrol); hyperprolactinemia (usually drug-induced); malnutrition/energy deficit; chronic systemic illness (eg, malignancy); organ failure (lung, heart, liver, kidney) (Table 97-5, p1534).

### Calcium, Parathyroid, Vitamin D
- Primary hyperparathyroidism etiology: single adenoma **80%**, diffuse hyperplasia **10–15%**, multiple adenomas **<5%**, carcinoma **<1%** (p1536).
- Incidence peaks at **6.3 per 1000 person-years** at age **65–74**; women:men **2:1** (p1536).
- Parathyroidectomy indications: serum calcium **>1 mg/dL above ULN**, DXA T-score **<−2.5**, creatinine clearance **<60 mL/min**, or 24-hour urine calcium **>400 mg/24h** (p1526 KCP, p1538–1539).
- Hypercalcemia causes split by PTH status: **PTH-dependent** (common: primary hyperparathyroidism; uncommon: tertiary hyperparathyroidism, lithium, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, ectopic PTH) vs **PTH-independent** (common: malignancy — osteolytic or PTHrP-humoral — and 1,25(OH)₂-vitamin D-mediated lymphoma; uncommon: thiazides, AKI, parenteral nutrition, immobilization with malignancy/Paget, granulomatous disease, hyperthyroidism/adrenal insufficiency, vitamin D/A toxicity, milk-alkali, hyperproteinemia) (Table 97-9, p1538).
- **Vitamin D thresholds differ within the chapter.** Key Clinical Point 2 defines deficiency in older patients as serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D **<30 ng/mL** (p1525). Page 1539 separately reports AGS sufficiency **>30 ng/mL** for frail, high-risk older adults and IOM sufficiency **>20 ng/mL**, insufficiency **12–20 ng/mL**, and deficiency **<12 ng/mL**.
- Vitamin D deficiency prevalence **40–50%** in community-dwelling older adults (p1539).
- Treatment: mild-moderate deficiency → vitamin D2/D3 **800–1000 IU/day**; severe deficiency → vitamin D3 **2000 IU/day for 6–12 months**, or vitamin D2 **50,000 IU weekly for 8–12 weeks** then vitamin D3 800–1000 IU daily (p1540).
- Toxicity is rare up to the IOM safe upper limit of **4000 IU/day**; early biochemical toxicity signs appear **>90 ng/mL**, severe toxicity **>150 ng/mL** (p1540).

### Paget Disease
- Prevalence **2–3%** in persons **55+** in the US, predominantly White people, slightly more men (p1541).
- Distribution: pelvis **~70%**, lumbar spine/proximal femur **~50%**, skull **~40%**, proximal tibia/humerus **~30%** (p1541).
- Serum alkaline phosphatase is elevated in **90%** of patients; normal/near-normal AP does not exclude monostotic disease (p1542).
- Treatment of choice: **zoledronic acid 5 mg IV** over 15–20 minutes, often a single dose sustaining remission **5–6 years**; contraindicated if creatinine clearance **<35 mL/min** (p1542).

* Also, hematocrit >50 is the caution threshold before starting testosterone (p1534) but >54 is the threshold to stop/reduce it during treatment (p1535–1536) — these are not the same number.*

## Recall

**3. What is the sick-day glucocorticoid rule for a patient with known adrenal insufficiency?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Triple the usual daily hydrocortisone dose (about 60 mg total) for 3 days with a fever ≥101.5°F; give 100 mg IM hydrocortisone if oral intake is not possible (p1526, p1531–1532).

</details>

**4. What serum prolactin range defines "mild" hyperprolactinemia that generally does not need treatment, and what should still be checked?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

20–50 ng/mL; check a serum TSH to exclude primary hypothyroidism as the cause (p1525, p1528).

</details>

**7. Which drug can interfere with the aldosterone-to-renin ratio, and what washout can be used when clinically safe?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Spironolactone for 4 weeks if safe and feasible. Monitor potassium and blood pressure and arrange alternative therapy when needed (pages 1532–1533).

</details>

**8. In the Testosterone Trials, what was the baseline testosterone cutoff and the treatment target range?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Baseline total testosterone <275 ng/dL; testosterone dosed to a target of 500–800 ng/dL (p1534–1535).

</details>

**12. What laboratory and skeletal thresholds does Hazzard give for parathyroidectomy, and how do the current renal thresholds differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The book gives serum calcium >1 mg/dL above the upper limit of normal, T-score <−2.5, creatinine clearance <60 mL/min or 24-hour urine calcium >400 mg (pp1526, 1538–1539). For asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism, the Fifth International Workshop uses 24-hour urine calcium >250 mg in women or >300 mg in men and a T-score ≤−2.5 (2022, recommendation 5.1).

</details>

**14. What is the safe upper limit of daily vitamin D3 intake per the Institute of Medicine, and above what level does early biochemical toxicity appear?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Safe upper limit 4000 IU/day; early biochemical indications of toxicity have been reported when 25-hydroxyvitamin D exceeded 90 ng/mL (the first indication of over-treatment is hypercalciuria) (p1540).

</details>

**15. What is the treatment of choice for Paget disease, and what renal function cutoff contraindicates it?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Zoledronic acid 5 mg IV (often a single dose); contraindicated if creatinine clearance <35 mL/min (p1542).

</details>
