# Chapter 25 Study: Patient-Centered Management of Chronic Diseases

## Summary

**Self-management is part of clinical care**
- Patients manage most chronic illness at home; clinicians and systems must support knowledge, skills and practical capacity (p345-346).
- Core skills include problem solving, decisions, resource use, partnership, tailoring and action; tasks include medical, emotional and role management (p346).
- Support draws on behavioral models and organized chronic care: education alone is only one component (p347-350).
- Options include individual/group education, coaching, care management and technology; match the approach to patient needs and available support (p350-352).

**Match the model to the person**
- Single-disease guidelines fit functional adults resembling trial populations better than people with complex multimorbidity (p349-350).
- Symptom-focused and palliative approaches may fit advanced illness; intermediate complexity often requires explicit trade-offs among goals (p350).
- Disease-specific interventions can improve selected outcomes, but effects and outcome measures vary; evidence for multimorbidity support is less developed (p351-354).
- Housing, food, finances, access, trust and culture shape the ability to self-manage; community resources and tailored support matter (p354-355).
- Applying multiple guidelines can create drug–drug, drug–disease and disease–disease harms, alongside treatment burden (p355).
- The book describes **2 hours/day** of self-care plus another **2 hours/visit** spent in health facilities for patients with multiple chronic conditions and caregivers (p355).

**Care around priorities**
- Ask what outcomes matter and what treatments the patient is willing and able to undertake; independence and meaningful activities may outweigh disease targets (p355-356).
- Patient Priorities Care identifies values, actionable outcome goals, care preferences and the main issue to address (p356, p358).
- Use priorities to decide which treatments to start, continue or stop; serial trials can clarify benefit, burden and trade-offs (p356, p358).
- VA Whole Health combines the person, self-care, professional care and community; the approach can apply beyond veterans (p356, p358).
- Coordinate across clinicians and involve care partners. Revisit decisions as health, capacity and priorities change (p356-358).

### Translate a priority into a manageable plan

- Separate the daily work of chronic illness into medical tasks, emotional challenges and changes in family or social roles. A patient who knows the medication schedule may still need help managing grief, asking for resources or adapting the schedule to what happens at home (p346).
- Self-efficacy means confidence in carrying out a specific task. The chapter's arthritis self-management program uses trained facilitators and teaches skills such as pain coping and communication with clinicians. Its reported gains include confidence and self-management behavior, with less certain effects on general health status (p347, p351).
- Distinguish a desired outcome from a care preference. In the chapter's case, attending church and feeling less tired are outcomes; willingness to undertake extra visits, tests or medication changes helps determine which plan is feasible. Agree on a realistic goal with a time frame, make a change and reassess its effect on that activity (p356–358).
- Cognitive impairment can make complex heart-failure self-care difficult. Check whether the person or caregiver can carry out the necessary steps. When clinicians across specialties give conflicting instructions, bring the plan back to the patient's main goal and the total workload (p353–356).

## Exam traps

- Confidence in performing a task, knowing the facts and having practical resources to carry it out are different parts of self-management (p346–349, p354–355).
- A disease-specific program may improve one measure without demonstrating the same benefit in a frail, multimorbid population (p349–354).
- An outcome goal describes what the patient wants to achieve; a care preference describes which actions are acceptable and feasible (p356–358).

## Recall drill

**1. Where does most work of chronic disease management take place?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In the patient's daily life, usually outside the clinic, often with help from family or other care partners (p345–346).</details>

**2. What are the three broad types of self-management task?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Medical management, emotional management and managing changes in roles or relationships (p346).</details>

**3. Which skills help a patient adapt the plan when a problem arises?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Problem solving, decision making, use of resources, partnership with clinicians, tailoring and taking action (p346).</details>

**4. What is self-efficacy in this context?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Confidence in the ability to carry out a particular self-management behavior (p347, p351).</details>

**5. Why might education alone fail to change health behavior?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Knowledge does not guarantee confidence, practical skills, access or help to carry out the plan (p346–350, p354–355).</details>

**6. Which outcomes improved in the arthritis self-management program described?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Self-efficacy and self-management behavior improved; general health status may also improve (p351).</details>

**7. When are single-disease guidelines more readily applicable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In healthier, functional adults who resemble the populations studied and have fewer competing conditions and goals (p349–350).</details>

**8. What becomes a central aim in a patient with serious, life-limiting disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Relief of urgent symptoms and care fitted to the person's goals, with advance planning and caregiver support (p350).</details>

**9. How may cognition affect heart-failure self-management?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cognitive impairment can compromise confidence, day-to-day self-care and ability to manage worsening disease (p353–354).</details>

**10. What social factors deserve assessment before labeling a plan unsuccessful?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Food and housing security, finances, access, trust and cultural fit may constrain the patient's choices (p354–355).</details>

**11. How much time can health care consume in the example for multimorbidity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>On average <strong>2 hours per day</strong> for health-related tasks and <strong>2 hours per visit</strong> for patients and caregivers (p355).</details>

**12. What makes an outcome goal actionable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It is meaningful to the patient, specific, realistic and linked to a time frame, such as resuming a valued activity (p356–358).</details>

**13. What is a care preference?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The treatments, tests, visits or self-management tasks the person is willing and able to undertake for that goal (p356–358).</details>

**14. How can a team resolve uncertainty about whether a treatment serves a patient's goal?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Agree on a trial of a change, define the outcome that matters, reassess benefits and burden, and revise the plan together (p356, p358).</details>

**15. What are the elements of the VA Whole Health approach described here?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The person, self-care, professional care and the surrounding community are considered together (p356, p358).</details>
