# Chapter 25 Study: Patient-Centered Management of Chronic Diseases

## Summary

**Self-management is part of clinical care**
- Patients manage most chronic illness at home; clinicians and systems must support knowledge, skills and practical capacity (p345-346).
- Core skills include problem solving, decisions, resource use, partnership, tailoring and action; tasks include medical, emotional and role management (p346).
- Support draws on behavioral models and organized chronic care: education alone is only one component (p347-350).
- Options include individual/group education, coaching, care management and technology; match the approach to patient needs and available support (p350-352).

**Match the model to the person**
- Single-disease guidelines fit functional adults resembling trial populations better than people with complex multimorbidity (p349-350).
- Symptom-focused and palliative approaches may fit advanced illness; intermediate complexity often requires explicit trade-offs among goals (p350).
- Disease-specific interventions can improve selected outcomes, but effects and outcome measures vary; evidence for multimorbidity support is less developed (p351-354).
- Housing, food, finances, access, trust and culture shape the ability to self-manage; community resources and tailored support matter (p354-355).
- Applying multiple guidelines can create drug–drug, drug–disease and disease–disease harms, alongside treatment burden (p355).
- The book describes **2 hours/day** of self-care plus another **2 hours/visit** spent in health facilities for patients with multiple chronic conditions and caregivers (p355).

**Care around priorities**
- Ask what outcomes matter and what treatments the patient is willing and able to undertake; independence and meaningful activities may outweigh disease targets (p355-356).
- Patient Priorities Care identifies values, actionable outcome goals, care preferences and the main issue to address (p356, p358).
- Use priorities to decide which treatments to start, continue or stop; serial trials can clarify benefit, burden and trade-offs (p356, p358).
- VA Whole Health combines the person, self-care, professional care and community; the approach can apply beyond veterans (p356, p358).
- Coordinate across clinicians and involve care partners. Revisit decisions as health, capacity and priorities change (p356-358).

### Translate a priority into a manageable plan

- Separate the daily work of chronic illness into medical tasks, emotional challenges and changes in family or social roles. A patient who knows the medication schedule may still need help managing grief, asking for resources or adapting the schedule to what happens at home (p346).
- The chapter's arthritis self-management program uses trained facilitators and teaches skills such as pain coping and communication with clinicians. Its reported gains include confidence and self-management behavior, with less certain effects on general health status (p347, p351).
- Distinguish a desired outcome from a care preference. In the chapter's case, attending church and feeling less tired are outcomes; willingness to undertake extra visits, tests or medication changes helps determine which plan is feasible. Agree on a realistic goal with a time frame, make a change and reassess its effect on that activity (p356–358).
- Cognitive impairment can make complex heart-failure self-care difficult. Check whether the person or caregiver can carry out the necessary steps. When clinicians across specialties give conflicting instructions, bring the plan back to the patient's main goal and the total workload (p353–356).

### The work done between visits

A clinician prescribes treatment at a visit; the patient and care partner decide how it will actually be carried out the next morning and every day thereafter. That work includes taking and refilling medicines, recognizing changes in symptoms, arranging visits, managing emotions and keeping up valued roles. A record may show that a patient received education without revealing whether they can read the instructions, obtain the medicine, recognize the threshold for action or reach the team when something changes. Ask the patient to describe an ordinary day before adding another task (p345–347).

Distinguish **self-management** from **self-management support**. The first describes what the person does to live with the condition, including the decisions made when symptoms disrupt a routine. The second is what clinicians, caregivers, community programs and the health system do to help. Coaching a patient to recognize worsening edema, helping them solve the problem of obtaining a scale and arranging for someone to respond to a concerning change are different kinds of support. The patient retains an active role even when tasks have to be adapted or shared because of frailty, vision loss or cognitive impairment (p345–348, p353–355).

The chapter separates medical management from emotional management and management of roles. Knowing a drug schedule addresses one medical task. It does little by itself for the person who avoids leaving home because incontinence makes a long journey embarrassing, or for the spouse worried about coping with another episode of illness. Skills that transfer across conditions include recognizing a problem, choosing among realistic actions, finding resources, working with the clinical team and adapting a plan to the person's circumstances. Ask which part of the task has broken down instead of assuming that repeating instructions will restore it (p346–347).

### Behavior theories describe different barriers

Table 25-1 compares four approaches. In the Health Belief Model, perceived susceptibility and seriousness, expected benefits, barriers and cues to action shape behavior. A patient may believe a preventive treatment works but regard its likely benefit as too remote relative to the work it requires today. The stages-of-change model asks whether a person is considering a change, preparing for it, acting or maintaining it. The useful conversation differs between someone who has not decided to change and someone who already wants to act but cannot get supplies (p347–348).

Social cognitive theory emphasizes observation, reinforcement, self-control and confidence in performing the specific behavior. Having done part of a task successfully can be a better starting point than general encouragement to be more independent. The social ecological model extends the analysis to the household, organization, neighborhood and policy environment. A person can have the skill and motivation to follow a food or activity plan and still lack affordable food, accessible transport or a safe place to exercise. These frameworks suggest different interventions; the obstacle identified in the patient's account determines which one fits (p347–349).

Self-efficacy is confidence about a particular task, not a general personality trait or proof that the task was successfully performed. A patient can be confident using an inhaler yet unsure whether to seek help for new breathlessness. Ask for a demonstration where appropriate and explore the actual decision that caused difficulty. A peer can model a practical skill; a clinician may need to settle conflicting instructions. A home or community resource may be needed when the difficulty reflects the environment. The aim is to change what the person can realistically accomplish (p346–351).

### What the care-delivery model contributes

Table 25-2 compares the Chronic Care Model, the patient-centered medical home and principles of effective primary care. The Chronic Care Model places an informed, engaged patient in contact with a prepared, proactive team. Community resources, decision support, self-management support, organized delivery and clinical information systems all contribute. The medical home emphasizes accessible, continuous team care, coordination and improvement. The primary care framework adds responsibility for a patient panel, timely access and the patient–team partnership. The common feature is a system capable of responding to the patient between isolated encounters (p347–349).

Take a person who monitors blood pressure at home. The intervention is incomplete if no one has explained what counts as a concern, who will review readings or what to do when the usual clinician is away. Home monitoring linked to a responsive team differs from simply collecting more readings. The same distinction applies to a portal, a telephone call or a group visit. Decide whether the intervention conveys information, teaches a skill, changes a clinical decision or makes follow-up possible. A program called “self-management” may provide one of these functions and leave the others to the practice (p348, p350–353).

### Matching evidence to a heterogeneous older population

Read Figure 25-1 from the standpoint of the particular patient. At one end are people with few conditions and preserved function for whom a disease guideline may fit well if consistent with their preferences. At the other end are advanced or end-stage illnesses in which comfort, symptom management or de-escalation may dominate. The middle group has increasing disease burden, declining function and substantial treatment workload without an obvious single goal. Neither automatic intensification nor automatic withdrawal follows from age. Explicit priorities and current care planning help choose among competing reasonable actions (p349–350).

Published disease trials often enroll people different from older patients who have several coexisting conditions. The intended effect of a treatment on one disease may be credible while its net value in a particular combination remains uncertain. Ask about the time needed to realize benefit, the likelihood of adverse effects, the ability to carry out monitoring and the opportunity cost for a patient and caregiver with limited energy. Adding each individually endorsed action can create a plan no participant in the original trials had to follow. The decision concerns the whole plan the patient will experience at home (p349–350, p354–356).

### What the intervention actually delivers

Table 25-3 separates the targeted disease from the intervention and its method of delivery. Diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and COPD are frequent targets. A program may provide patient education, community-based behavioral support, a component of formal chronic disease management, or a combination. A nurse can coach by telephone, a pharmacist can reconcile medicines, a peer group can demonstrate practical approaches and an app can prompt monitoring. A group visit supported by the patient's practice can connect a problem to an immediate clinical decision; a stand-alone class may need a separate route back to the prescriber (p350–352).

Diabetes programs illustrate why device use or attendance is only an intermediate outcome. A person might learn a monitoring skill but need a simpler prescription to use the information safely. The chapter reports improvements in selected outcomes from diabetes education and community-based training, including experience with culturally adapted support. The intervention must fit language, literacy, vision, dexterity and daily routines. When evaluating a study, identify whether it measured knowledge, glycemic control, use of hospital care or outcomes the patient experiences. Improvement in one does not guarantee improvement in all of them (p351–354).

The Arthritis Self-Management Program developed by Lorig uses trained facilitators and social cognitive theory. Participants practice coping with pain, communicating with clinicians and managing exercise, nutrition and medicines. The reported gains in self-efficacy and self-management behavior are clearer than the evidence about general health status. The broader Chronic Disease Self-Management Program applies transferable skills to several diagnoses, including cardiac and pulmonary disease. In a patient with both arthritis and heart failure, one useful outcome could be an achievable activity plan rather than two separate sets of instructions about exercise (p351–352).

Heart failure exposes the limits of a single-disease approach. The chapter reports that **fewer than 5%** of heart-failure patients have heart failure alone; many have **five to six comorbidities**, sometimes including cognitive impairment. A plan to respond to weight, edema or breathlessness involves memory, interpretation, physical ability and access to help. COPD support similarly may combine education, coaching, monitoring and coordination. Stroke or depression can add communication, motivational or functional limitations. A program's disease label does not show whether it addressed these barriers or trained the person who actually helps at home (p352–354).

### How to read the evidence on support and technology

A review of **58 studies** in primary care examined what effective support programs actually did. A separate review evaluated **29 studies** of nurse-led interventions. The programs combined different services, enrolled different populations and measured different outcomes. “Nurse-led” tells you who delivered an intervention; it does not tell you whether the nurse could change treatment, coordinate referrals or only provide information. Before copying a program, identify the tasks, the support available to the team and how the patient could act on the advice (p353).

The telehealth overview covered **56 systematic reviews and 256 studies** across several chronic conditions. Some reported improved outcomes; the results were inconsistent. Contact that saves the patient a long trip can still shift work onto a caregiver who must operate equipment, upload readings and troubleshoot access. Conversely, useful monitoring can be lost if a clinical service does not integrate it into its workflow. Ask which action follows an alert, who takes responsibility and whether that process solves a problem the patient values. The mode of communication is one component of the intervention, not its mechanism of benefit (p351–353).

The evidence specifically addressing people with multiple chronic conditions is more limited than the single-disease literature. The chapter describes a review of **14 studies** of heart-failure self-management in people with cognitive impairment. It also describes guidance synthesized from **eight international guidelines** and **246 recommendations** for multimorbidity and polypharmacy. The resulting principles focus on matching workload to capacity, identifying goals, tailoring the care and medication plan and reviewing it over time. A patient who cannot execute a plan unaided may benefit from support, simplification or redistribution of tasks. Identifying impaired capacity is not a reason to abandon the patient's own priorities (p353–355).

### Treatment burden, equity and conflicting instructions

Table 25-4 makes the workload visible. Count not only tablets but refills, monitoring, recognition of adverse effects, trips for blood tests, specialist appointments, imaging, procedures, discomfort and recovery. The book describes approximately **two hours a day** on health-related tasks and an additional **two hours per visit** in health facilities for patients with multiple conditions and caregivers. These are estimates of effort, not a universal budget. Ask which task consumes a disproportionate share of this household's time and whether it is essential to a goal the patient wants to pursue (p355).

Consider interactions at three levels. A drug for one problem can worsen another condition; two drugs can interact; and one disease can make another disease's self-management task impossible. For example, a person may understand the need for monitoring but be unable to read the display or climb the steps to the clinic. The caregiver doing the transport may also be the person preparing meals and assisting with medicines. A missed appointment can reflect competing obligations rather than unwillingness. Ask about cost, trust, food, housing, transport and the local services actually available before interpreting that absence (p353–355, p357–358).

For a culturally and linguistically diverse population, translation alone may leave major barriers untouched. Ask what the family already does, who shares treatment decisions, which instructions conflict with work or caregiving, and what sources of support they trust. An intervention can be adapted without assuming that everyone from the same community has the same preferences. A person with limited digital access may benefit from a telephone contact, an accessible in-person visit or community support. Follow the referral through to a usable resource rather than counting the recommendation as successful delivery of care (p353–355).

### Reconstructing a plan in the chapter's case

The case on p357 concerns an 80-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension and stable heart failure with a reported ejection fraction of **40%**. He also has knee osteoarthritis, prostate symptoms and macular degeneration. His wife helps with transportation and several instrumental activities; he manages his medicines. Blood pressure is at goal and HbA1c has ranged from **7.5% to 7.8%**. Yet he is tired, has trouble climbing steps and has stopped attending church. The laboratory values are case findings, not recommended targets for every older person. The decline in participation is the problem he brings to the visit (p357).

The book lists his actual regimen so the reader can see the workload. His glucose-lowering medicines are metformin **500 mg twice daily**, glyburide **10 mg twice daily** and insulin glargine **15 units nightly**. He also takes aspirin **81 mg daily**, furosemide **40 mg twice daily**, extended-release metoprolol **100 mg daily**, lisinopril **10 mg twice daily**, spironolactone **25 mg daily** and atorvastatin **80 mg daily**. Tamsulosin **0.4 mg nightly** and acetaminophen **650 mg twice daily** add symptom-directed treatment. These doses describe this patient's starting list. Read it alongside his fatigue, fall concerns, glucose checks, foot care, weighing and travel to multiple specialists. The clinical question is what can safely be simplified while preserving the outcomes he values (p357–358).

The primary clinician tried reducing a beta-blocker and later insulin, but different specialists increased the treatments again. The case does not establish that either drug is the cause of fatigue or should always be stopped. It shows why a monitored trial is uninterpretable when the reason for it, intended outcome and follow-up are not shared. Identify the main activity the patient wants to regain, review the likely benefits and burdens of each intervention, agree who will coordinate changes and communicate the rationale. A trial also needs a stopping or restart decision if symptoms worsen or the hoped-for benefit does not appear (p357–358).

Coordinate access to physical therapy, fall prevention, community or faith-based activities and transport when those are the means of reaching the patient's goal. Choose carefully which visits require an examination and which can be conducted remotely. Check what the wife is able and willing to do; a plan that silently assigns her many new duties can undo an apparent reduction in patient burden. The chapter presents options for managing several diseases in one plan rather than a universal beta-blocker or insulin dosage. Dose changes in a vignette cannot be transferred to someone with a different indication, risk or priority (p357–358).

### Turning values into shared decisions

Figure 25-3 distinguishes values, achievable health-outcome goals, care preferences and the one thing a person most wants addressed. “Independence” is a value; getting from the door to a particular meeting is an observable outcome. Willingness to undergo another test or accept a daily monitoring task is a care preference. Document these distinctions so a second clinician can understand why an otherwise reasonable treatment was started, deferred or stopped. Revisit the goal and trade-offs after a change in health, a new caregiving constraint or a disappointing trial (p355–358).

Figure 25-2 presents the VA Whole Health approach in terms of the person, self-care, professional care and community. A coach can help a person express their purpose and identify shared goals for a personalized health plan. The options discussed extend beyond conventional appointments to exercise, nutrition, pain management and selected complementary approaches. Patient Priorities Care and Whole Health both start from what the person wants to be able to do. The practical test is whether the agreed plan relieves a real obstacle, fits the work the household can undertake and is followed by a reassessment that can change the next decision (p356, p358–359).
The Patient Priorities Care comparison in a large private practice included primary care clinicians and cardiologists and was **nonrandomized**. Relative to usual care, participants reported a greater reduction in treatment burden, and the reported odds ratio for deprescribing was **2.05**. This supports testing whether an existing treatment contributes to a patient's goals; it does not identify which drug should be withdrawn in the next patient or demonstrate improved survival (p358).

## Exam traps

- Self-efficacy refers to confidence in a specific task. It is distinct from knowledge, independence and the number of sessions attended (p346–351).
- Do not equate improved disease markers, reduced service use, reduced daily treatment workload and better quality of life (p351–355).
- A person can have adequate motivation and still lack affordable food, transport, equipment or an accessible professional response (p353–355).
- The case patient's HbA1c range describes his starting position; it is not an exam target for every person with multimorbidity (p357).
- An outcome the patient hopes for and the treatment workload they will accept to pursue it answer different questions (p356–358).
- A medication-change trial loses its meaning when another clinician reverses it without knowing the goal, observation interval or result (p357–358).


- Confidence in performing a task, knowing the facts and having practical resources to carry it out are different parts of self-management (p346–349, p354–355).
- A disease-specific program may improve one measure without demonstrating the same benefit in a frail, multimorbid population (p349–354).
- An outcome goal describes what the patient wants to achieve; a care preference describes which actions are acceptable and feasible (p356–358).

**8. What becomes a central aim in a patient with serious, life-limiting disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cognitive impairment can compromise confidence, day-to-day self-care and ability to manage worsening disease (p353–354).</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Food and housing security, finances, access, trust and cultural fit may constrain the patient's choices (p354–355).</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>On average <strong>2 hours per day</strong> for health-related tasks and <strong>2 hours per visit</strong> for patients and caregivers (p355).</details>

**12. What makes an outcome goal actionable?**
