# Parkinson disease and related disorders

## Recognizing parkinsonism

### The motor syndrome

Parkinsonism describes a pattern of **six motor features**: bradykinesia, rest tremor, rigidity, loss of postural reflexes, flexed posture and freezing of gait. Bradykinesia is central. Parkinson disease (PD), the most common neurodegenerative cause, affects **1% to 2% of people over 60** (p935). A clinical diagnosis can be wrong even when the pattern seems familiar: at autopsy **10% to 20%** of clinical diagnoses are not confirmed, while **about 20%** of older adults without known parkinsonism have midbrain Lewy bodies. Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra pars compacta are the diagnostic standard at autopsy (p936).

### Frequency and susceptibility

Incidence across all ages is **4.5–19 per 100,000 person-years**, rising to **27.2–107.2 per 100,000 person-years** after age 60. Prevalence is **100–200 per 100,000** overall and **more than 1 in 100** after age 60. PD is about **1.5 times** as common in men (p936). Manganese exposure has been associated with higher risk. Smoking and caffeinated coffee show inverse associations, which do not establish a preventive treatment (pp936–937).

More than **90%** of PD is sporadic, despite the **20 known PARK loci** described at publication. GBA (*GBA1*) mutations are associated with early-onset PD, particularly in patients of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (pp937–938). PSP, MSA and corticobasal syndrome together have roughly **5% to 10% of the prevalence of PD**. Dementia with Lewy bodies is more common than these other atypical syndromes. This prevalence ratio does not give the proportion of all parkinsonism that is atypical (pp938, 941).

## From nigral cell loss to motor symptoms

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-66ccc4806be30db38956eb5f mcq-c52415f4d7c73e695361110a mcq-5461c18463aace56c08459fe"></span>

Loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and misfolded alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies are characteristic. Normally dopamine promotes movement through the excitatory D1 direct pathway and limits the inhibitory D2 indirect pathway. Loss of dopamine shifts the balance toward motor inhibition. After the third decade, normal aging costs **0.5% to 1%** of nigrostriatal terminals per year. In PD the posterior and dorsal putamen are preferentially affected: an estimated **60% to 80%** of posterior-putaminal terminals are lost by motor onset (p938).

### Premotor and cognitive features

In the **six-stage** Braak model, olfactory, sleep and autonomic changes can precede the motor syndrome in stages **1–2**. Motor symptoms emerge with nigral involvement at **stage 3**. Cortical Lewy bodies and cognitive decline occur in stages **5–6** (p939). Longer exposure to repetitive head impacts in contact sports has also been associated with neocortical Lewy body disease and with parkinsonism and dementia (Chapter 64, p999).

## Examining and distinguishing the syndromes

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-13fabc9f0d6f8fd34d20852e mcq-a107f1d68006598cfe6fe356 mcq-639704ca0a2e07b231f57fca mcq-fe0fe26539d3780822b8f018"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-c1a3dda929d99ddfdddc6b5c"></span>

### Rest tremor, bradykinesia and rigidity

Look first for slowness and loss of amplitude with repeated movement, often with gradual asymmetric onset. A supported limb trembles at rest in typical PD. Tremor while holding out the arms, writing or using a spoon suggests essential tremor, although mixed tremor and coexistence are possible. Bilateral hand and head tremor that improves after alcohol fits the latter pattern. A beta blocker was the accepted treatment in the December 2021 case; propranolol has evidence for limb tremor (p940; AAN clinician summary, 2011).

The 2015 MDS criteria for clinically established PD require bradykinesia, no exclusion criterion and **at least two of four** supportive findings: a levodopa response, levodopa-induced dyskinesia, rest tremor, and olfactory loss or cardiac sympathetic denervation. A rest tremor beginning in one hand supports PD; it is not a prerequisite. Impaired postural reflexes can accompany bradykinesia later. Early prominent postural instability, symmetric onset, an absent rest tremor or a poor levodopa response should prompt consideration of an alternative (pp939–941, Tables 61-3 and 61-4).

### What levodopa and DaT imaging can tell you

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-584a3091801f4b8f87c4f086 mcq-592ed9671a2804086a3079b9 mcq-5aff8ef3237b31ebc14818a4"></span>

Bradykinesia, limb rigidity and distal dexterity usually respond best to levodopa. Tremor responds variably. Axial symptoms such as hypophonia, dysphagia and postural instability often persist because they also reflect nondopaminergic injury (p939).

DaT SPECT demonstrates nigrostriatal terminal loss. Essential tremor normally preserves uptake; DaT SPECT is approved to distinguish it from PD in atypical tremor and can help distinguish drug-induced parkinsonism. PSP, MSA and DLB can all reduce uptake. An abnormal scan therefore cannot choose among them (pp940–941).

Severe early orthostatic hypotension, bladder or erectile dysfunction, cerebellar signs and nocturnal stridor suggest MSA. Median onset is in the **sixth decade** and mean time from diagnosis to severe disability is **about five years** (pp942–943). Early backward falls and loss of downward gaze favor PSP. Asymmetric rigidity or dystonia with limb myoclonus, apraxia, cortical sensory loss or an alien limb favors corticobasal syndrome (Table 61-3, p940; Table 61-4, p941).

## Treating motor symptoms

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-991b3e62e78d2629cd03bad6 mcq-7a90308e12b3ad9cc1b6f81a mcq-16cdb9d2de9f736383dda397"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-3ed537d091c4ac13b638e895"></span>

### Choosing initial treatment

Levodopa crosses the blood–brain barrier. Carbidopa (Sinemet in combination) or benserazide (Madopar in combination) limits peripheral conversion, increases availability and reduces gastrointestinal adverse effects. Levodopa gives the strongest motor benefit when symptoms impair quality of life. After **five years**, more than **50%** develop wearing-off or dyskinesias (p943). Agonists cause fewer motor fluctuations but more hallucinations, confusion and psychosis in older people; levodopa is generally safer after **age 70** (p944).

Pramipexole and ropinirole may cause sudden sleep attacks, daytime somnolence, ankle edema and impulsive eating, spending, gambling or sexual behavior. Ask the family as well as the patient and consider reducing the agonist as tolerated if the timing fits. Ergot agonists—pergolide, bromocriptine and cabergoline—can cause valvular, retroperitoneal or pleuropulmonary fibrosis; pergolide was withdrawn from the US market. Transdermal rotigotine can help when swallowing or nocturnal akinesia is a problem. Subcutaneous apomorphine or the sublingual film (Kynmobi) can rapidly rescue an off state; apomorphine may be the most powerful agonist and can cause intense nausea (pp943–946).

### When benefit wears off

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-8967c8cfe2bde76fda19ff30 mcq-704a1bc1d1352115cfd1c494"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-229f23d07885f884509c55bc"></span>

With wearing-off, a useful levodopa dose fails to control symptoms for **six hours**. Shorten the interval or add a COMT inhibitor. Entacapone **200 mg with each levodopa dose** increases effective exposure and may require a lower levodopa dose to avoid dyskinesia. Tolcapone comes in **100- and 200-mg tablets**; the printed account attributes US market withdrawal to hepatotoxicity (p944). Opicapone is given once daily. Once-daily istradefylline, an A2A antagonist, also reduces off time with carbidopa/levodopa in patients with fluctuations. Selegiline, rasagiline and safinamide (MAO-B inhibitors) offer mild symptomatic benefit, including a small benefit for mild wearing-off, without proven neuroprotection. Domperidone acts peripherally against gastrointestinal upset from levodopa or agonists and is unavailable in the US (Table 61-5, p944; p945). Continuous intestinal levodopa is an option for troublesome fluctuations when a good motor response remains (pp943–946).

### Dyskinesias at peak dose and at its edges

Irregular, dance-like movements at peak exposure favor smaller individual levodopa doses, sometimes more often to maintain mobility. Diphasic dyskinesias instead occur as a dose starts and ends; mixed chorea and dystonia predominantly affect the legs (Table 61-6, pp945–946). The September 2024 dose-reduction answer assumes peak-dose dyskinesia; the question does not give the timing.

Amantadine blocks NMDA receptors. Immediate-release amantadine is described at **100 mg twice daily** for motor symptoms, with higher doses for dyskinesia (p944); **100–200 mg twice daily** is described for selected patients with peak-dose dyskinesia (p946).

> **Current practice: amantadine and renal function.** Reduced renal clearance in older adults can cause accumulation. Adjust immediate-release dosing to renal function, age and neuropsychiatric tolerability; monitor confusion, hallucinations, edema and livedo reticularis. The **300–400 mg/day** range is not a routine geriatric starting dose (US prescribing information).

Buspirone up to **20 mg/day** is another option described for selected patients with dyskinesia (p946).

## Nonmotor complications and advanced treatment

### Psychosis and depression

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-c422e711b9b76e815a130a7a mcq-d1250479506c48ee2bf3b920"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-b722617aa8858adb7209c45d"></span>

Look for acute illness and other psychoactive drugs first. If hallucinations track dopaminergic treatment, reduce the burden as motor function allows. Withdraw antiparkinsonian drugs stepwise over **about three days** when severe psychosis requires withdrawal: abruptly stopping levodopa can cause a neuroleptic-malignant-like syndrome (p946). Depression may precede motor symptoms and needs its own assessment; lowering dopaminergic treatment can improve medication-related sleepiness or psychosis without treating depression (pp946–947).

If symptoms persist, the printed options include quetiapine starting at **25 mg at bedtime** or pimavanserin **17 mg once or twice daily**, with a lower dose for CYP3A4 inhibitors (p946).

> **Current practice: pimavanserin dose.** Pimavanserin 34 mg PO once daily, no titration; 10 mg once daily with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors — FDA Nuplazid label, 2026.

Clozapine may be considered if quetiapine or pimavanserin is ineffective or hallucinations are frightening. The printed regimen specifies **weekly blood counts**, an agranulocytosis risk of **1% to 2%**, and discontinuation if leukopenia develops (pp946–947).

> **Current practice: clozapine blood counts.** For stable normal absolute neutrophil counts, the US Clozaril label specifies weekly ANC in months 1–6, every 2 weeks in months 7–12 and monthly thereafter — [US prescribing information, Table 1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=90876802-0e3a-44c9-9ff7-1754dfbe736a).

### Sleep, autonomic symptoms and cognition

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-dac49a0a832949e5496bfde8"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-fb38ffdddc5df94e127de7f1"></span>

Loss of REM atonia permits dream enactment and injury to a bed partner. Ask about the movements and protect the sleeping space. Clonazepam **0.5–2 mg at bedtime** can suppress episodes; melatonin is a less effective option that may be tried first. Nighttime rigidity from wearing-off may instead respond to longer nocturnal motor coverage. Constipation and drooling are nonmotor features, and anticholinergic drugs can worsen constipation. Trihexyphenidyl and benztropine are best avoided after **age 70** because of cognitive impairment, hallucinations and an association with freezing of gait (pp942, 947–948).

Attention and executive function are often impaired. About **50%** develop dementia within **10 years** of diagnosis (p942). Parkinson disease dementia (PDD) follows established motor parkinsonism by **more than one year**; dementia preceding, accompanying or beginning within one year of motor onset favors DLB (p939; DLB Consortium, 2017).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-987cf6b109f3fe9f8d976b83"></span>

Presyncope with an abnormal ECG in a patient taking donepezil should prompt consideration of bradycardia from cholinesterase inhibition (Chapter 59, p912). Distinguish it from adverse effects and motor fluctuations of antiparkinsonian drugs (pp945–946).

### Surgery and device-assisted treatment

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7c399ddd1ed048f9ccb52dbf"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-5fc8a91de7d78511b9bbba72"></span>

Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus was FDA-approved in **2002**. Consider it for disabling fluctuations, dyskinesia or medication-refractory tremor. A good levodopa response helps predict which motor features improve, with refractory tremor an exception. Recurrent falls from postural instability, substantial cognitive impairment or serious depression reduce suitability. Postural instability and other axial deficits do not improve reliably (p949).

> **Current practice: infusion when apomorphine or DBS cannot control symptoms.** NICE recommends subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa infusion as an option for adults with advanced levodopa-responsive Parkinson disease, severe motor fluctuations and hyperkinesia or dyskinesia despite available medicines, only when apomorphine or DBS cannot be used or no longer controls symptoms and the commercial arrangement applies; FDA approved it on 16 October 2024 for motor fluctuations in adults with advanced Parkinson disease - NICE TA934 2023, 1.1 p4; FDA NDA216962 approval package cover and approval letter p1

Focused ultrasound produces an irreversible lesion that cannot be reprogrammed. The printed description uses “radiofrequency ablation to the STN,” mentions other possible targets and says the procedure “does not involve anesthesia” (p949).

> **Current practice: focused ultrasound target and anesthesia.** The 2018 US device indication specifies **unilateral thalamotomy** for medication-refractory tremor in tremor-dominant PD, using ultrasound thermal ablation of the ventral intermediate nucleus. Local anesthesia has been used for placement of the stereotactic frame — [FDA device indication, 2018](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf15/P150038S006B.pdf), [prospective PD thalamotomy study, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8280323/).

## Continuing care

Exercise, gait cueing and physical therapy support mobility. Ask about falls, swallowing and the tasks for which help is needed. Discuss feeding choices and goals with the patient and caregivers as independence declines (pp939, 942, 948–950).

## Key numbers

**≥2 of 4** supportive findings for clinically established PD (p940) · **1 year** separates the PDD and DLB timing conventions (p939) · **6 hours** is the wearing-off threshold (p944).

## Recall

**1. What six motor features define parkinsonism, and which one is considered central?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Bradykinesia, rest tremor, rigidity, loss of postural reflexes, flexed posture, and gait freezing; bradykinesia is the central feature (p935).

</details>

**4. What fraction of PD cases is sporadic, and how many PARK genetic loci are recognized?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Over 90% sporadic; 20 PARK loci (p937).

</details>

**8. What is the "1-year rule" used to distinguish PDD from DLB?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In PDD, established motor parkinsonism begins more than one year before dementia. Dementia before, together with, or within one year after parkinsonism fits the DLB timing convention (p939, DLB Consortium 2017).

</details>

**10. Can DaT SPECT distinguish PD from PSP, MSA, or DLB?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No — all of these show nigrostriatal dopaminergic loss on DaT SPECT (p940–941). It is approved to distinguish essential tremor from PD in atypical tremor, and can help distinguish PD from drug-induced parkinsonism (p941).

</details>

**11. Which ergot dopamine agonists carry a fibrosis risk, and which one has been withdrawn from the US market?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Pergolide, bromocriptine, and cabergoline carry fibrosis risk (cardiac valvulopathy, retroperitoneal/pleuropulmonary/pericardial fibrosis); pergolide has been withdrawn from the US market (p944).

</details>

**12. Above what age is levodopa generally preferred over dopamine agonists, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Above age 70, because dopamine agonists are more likely than levodopa to cause hallucinations, confusion, and psychosis in older adults (p944).

</details>

**13. How is "wearing-off" defined, and what is the simplest first-line management step?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An adequate levodopa dose that fails to last at least 6 hours before parkinsonian symptoms return (p944); more frequent levodopa dosing may be the simplest approach; an MAO-B inhibitor can help mild wearing-off slightly and COMT inhibitors are useful (p945).

</details>

**14. What is the only established effective antidyskinetic agent, and what receptor does it block?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Amantadine, via blockade of glutamate NMDA receptors (antiglutamatergic action) (p944).

</details>

**15. For drug-induced psychosis in PD, if anti-PD medications must be withdrawn, how should this be done and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Stepwise, over about 3 days — sudden levodopa withdrawal risks a neuroleptic-malignant-like syndrome (p946).

</details>
