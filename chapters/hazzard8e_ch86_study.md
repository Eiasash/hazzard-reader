# Chapter 86 Study: Hepatic, Pancreatic, and Biliary Diseases

## Summary

**Liver aging & injury**
- Liver tests and coagulation factors generally do not change with age; functional hepatocyte volume and hepatic blood flow decline (p1337–1338).
- Phase I metabolism of some drugs falls by **as much as 50%**; phase II is relatively preserved. Start low and titrate slowly (p1338).
- Drug-induced liver injury is more frequent and severe in older adults; review prescription and nonprescription exposure (p1342).
- Metabolic syndrome raises fatty-liver risk. Alcohol abstinence can improve alcohol-associated disease even after decompensation (p1339–1340).

**Chronic disease & malignancy**
- PBC: cholestatic tests, antimitochondrial antibody; ursodeoxycholic acid slows progression. Autoimmune hepatitis requires biopsy; immunosuppression is often effective (p1342).
- PSC: evaluate with cholangiography, usually MRCP; manage cholangitis/strictures and monitor for cholangiocarcinoma (p1342–1343).
- Ascites, encephalopathy and frailty complicate cirrhosis care. Search for triggers of each encephalopathy episode; balance symptom benefit against treatment burden (p1343).
- Transplantation can suit selected older adults; age alone is not a contraindication (p1343).

**Biliary disease**
- The key points say that older patients may lack fever, nausea, vomiting or severe abdominal pain (p1338). The diagnostic discussion also describes typical right-upper-quadrant or epigastric pain (p1345). Ultrasound is the initial investigation; ERCP is therapeutic for common-duct stones (p1345).
- Incidental asymptomatic gallstones do not require treatment. Early laparoscopic surgery suits appropriate patients; drainage may suit very ill patients (p1346).

**Pancreas**
- Table 86-7 describes fat, fibrosis and reduced pancreatic weight after the seventh decade, and steadily declining bicarbonate after the fourth decade (p1348). Pancreatic duct caliber can increase with age; frank exocrine deficiency is rare without underlying pathology.
- Acute pancreatitis: pain with amylase/lipase typically **at least 3×** normal; lipase remains elevated longer (p1348).
- Without gallstones or substantial alcohol exposure, consider underlying malignancy; check triglycerides early (p1348).
- Chronic pancreatitis: pain, malabsorption and diabetes; enzyme replacement treats malabsorption, and normal enzymes do not exclude disease (p1349).
- Pancreatic cancer needs staging for vascular invasion/metastases. Mucinous cysts/main-duct IPMN may warrant surgery in patients fit enough; branch-duct lesions may undergo surveillance (p1349–1350).

### A painful gallbladder in a frail patient

Incidental stones are common; a substantial new illness with right-upper-quadrant tenderness warrants a different assessment. Prolonged cystic-duct obstruction causes acute cholecystitis. Ultrasound can show stones, pericholecystic fluid, edema or a sonographic Murphy sign. When the gallbladder fails to fill on a HIDA scan despite opacification of the bile ducts, cystic-duct obstruction is supported. Fever, nausea or vomiting may be absent even with serious inflammation (p1344–1345).

A common-duct stone may cause jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, pancreatitis or ascending cholangitis. Pain, fever and jaundice together suggest cholangitis. Ultrasound is the usual first study, but its reported sensitivity for common-duct stones is only **20% to 38%**; duct dilatation alone is also nonspecific in older people. MRCP can clarify the anatomy, while ERCP can remove a stone or decompress infected bile (p1344–1345).

For a frail, acutely unstable patient with acute cholecystitis, weigh the need for source control against operative risk. Fluids and antibiotics are part of treatment. Gallbladder drainage can provide source control when immediate surgery is unsafe. The book discusses early laparoscopic cholecystectomy for suitable operative candidates (p1346).

### Pancreatic reserve and acute pain

Fat and fibrosis increase with age while pancreatic weight can fall. Bicarbonate output declines, yet frank malabsorption from normal aging alone is unusual. In an older patient with acute pancreatitis, gallstones remain an important cause. In the absence of gallstones or substantial chronic alcohol exposure, investigate other causes including occult malignancy. In chronic pancreatitis, serum enzymes may be normal even in someone with malabsorption (p1348–1349).

### Interpreting liver tests and pharmacology in an older patient

Liver size and the volume of functioning hepatocytes decrease with age, and hepatic blood flow can be lower. Reserve at rest is often sufficient to maintain normal conventional liver chemistries and clotting-factor production. Table 86-1 describes a modest fall in albumin that generally remains within the normal range. A markedly low albumin, jaundice, prolonged coagulation tests or substantially raised liver enzymes therefore deserves a clinical explanation. Consider nutrition and inflammation alongside hepatic synthetic function. The same resting measurements cannot tell whether a patient will regenerate liver after resection or tolerate a serious toxic insult (p1337–1339).

Phase I metabolism includes oxidation and other reactions that depend on the drug, enzyme system and hepatic extraction. Table 86-2 gives diazepam, alprazolam, selected tricyclic antidepressants, several statins, phenytoin, theophylline and warfarin as examples of medicines with substantial phase I handling. Relatively preserved phase II conjugation does not make every drug in a class interchangeable, and an age effect does not justify applying a fixed percentage reduction to every prescription. Assess the actual drug, other interacting medicines, signs of accumulation and the clinical response. Reduced hepatic blood flow can matter even if bilirubin and aminotransferases are normal (p1338–1339).

### Fat accumulation, alcohol and medication-related injury

Hazzard uses the terms NAFLD and NASH. In the chapter's definitions, more than **5% hepatic steatosis** without the inflammatory injury of steatohepatitis differs from steatosis accompanied by inflammation and hepatocyte damage. The second pattern can progress to fibrosis, portal hypertension and liver failure. Many affected patients first present with metabolic risk factors, incidental fatty liver on imaging or a modest biochemical abnormality. Exclude substantial alcohol exposure, medication toxicity, viral disease and other causes before assigning etiology. Noninvasive fibrosis scores followed by elastography can help select patients needing further assessment; the amount of visible fat alone does not stage fibrosis (p1339).

In one US multicenter comparison, older patients with fatty liver more often had steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis than younger participants. This is a cohort finding, not a prediction of the stage in any one older adult. The book describes improved metabolic indices and liver chemistries with weight loss of **more than 10%** in patients with elevated BMI and metabolic syndrome. Ask how this goal interacts with muscle mass and nutritional status in a frail patient rather than interpreting better enzymes as the sole treatment objective. Continue to address the underlying metabolic illness and assess progression instead of assuming that identifying fatty liver completes the evaluation (p1339–1340).

Alcohol-associated injury can be overlooked when history focuses exclusively on youth or occupational disability. Clarify current intake, the pattern over time and the patient's understanding of standard servings. In advanced disease, cessation can improve function even after prior decompensation. Treating ascites and encephalopathy without addressing continuing alcohol exposure leaves a driver of injury in place. Conversely, an abnormal enzyme result in a person reporting alcohol use is not proof of alcohol as the only cause. Consider metabolic, viral and drug-related contributors, and assess what support makes abstinence realistic (p1340, p1343).

Table 86-3 organizes drug injury by pattern. Acetaminophen can produce dose-related hepatocellular necrosis, amoxicillin–clavulanate a cholestatic pattern, and isoniazid hepatocellular injury. Methotrexate is linked to steatosis and fibrosis; alcohol can compound the risk. Amiodarone can mimic fatty liver histologically and disease may progress despite discontinuation. Nitrofurantoin can cause a chronic hepatitis syndrome, an important connection in an older woman treated repeatedly for urinary symptoms. Tamoxifen, phenytoin and several antifungals can also cause injury. Establish start and stop dates, dosing history and nonprescription products. A list of potential culprits alone cannot establish causation when biliary obstruction, infection or another liver disease fits the presentation (p1339–1342).

### Viral and immune-mediated disease

Table 86-4 distinguishes prior exposure, immunity and active disease. Acute hepatitis A is supported by an IgM response; IgG can follow earlier infection or vaccination. Hepatitis A does not cause chronic hepatitis in the chapter's comparison. For hepatitis B, surface antigen supports current infection and surface antibody can reflect immunity. Core antibody distinguishes natural exposure from vaccine-only immunity; ambiguous combinations need interpretation in context. In chronic hepatitis B, viral burden and the state of the liver help guide specialist evaluation. Hepatitis D is associated with hepatitis B; hepatitis E can merit consideration when other causes are excluded. The treatment and prevention options in the table were current when the chapter was written (p1340–1342).

Hepatitis C antibody testing addresses exposure. Table 86-4 abbreviates a positive antibody result as infection and also lists quantitative RNA. For the individual patient, a positive antibody after successful therapy can persist, so establish present viremia before planning treatment. Direct-acting antivirals changed the prospects for cure in older people with chronic HCV; drug interactions and the stage of liver disease still affect selection. Liver injury due to another cause can persist after successful antiviral therapy. In particular, established cirrhosis remains clinically relevant when considering complications and surveillance (p1341–1342).

> Current practice (not the book): A reactive HCV antibody is followed by HCV RNA testing. Detectable RNA establishes current infection; reactive antibody with undetectable RNA usually indicates no current infection. - [CDC hepatitis C testing guidance, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/hcp/diagnosis-testing/).

Distinguish the predominantly small-duct autoimmune process of primary biliary cholangitis from large or medium duct scarring in primary sclerosing cholangitis. In PBC, a cholestatic pattern and the antimitochondrial M2 antibody can establish the diagnosis without routine biopsy in a typical case. Pruritus, fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption and osteoporosis need attention. Ursodeoxycholic acid slows disease progression. The book also names obeticholic acid as a possible add-on for selected patients, warning about greater itching and contraindication in decompensated disease. That historical option has changed in the United States (p1342).

> Current practice (not the book): The US approval for obeticholic acid for primary biliary cholangitis was withdrawn in November 2025. - [FDA withdrawn indications, 2025](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/accelerated-approval-program/withdrawn-non-malignant-hematological-neurological-and-other-disorder-indications-accelerated).

PSC instead calls for imaging of the biliary tree. MRCP can demonstrate the pattern without the procedural risk of ERCP; a dominant stricture or ascending cholangitis may require endoscopic intervention. Monitor for cholangiocarcinoma as well as the effects of progressive cholestasis. Autoimmune hepatitis has a different diagnostic route, with biopsy important to confirmation and assessment of active injury. Immunosuppression can be effective in older adults, but systemic glucocorticoid toxicity can include osteoporosis, impaired glucose tolerance and cataracts. Similar complaints of fatigue or itch do not make these three diagnoses interchangeable (p1342–1343).

Figure 86-1 contrasts the tissue patterns in drug-induced liver injury. Methotrexate illustrates macrovesicular steatosis; valproate, tamoxifen and tetracyclines are associated with microvesicular steatosis and mitochondrial injury. Acetaminophen illustrates zone 3 hepatocellular necrosis, and the final panel shows necrosis from isoniazid. Use the exposure history alongside the biochemical and histologic pattern rather than equating fatty liver with a single cause (p1340).

### Managing complications and recognizing malignancy

Recurrent ascites or hepatic hydrothorax can cause pain, dyspnea, reduced mobility and repeated admissions. Before escalating a diuretic, ask whether the patient can adhere to sodium restriction and reach a toilet, whether renal function or electrolytes are worsening and whether other disease is still injuring the liver. Large-volume paracentesis can relieve symptoms but entails repeat procedures and travel. A transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt can worsen encephalopathy. These tradeoffs matter when the patient prioritizes staying at home. Transplant evaluation considers reserve, comorbidity, functional status and ability to manage postoperative care alongside chronological age (p1343–1344).

Do not attribute every new change in attention to pre-existing cognitive impairment or to irreversible terminal encephalopathy. A recurrent episode warrants a search for gastrointestinal hemorrhage, infection, electrolyte disturbance or sedating medicines. Intracranial bleeding may need to be considered in the appropriate presentation. Lactulose treatment can itself become difficult because of taste, flatulence, loose stool or fecal incontinence. Ask what the patient actually takes and what happens when doses are missed. Treatment planning can require help with medications, mobility, continence and decisions about driving rather than titration of a drug in isolation (p1343).

Hepatocellular carcinoma often develops on a background of cirrhosis. The treatment decision depends on tumor burden, extrahepatic spread, vascular involvement and hepatic reserve. A localized lesion in a patient with adequate reserve presents a different question from extensive cancer in a person already decompensating. Cholangiocarcinoma can present through a dominant stricture, recurrent cholangitis or jaundice, particularly in PSC. Obtaining diagnostic tissue may be difficult because of access and the nature of the lesion. Gallbladder carcinoma may first be found at surgery or present with advanced obstructive disease. In each, ascertain both the potentially curative options and the likely burden of diagnostic or palliative intervention (p1343–1344).

For HCC surveillance, the authors describe ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein **every 6 months** for patients with cirrhosis while detection would still have clinical value. Surveillance and diagnostic staging are separate. A newly suspicious lesion calls for characterization; a serial AFP value or the fact that previous screening was normal does not establish resectability. In carefully selected older patients, a multidisciplinary team can consider resection, transplantation or local treatments such as radiofrequency ablation, chemoembolization or radioembolization. Consider tumor control alongside the probability of precipitating liver failure in the remaining liver (p1344).

A dominant biliary stricture in PSC raises two immediate possibilities with overlapping symptoms: impaired drainage with cholangitis, and cholangiocarcinoma. Drainage may be urgent when infection is present while assessment for a tumor proceeds. Tissue diagnosis can be difficult because an infiltrating tumor may be sparsely cellular and inaccessible. For gallbladder malignancy, long-standing stones can be a risk association, but the high prevalence of benign stones makes a stone an unreliable cancer marker by itself. Interpret a mass, progressive obstruction or concerning wall changes in the complete clinical and imaging context (p1344).

### Stones, duct imaging and source control

A stone in the gallbladder, a stone obstructing the cystic duct and a stone passing through the common duct create different syndromes. Incidentally detected gallbladder stones do not reliably explain diffuse nonspecific abdominal symptoms. Persistent cystic-duct obstruction can produce localized inflammation with continuous right-upper-quadrant or epigastric pain, sometimes radiating to the back or shoulder. Common-duct obstruction can present with jaundice, dark urine, pale stools and pruritus; adding fever and abdominal pain raises concern for ascending infection. Acalculous cholecystitis is particularly relevant in a severely ill or debilitated patient, where the absence of a stone can delay recognition (p1344–1345).

Table 86-5 lists increasing age, female sex, obesity, dyslipidemia, rapid weight loss, certain medications and population associations as factors in gallstone formation. They alter the likelihood of stones, not the distinction between an incidental gallstone, cystic-duct inflammation and infection above an obstructed common duct (p1344).

For suspected acute cholecystitis, ultrasound can show gallstones, edema, fluid around the gallbladder and focal tenderness under the probe. A HIDA study answers a different question: if the biliary tree fills but the gallbladder does not, cystic-duct obstruction is supported. Chronic cholecystitis may show less dramatic imaging changes. The printed chapter notes that older patients commonly still have focal pain even when fever, nausea and vomiting are absent. Do not turn the warning about atypical presentation into a rule that older adults do not experience localized pain (p1338, p1345).

For suspected common-duct stones, ultrasound is convenient but its sensitivity is limited. A duct can be dilated because of age or previous cholecystectomy, so compare old imaging. CT can reveal complications or another diagnosis, and MRCP delineates the ducts noninvasively. In the chapter's cited estimates endoscopic ultrasound has **94% sensitivity and 95% specificity** for choledocholithiasis. ERCP permits sphincterotomy, actual stone extraction and drainage of infected bile; it has procedure-related risks and need not be the initial purely diagnostic study in every patient. The progression in Figure 86-2 illustrates why visualizing a filling defect and removing it are separate decisions (p1344–1346).

Table 86-6 concerns **common-duct** stones specifically. The book gives transabdominal ultrasound sensitivity of **20%–38%** and specificity of **80%–100%**, compared with **94%** sensitivity and **95%** specificity for endoscopic ultrasound. MRCP has reported sensitivity **57%–100%** and specificity **93%–97%**; it is diagnostic. ERCP can treat the obstruction but introduces procedural risks, including pancreatitis. Percutaneous transhepatic access can also be therapeutic and carries bleeding and pain risks (p1345).

The earlier exam scenario of cholecystitis with acute pulmonary congestion and atrial fibrillation tests whether this patient is fit for immediate definitive surgery. Fluids and antimicrobials treat systemic consequences but may not control an infected obstructed gallbladder. Gallbladder drainage can provide source control when acute illness makes cholecystectomy unsafe. The intervention is chosen because of present physiological risk, not because a diagnosis of dementia by itself excludes surgery. For an appropriate operative candidate, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy can be preferable to waiting until complications develop. For asymptomatic uncomplicated stones, the balance is different (p1346).

### From loss of pancreatic reserve to clinically important disease

In Figure 86-3 the common bile duct and main pancreatic duct approach the duodenum at the pancreatic head. The position helps explain why an impacted stone can impair pancreatic drainage and why an obstructing lesion in the head can present with jaundice. The body and tail lie away from that outlet (p1347).

Acinar cells supply enzymes while duct cells contribute bicarbonate-rich fluid. The latter buffers acidic contents delivered to the duodenum. Pancreatic secretory capacity has a reserve large enough that modest physiologic changes do not usually cause symptomatic maldigestion. The chapter's aging table describes increasing fat and fibrosis and a reduction in pancreatic weight after the seventh decade. Bicarbonate output falls after the fourth decade, and the duct in the head or body can become a little wider. For the earlier exam, both declining exocrine function and declining gland weight can be true; increasing fat does not imply that gland weight must increase. Steatorrhea or unexplained weight loss should still prompt investigation (p1347–1348).

Acute epigastric pain radiating to the back, often accompanied by vomiting, suggests pancreatitis in an appropriate clinical setting. Lipase stays raised for longer than amylase, useful when presentation is delayed. Minor enzyme elevations also occur in other conditions, so interpret the magnitude and the clinical syndrome together. Ultrasound can identify a biliary cause. Additional cross-sectional imaging can clarify uncertain presentations, complications or an underlying lesion; the initial clinical diagnosis does not demand that every uncomplicated presentation be scanned solely to confirm a high enzyme value. Table 86-8 includes hypercalcemia, hypertriglyceridemia, drugs, trauma, duct obstruction and tumors alongside gallstones and alcohol (p1348–1349).

Measure triglycerides early because fasting and IV fluids can lower the value rapidly. In a previously well older adult with no gallstones and no history of sustained heavy alcohol use, ask why the pancreatic duct became obstructed. A small tumor may cause pancreatitis before obvious metastatic or constitutional symptoms. Determine cardiopulmonary reserve and renal function when planning fluids; the same amount may be tolerated very differently by someone with heart failure. The book describes bowel rest, IV fluids and analgesia for mild pancreatitis and urgent ERCP when cholangitis or persistent obstruction accompanies gallstone pancreatitis (p1348–1349).

> Current practice (not the book): In mild acute pancreatitis, ACG supports low-fat oral feeding within 24–48 hours as tolerated and early, reassessed hydration. Cholecystectomy before discharge applies to mild biliary pancreatitis; stable necrosis generally allows deferral of intervention while the collection matures. - [American College of Gastroenterology, 2024](https://webfiles.gi.org/links/journals/AJG-Clinical-Guidelines-Highlights-Acute-Pancreatitis-2024-FINAL.pdf).

Severe pancreatitis can become a multiorgan illness. Predicted severity or sterile necrosis alone is not an indication for prophylactic antibiotics in the chapter. Suspect infected necrosis when the clinical course, sepsis and imaging support it; an agent with pancreatic penetration such as imipenem and a specialist drainage strategy may be needed. After an episode, fluid collections may resolve spontaneously. Early satiety, ongoing pain, infection or bleeding can justify intervention in a symptomatic pseudocyst. In alcohol-related pancreatitis, the admission is also an opportunity to offer treatment for the alcohol use disorder rather than discharging the patient after symptom control alone (p1349).

### Chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer and cysts

Fibrosis, acinar loss and ductal distortion in chronic pancreatitis can cause recurrent pain, malabsorption and eventually diabetes. Circulating pancreatic enzymes may no longer rise because functional tissue has been lost. Calcification or duct change on imaging can supply evidence; neither a normal lipase nor attribution of loose stool to a common drug should close the assessment when weight is falling. Treat malabsorption with pancreatic enzyme supplementation; consider nutrition, glycemic management and cause of obstruction as well as pain. A tumor can mimic or cause chronic pancreatitis in an older person (p1349).

Stage pancreatic adenocarcinoma for distant disease and vascular invasion before determining whether curative-intent resection is possible. CT and endoscopic ultrasound provide complementary information. A mass identified because of obstructive jaundice and an incidental tiny cyst require different workups. For a cyst, first ask about pancreatitis and communication with the pancreatic duct, then consider wall or nodule characteristics and whether further information would change the treatment of this particular patient. Fluid analysis assists classification; a mucinous result does not itself stage cancer (p1349–1350).

Table 86-9 contrasts a post-pancreatitis pseudocyst with a serous lesion that can have a central scar and very low malignant potential. Mucinous cystic neoplasms may show peripheral calcification without duct communication; intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms communicate with the duct. The table gives cyst-fluid CEA **above 192 ng/mL** as a feature of the two mucinous groups and **below 10 ng/mL** for the serous group. These are fluid measurements used along with imaging and cytology, not a blood test for cancer. Main-duct involvement carries more concern than branch-duct disease in the book. Resection or surveillance depends on the type, degree of suspicion, surgical fitness and life expectancy (p1350).

Figure 86-4 shows an endoscopic ultrasound-guided aspiration of a cyst ultimately identified as a serous cystadenoma. The image illustrates how fluid was obtained, not a rule that every incidental cyst requires puncture. Interpret fluid, duct anatomy, symptoms and imaging features together when deciding on surveillance, tissue assessment or referral (p1350).

## Exam traps

- The finding of a widened bile duct can reflect age or previous cholecystectomy; compare with prior imaging (p1344).
- Uncomplicated incidental gallstones and acute cholecystitis in a high-risk patient require different decisions (p1344–1346).
- A physiologic decline in exocrine reserve does not usually produce overt pancreatic insufficiency in a healthy older adult (p1348).

## Recall drill

**1. Which liver test abnormalities should prompt investigation instead of attribution to age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Aminotransferases, bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase do not normally rise solely because of aging. Coagulation factors also remain largely stable (p1338).</details>

**2. Which component of drug metabolism is relatively preserved?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Phase II conjugation. Phase I metabolism of some drugs falls by as much as half (p1338).</details>

**3. Which antibody supports primary biliary cholangitis in cholestatic liver disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Antimitochondrial M2 antibody. Ursodeoxycholic acid slows progression, and fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption also needs attention (p1342).</details>

**4. How is suspected primary sclerosing cholangitis usually assessed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cholangiography, generally MRCP initially. ERCP can also treat a dominant stricture (p1342–1343).</details>

**5. What should trigger a renewed search for bleeding, infection or medication effects in cirrhosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A new episode of encephalopathy. Assess electrolytes and consider an intracranial bleed as well (p1343).</details>

**6. Why might a patient stop taking lactulose?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Taste, gas, loose stools and fecal incontinence may make treatment difficult to sustain (p1343).</details>

**7. What distinguishes acute cholecystitis from ascending cholangitis anatomically?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cholecystitis is gallbladder inflammation, often following cystic-duct obstruction. Cholangitis involves infection in the biliary tree, often with common-duct obstruction (p1344–1345).</details>

**8. Which three findings suggest ascending cholangitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Abdominal pain, fever and jaundice comprise Charcot's triad (p1345).</details>

**9. What does a negative ultrasound mean for possible common-duct stones?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It does not reliably exclude them. The reported sensitivity is only <strong>20% to 38%</strong>, and further imaging depends on the clinical suspicion (p1345).</details>

**10. How does ERCP differ from MRCP in the setting of obstruction?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>ERCP permits sphincterotomy, stone extraction or drainage; MRCP provides noninvasive imaging (p1345).</details>

**11. When is gallbladder drainage a reasonable alternative to immediate surgery?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In acute cholecystitis when severe illness makes operation unsafe and gallbladder decompression is needed (p1346).</details>

**12. How do pancreatic weight, composition and bicarbonate secretion change with age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fat and fibrosis increase, weight falls after the seventh decade, and bicarbonate output declines after the fourth decade (p1348).</details>

**13. Why is lipase valuable when acute pancreatitis presents late?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It remains elevated for longer than amylase. Both are typically at least three times normal during acute pancreatitis (p1348).</details>

**14. What diagnosis deserves particular attention in otherwise unexplained acute pancreatitis in an older person?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An underlying pancreatic malignancy, particularly in the absence of gallstones or a history of substantial alcohol exposure (p1348).</details>

**15. What treatment targets malabsorption in chronic pancreatitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Pancreatic enzyme replacement. Normal serum enzyme concentrations do not exclude chronic disease (p1349).</details>
