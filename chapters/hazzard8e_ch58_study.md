# Delirium

An acute change in attention or arousal warrants a search for a precipitant. Establish the baseline, follow the time course and address distress while treating the cause.

## Definition, vulnerability and mechanisms

Delirium involves widespread dysfunction of attention and arousal networks. EEG often shows diffuse slowing of the normal cortical background, including alpha activity. Focal epileptiform spikes or temporal sharp waves raise a different question. Structural abnormalities can coexist, and perfusion imaging has sometimes shown lower cerebral blood flow, but no single focal lesion or perfusion defect identifies delirium. EEG helps when nonconvulsive seizure is suspected and has limited accuracy as a stand-alone delirium test. May 2024 Q83 and September 2024 Q6 ask about the generalized background change (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p881">881</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>).

Acetylcholine supports attention. Increased dopaminergic activity, reduced cholinergic activity or a relative imbalance have been implicated. Serotonin may change in either direction. Sepsis and surgery may disrupt networks through inflammation, altered blood flow and the stress response. The network hypothesis begins on <a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>. The dopaminergic and cholinergic directions are spelled out on <a href="?chapter=58#p881">p881</a> (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p881">881</a>).

Predisposition describes the patient before the event. Dementia, depression, chronic disease burden, sensory or functional impairment and prior stroke or delirium raise vulnerability. Precipitants include an acute biochemical disturbance, infection, surgery, a new psychoactive drug, an indwelling catheter or restraint. Low serum albumin can be a physiologic precipitant. Restraints are a hospital precipitant. When vulnerability is high, a single sedative may be enough. In a more resilient patient several exposures may need to coincide (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p882">882</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#book-figure-58-1">Figure 58-1</a> and <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-1">Table 58-1</a>).

Delirium = acute disorder of attention + global cognition; affects **as many as half** of hospitalized adults 65+ (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>).

65+ patients account for **~40%** of all hospital days; costs **>$60,000/patient**, **$183B/year** nationally (2018 USD) (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>).

**Up to 50%** of cases are preventable (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>).

ICU delirium → **4×** in-hospital mortality, **6×** mortality at 6 months (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>).

Predisposing: dementia (**2–5×** risk, present in up to **two-thirds** of delirium cases), age **>70**, severe illness/multimorbidity, functional/sensory impairment, prior delirium or stroke (<a href="?chapter=58#p881">p881</a>).

Precipitating (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-1">Table 58-1</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>): polypharmacy, restraints, bladder catheter, infection, iatrogenic complications, surgery, urgent/trauma admission, coma, **ICU stay >10 days**, and physiologic derangements (elevated BUN/creatinine ratio, **elevated serum urea**, **abnormal serum albumin**, abnormal Na/glucose/K, metabolic acidosis).

Validated hospital precipitants (restraints, malnutrition, >3 medications added in the previous day, bladder catheter, any iatrogenic event): each independently confers **2–4×** risk; effects are cumulative (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>).

## Recognition and differential diagnosis

### Recognizing delirium at the bedside

Start by asking a relative or nurse when the patient last followed a conversation normally. Inattention may look like losing a thread halfway through a sentence, answering an earlier question or failing months backward. Acute change and fluctuation carry more diagnostic weight than a poor memory score alone. A patient may register three words yet struggled to sustain attention. A quiet older person who becomes slow and drowsy over hours deserves the same medical assessment as someone trying to climb out of bed. Hypoactive delirium is the more common motor presentation in older adults and is often overlooked as fatigue or low mood. Hyperactive and hypoactive features may alternate (<a href="?chapter=58#p883">p883</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>).

Cardinal features: acute onset **hours–days**, fluctuating course, inattention — vs dementia's insidious **weeks–months** course (<a href="?chapter=58#p883">p883</a>).

Hypoactive form is the more common form in older patients; it often goes unrecognized and carries an overall poorer prognosis (<a href="?chapter=58#p883">p883</a>).

CAM requires acute onset with a fluctuating course and impaired attention, accompanied by disorganized thinking or a change in level of consciousness. Normal arousal during one examination can coexist with a positive CAM. Orientation to place can also be preserved in a patient who repeatedly falls asleep or cannot sustain a simple task. During an acute admission, do not wait to identify one reversible cause before recognizing the syndrome and investigating it. Heart failure with new fluctuating drowsiness can fit delirium despite a normal CT (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p880">880</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p883">883</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>).

DSM-5: attention/awareness disturbance + acute/fluctuating onset + added cognitive deficit + not better explained by dementia and not in the context of severely reduced consciousness/coma + medical etiology (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p880">880</a>).

The short CAM applies its features after a cognitive assessment. UB-2 is an initial screen using the day of the week and months backward. One incorrect answer leads to a more complete assessment, such as the 3D-CAM. Disorientation without a baseline history leaves the onset uncertain. Ask whether this represents an acute or fluctuating change before completing CAM. Nursing observations can establish the missing time course (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p884">884</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-3">Table 58-3</a> and <a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>).

The 4AT assesses alertness, orientation, attention and acute change or fluctuation. Four or more points suggest possible delirium and warrant clinical evaluation. A recent significant change alone scores four, and seven correct months backward give zero on the attention item. In September 2024 Q89, good morning attention makes the short CAM negative on that examination even though an incorrect weekday makes UB-2 positive and the overnight change still counts on the 4AT. An acute change and inattention can accompany pneumonia. The anticipated result is a positive CAM with a 4AT of at least four (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p884">884</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-3">Table 58-3</a> and the 4AT user guide, items 3–4).

CAM sensitivity was **94–100%** and specificity **90–95%** (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>).

### Distinguishing delirium, dementia and psychosis

A patient who was conversing yesterday and is now distractible, sleepy and misidentifying people has an acute disorder even if dementia is already on the chart. Dementia usually develops gradually and attention and arousal are often relatively preserved until late disease. Acute psychosis can begin abruptly, with complex delusions and disorganized speech, yet consciousness usually stays normal. Visual hallucinations are characteristic of delirium, whereas hallucinations in acute psychosis are more often auditory. Visual hallucinations accompanied by evening worsening and sleepiness warrant evaluation. A changing level of consciousness helps distinguish delirium from primary psychosis. The comparison on the page is <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-4">Table 58-4</a> (<a href="?chapter=58#p883">p883</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p886">886</a>).

Depression can reduce concentration and slow speech while leaving arousal intact. Hallucinations are rare in depression, and memory is usually normal in primary acute psychosis. <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-4">Table 58-4</a> qualifies the statements about gradual onset, visual hallucinations, memory and consciousness in end-stage dementia (<a href="?chapter=58#p886">p886</a>).

Dementia increases susceptibility to a smaller acute insult, with a two- to fivefold higher risk of delirium across the studies cited. In the other direction, an episode of delirium is associated with later cognitive decline and incident dementia, even among people without a previous dementia diagnosis. This is a higher risk over follow-up, not an inevitable outcome for an individual. Cognitive or functional decline, new institutional care and excess mortality can extend far beyond discharge. Persistent symptoms deserve follow-up even after the immediate illness improves. Delirium can affect subsequent survival and cognition (<a href="?chapter=58#p879">p879</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p881">881</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p883">883</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p884">884</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>).

## Evaluation for precipitants

### Looking for a cause, including medicines and pain

Recognize the syndrome, assess safety and seek causes at the same time. Assess temperature, blood pressure, pulse, oxygenation, hydration and findings on chest, cardiac, abdominal and neurologic examination. Delirium may be the only obvious sign of infection, respiratory failure or myocardial infarction. Order tests guided by the history, including glucose, electrolytes, renal and liver function where indicated. New confusion in a patient with bone metastases can occur despite an unchanged fentanyl dose. Check calcium for a reversible cause before attributing the episode to effective analgesia or to dying (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p884">884</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>).

Screening tools (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-3">Table 58-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p884">p884</a>): **4AT** scores 0–12, **≥4** suggests delirium; **Nu-DESC** scores 0–10.

> **Current practice: Selecting a delirium screen.** when delirium indicators are present, a competent practitioner should assess with 4AT; use CAM-ICU or ICDSC in critical care or the recovery room after surgery; a healthcare professional with relevant expertise makes the final diagnosis if assessment indicates delirium - NICE CG103, recommendations updated 2023, 1.6.1-1.6.2, <a href="https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg103/chapter/Recommendations#assessment-and-diagnosis">p11</a>

CAM short form applied in over **10,000** studies, translated into over **19** languages (<a href="?chapter=58#p884">p884</a>).

As few as **20%** attain complete symptom resolution at 6 months; cognition can be affected for **up to 1 year** (<a href="?chapter=58#p884">p884</a>).

Lumbar puncture is appropriate in febrile delirium when meningitis or encephalitis is suspected. Fever by itself is common with infections outside the nervous system. New focal findings, head trauma or persistent unexplained delirium can warrant CT or MRI. Suspected nonconvulsive seizures direct the clinician toward EEG. Clinical suspicion of nervous-system infection is the reason to examine cerebrospinal fluid (<a href="?chapter=58#p885">p885</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#book-figure-58-2">Figure 58-2</a> on <a href="?chapter=58#p887">p887</a>).

LP/brain imaging/EEG probably indicated in fewer than **5–10%** of cases — LP for the febrile patient with suspected meningitis/encephalitis; imaging for new focal signs, head trauma, or no other identifiable cause (<a href="?chapter=58#p885">p885</a>). EEG false-negative **17%**, false-positive **22%** (<a href="?chapter=58#p885">p885</a>).

Reconstruct the actual medication exposure, including home drugs, recent changes, as-needed medicines and over-the-counter sleep or pain products. Diphenhydramine, promethazine, bladder antimuscarinics and some muscle relaxants add anticholinergic effects. The analgesic combination Muscol contains orphenadrine. Zolpidem can aggravate delirium even though it is not a benzodiazepine. If a benzodiazepine has been taken regularly, assess dependence and plan a taper when indicated. Abrupt withdrawal can itself cause delirium. Review the full medication list (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p883">883</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p889">889</a> and AGS Beers 2023, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">Tables 3 and 7</a>).

Medications contribute to **≥30%** of cases (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>). Any psychoactive drug **4×** risk; ≥2 psychoactive drugs **5×** risk; sedative-hypnotics **3–12×**, narcotics **3×**, anticholinergics **5–12×** (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>).

Beers-listed culprits (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-2">Table 58-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p883">p883</a>): anticholinergics, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, corticosteroids, H2-blockers, meperidine, Z-drugs (zolpidem/eszopiclone/zaleplon), TCAs. Psychoactive drugs should be discontinued or minimized whenever possible (<a href="?chapter=58#p885">p885</a>); Required reading: see [AGS Beers 2023 required-article card](?chapter=laws&lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023) for opioids and delirium, including the balanced pain-management caveat (<a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">Table 3</a>, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">p2066</a>)

A patient who cannot communicate reliably may show untreated pain by grimacing or resisting a procedure. Assess pain around dressings, transfers and mobilization, and reassess after treatment. Use regular analgesia for sustained pain plus rescue treatment for breakthrough pain. The AGS persistent-pain guideline describes this timing for continuous pain. Balance analgesia against opioid sedation, using validated pain assessment and multimodal treatment to minimize opioid exposure (<a href="?chapter=58#p882">p882</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p883">883</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p885">885</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>, AGS persistent-pain guideline recommendations IX and XII, and AGS Beers 2023, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">Table 3</a>, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">p2066</a>).

Differential: delirium vs dementia vs depression vs acute psychosis (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-4">Table 58-4</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p886">p886</a>) — level of consciousness (altered vs normal) separates delirium from acute psychosis; attention differs too (impaired vs variable) but overlaps and doesn't separate as cleanly.

## Prevention and ward care

The interventions are matched to the stress they remove. Provide written and verbal orientation, a familiar companion, glasses and working hearing aids. Cluster medically appropriate overnight tasks to allow sleep, maintain a quiet environment with low lighting at night and assist patients to move and care for themselves during the day. Assess hydration and nutrition, pain, and the continuing indication for each catheter, line or monitor. Cardiac monitoring after an infarct continues while clinically needed. Restraints may worsen agitation, immobility and injury (<a href="?chapter=58#p887">p887</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p889">889</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-5">Table 58-5</a>).

After hip fracture, early physiotherapy and assisted mobility counter a modifiable risk. The multicomponent hospital intervention reduced incident delirium by 40% relative to standard care in one trial. A different trial found a 40% reduction after proactive geriatric consultation in hip-fracture care. These are outcomes for programs that target several exposures. They do not isolate the effect of one exercise or guarantee prevention. Involve the family in environmental care while investigating the acute cause (<a href="?chapter=58#p886">p886</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p888">888</a>).

Targeted multicomponent prevention (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-5">Table 58-5</a> risk factors) vs standard care → **40%** risk reduction in hospitalized older patients (<a href="?chapter=58#p886">p886</a>).

**Pitfall.** Acute-onset timeframe: Cardinal Features/<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-4">Table 58-4</a> say "hours to days," but the Algorithm-for-Evaluation text says "hours to weeks" for acute change — the two time windows differ; a 2–3-week history falls inside one window and outside the other (<a href="?chapter=58#p886">p886</a>).

## Medicines and special circumstances

### When a drug has a defined purpose

Reserve short antipsychotic treatment for persistent agitation that threatens the patient or staff or interrupts essential treatment despite support. A person pulling out an endotracheal tube or repeatedly climbing out of bed with an unstable hip fracture is different from a patient who mumbles at night, has hypoactive delirium awaiting rehabilitation or resists painful burn dressings. Investigate the underlying condition and provide a sitter or other supervision. Titrate to an awake patient who accepts necessary care and taper as the threat resolves. Trials have not reliably reduced delirium duration, mortality or length of stay (<a href="?chapter=58#p887">p887</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p889">889</a>).

The chapter starts haloperidol at 0.25 mg and calls for reassessing vital signs before repeat dosing. IV use is confined to monitored settings because of torsades and sudden death. Extrapyramidal effects and dystonia matter, particularly with Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia. Clozapine can cause serious neutropenia. The official row cites delirium <a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>. Chapter 60 supplies the clozapine-specific information on <a href="?chapter=60#p928">p928</a>. Check the continuing need for any antipsychotic because these drugs carry increased mortality in dementia (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>, <a href="?chapter=60#p928">928</a>).

Nonpharm sleep protocol (warm milk/tea + relaxation music + back massage) cut sleep-med use **54% → 31%** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

**Haloperidol** — start **0.25 mg** PO/parenteral, may repeat q30min after rechecking vitals; most antipsychotic-naïve older patients should need a total loading dose of no more than **2.5 mg**; maintenance = half the loading dose in divided doses over the next 24 h, tapered over the ensuing **48 h**. Caution: EPS/dystonia; avoid in Parkinson disease/Lewy body dementia; IV only in monitored settings (torsades risk) (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

Official warnings: increased mortality with haloperidol and atypical antipsychotics in patients with dementia (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

Different indications call for different drugs. In suspected alcohol-withdrawal delirium, a benzodiazepine treats the withdrawal and seizure risk. With alcohol dependence and severe postoperative agitation, establish timing of the last drink and evaluate the intracranial injury as well. Benzodiazepines otherwise commonly worsen alertness and confusion. Cautiously titrated IV haloperidol or dexmedetomidine may be considered in an intubated ICU patient disrupting essential care. Studies of dexmedetomidine that shortened delirium or ICU stay were conducted in ventilated patients. Evidence for clonidine or melatonin agents does not support their routine use (<a href="?chapter=58#p887">p887</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p889">889</a>).

**Benzodiazepines**: avoid as first-line (oversedation, worsens delirium); remain treatment of choice for delirium from seizures or alcohol/medication withdrawal (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

**Dexmedetomidine**: shortens delirium duration/ICU stay in ventilated patients; useful if haloperidol-resistant (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

### Decisions, recovery and comfort at the end of life

A lucid interval can allow a patient to participate in a decision. When the decision can safely wait, return at the patient's best time, explain the specific choice, and assess understanding, appreciation and ability to express a preference. A Mini-Cog score or a diagnosis of delirium alone cannot determine capacity for an operation. Reassess capacity when surgery can wait several days (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>).

Discuss how much alertness and contact with family the patient wants to preserve and what investigation would be consistent with their goals. Even at the end of life, treating dehydration, hypoxia, hypoglycemia or medication effects may improve comfort. Begin symptom relief while evaluating. When severe agitation remains distressing despite environmental and initial drug measures, proportionate sedation may be appropriate. Short action and titration allow adjustment to relief, interaction and breathing. Midazolam is a short-acting example. Hazzard names lorazepam, initially 0.5–1.0 mg orally, IV or subcutaneously. Terminal agitation is a distinct indication (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>).

At end of life, delirium affects **>80%** of patients. Hazzard names **haloperidol as first-line therapy** for terminally ill patients with delirium and acknowledges a randomized trial that did not support its use. For **severe agitated delirium**, sedation can be **additional therapy** when consistent with goals of care. If sedation is indicated, the chapter recommends **lorazepam**, initially **0.5–1.0 mg** PO, IV or SQ (<a href="?chapter=58#p890">p890</a>).

Recovery planning matters in postacute care. In one cohort, 16% of admissions met full CAM criteria and another 50% had subsyndromal features. Among those admitted delirious, more than half were still delirious a month later. Persistent delirium tracked with incomplete functional recovery, readmission and mortality. Record baseline function, residual attention deficits and who can report change after discharge. Older people with COVID-19 may present with delirium even without the usual respiratory symptoms. Isolation and barriers to communication can compound immobility and disorientation (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p890">890</a>).

Nonpharmacologic strategies are first-line for every patient: reorientation, mobilize early, correct vision/hearing, avoid restraints, protect sleep, treat dehydration early (<a href="?chapter=58#book-table-58-5">Table 58-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=58#p888">p888</a>).

Antipsychotics = last resort, for safety/symptom control only:

**Quetiapine** — start **12.5 mg**, 24-h max **25 mg** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

**Olanzapine** — start **2.5 mg**, 24-h max **10 mg** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

**Risperidone** — start **0.25–0.5 mg**, 24-h max **1.5 mg** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

COVID-19: hospitalization delirium rates **25–84%** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

Postacute/nursing home: **16%** meet full CAM criteria on admission, another **50%** are subsyndromal; of those admitted with delirium, **>50%** still delirious at 1 month (<a href="?chapter=58#p890">p890</a>).

**Pitfall.** <a href="?chapter=58#book-figure-58-2">Figure 58-2</a>'s flowchart ends "avoid benzodiazepines and other psychoactive drugs" and gives only starting doses; the body text on <a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a> adds that benzodiazepines remain the treatment of choice for seizure/withdrawal delirium and gives the 24-h antipsychotic maximums the figure omits (<a href="?chapter=58#p887">p887</a>).

**Pitfall.** Risperidone dose: <a href="?chapter=58#book-figure-58-2">Figure 58-2</a> gives **0.25 mg**; the Antipsychotics text gives **0.25–0.5 mg** (<a href="?chapter=58#p889">p889</a>).

## Key numbers

- Clinicians fail to detect up to **70–85%** of cases (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>).

- ICU incidence **16–82%**; delirium occurs in up to **48%** in nursing home/postacute settings and up to **83%** at end of life (<a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>).
