# Chapter 56 Study: The Aging Brain

## Summary

**Normal aging, disease and reserve**
- Gray-matter atrophy accompanies healthy aging; it does not itself establish disease. Evaluate cognition and function to distinguish normal aging, MCI and dementia (p835-837).
- The book describes MCI conversion to dementia at **10–15% annually**; independence is generally retained at diagnosis (p836).
- Similar autopsy abnormalities can coexist with very different cognitive outcomes: resilience matters (p837).
- Aging alters synapses, dendrites, glia and vascular function. Most unbiased studies do not find substantial total neuron loss in memory-processing areas (p838-839).

**Cellular mechanisms**
- Long-term potentiation/depression, calcium handling and transmitter signaling change with age; many mechanistic findings come from rodents (p836-837, p841-842).
- Hyperphosphorylated tau forms tangles; amyloid oligomers may be toxic. Synaptic loss and tangles correlate with memory deficits better than plaque burden (p839-840).
- Mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced energy availability, oxidative stress and senescent microglia can increase neuronal vulnerability (p840-841).
- Cholinergic deficits are more severe in Alzheimer disease; dopaminergic changes may contribute to susceptibility to extrapyramidal drug effects (p842).
- Stress/glucocorticoids may increase hippocampal vulnerability. Clinical findings do not convincingly show estrogen treatment prevents Alzheimer disease or enhances cognition (p843).
- Neurotrophin/receptor balance and selective autophagy influence survival and protein clearance; translation requires disease-specific approaches (p843-845).

**Genes, prevention and measurement**
- IGF signaling, Klotho and apolipoprotein pathways link aging and cognition; the brain also regulates lifespan through neuroendocrine pathways in animal models (p845-847).
- Progeroid syndromes are diseases, not proven replicas of normal aging. Epigenetic and glymphatic mechanisms remain active research areas (p846-848).
- Exercise, cognitive activity, diet, vascular-risk correction and reduced environmental stress may protect brain health; animal dietary-restriction findings are not established human prescriptions (pp848–849). Table 56-4 attributes reduced brain-volume loss and stimulation of neurogenesis to physical exercise (p848).
- Whether physical and cognitive training complement each other is unclear, although some studies report complementary improvements (p849).
- Distinguish biomarkers of general aging from Alzheimer pathology; combinations of amyloid and tau measures may improve disease prediction, but clinical context remains essential (p849-850).

### From microscopic change to bedside counseling

- Long-term potentiation and long-term depression are activity-dependent changes in synaptic strength. Rodent studies link altered calcium handling in hippocampal cells to changes in this plasticity. A mechanistic result in aged animals may explain vulnerability without determining an individual's cognitive trajectory (p836–837, p841–842).
- Plaques, tangles, vascular lesions, Lewy bodies and hippocampal sclerosis may appear in people who maintained cognition as well as in those who developed impairment. A neuropathologic lesion, an imaging measure and loss of independence answer different clinical questions (p835–837).
- Table 56-4 attributes reduced brain-volume loss and stimulation of neurogenesis to physical exercise. It also links intellectual and outdoor activities to plasticity and brain capacity. The chapter discusses dietary patterns and attention to hypertension, diabetes and lipids among possible protective measures; it does not claim each intervention prevents dementia (p836, p848–849).
- Distinguish disposal of protein aggregates from their production. An autophagosome sequesters unwanted material and then fuses with a lysosome for degradation. Different cellular sites and substrates can require different responses; broadly stimulating a pathway need not give the same outcome in every disease (p844–845).

### Interpreting a complaint of memory change

Begin with a comparison to the person's earlier abilities. A slower response, a measurable deficit on testing and loss of independence are different observations. Ask which daily tasks have changed, whether reminders suffice and what an informant has observed. Normal aging can affect working memory, delayed recall and processing speed. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) denotes a measurable deficit greater than expected for age with independence generally maintained. Dementia requires interference with independent function. Standardized testing helps characterize the deficit, but a single score or complaint does not describe its trajectory or cause (p835–837).

Figure 56-1 shows illustrative trajectories for normal aging, MCI and Alzheimer disease. As more people survive to advanced age, more can reach the period when those trajectories diverge. The curves are conceptual: one cannot assign an individual to a curve or date a future transition from a single measurement. The chapter quotes annual progression from MCI to dementia of **10%–15%**. This is a group estimate. Follow cognition and function over time, including the possibility of stability, rather than telling a patient that a particular rate applies to their own next year (p835–837).

Resilience helps explain a finding that otherwise seems contradictory. People with similar degrees of plaque deposition, tangles, vascular change or other abnormalities may have had quite different cognitive function before death. Greater brain reserve or differences in how networks respond may contribute. Distinguish an associated lesion from one sufficient to explain disability. The reverse error is also possible: a person can have clinically important cognitive impairment even if a particular structural measure falls within the broad range seen in older adults (p836–838).

### What an older brain looks like, and what MRI cannot count

Figure 56-2 compares a healthy younger adult with a cognitively intact older adult and shows scatterplots across ages. The older example has wider sulci and ventricles from tissue volume loss despite a Mini-Mental State Examination score of **30**. Gray matter shows a generally linear decline in the plotted data, while the relation for white matter is nonlinear. The scatter within age groups matters: the line describes a trend and cannot establish the diagnosis in an individual. Do not infer the number of neurons or the cause of an individual's cognitive change directly from ventricular size (p837).

Figure 56-3 and Table 56-1 place structural and cellular changes in context. A smaller gray matter volume can reflect altered dendritic branches and spines, loss or remodeling of synapses, vascular changes and changes in glia and mitochondria. The table includes neuronal loss, but the discussion qualifies its extent: unbiased counts do not support extensive loss of neurons throughout memory-related regions in normal aging. Loss of inputs and of connections can impair a network even if many neuronal cell bodies remain. This distinction changes the question from “how many cells are left?” to “which connections can still transmit and adapt?” (p837–839).

Functional imaging examines relationships among regions, including highly connected hubs such as hippocampal and cingulate regions, the precuneus and frontal cortex. Older adults may recruit regions bilaterally for tasks for which younger adults show more lateralized activity. Greater activation is not simply a sign of superior performance; it may reflect an attempt to compensate for less efficient processing. Reduced cerebral blood flow and changes in vascular structure and reactivity can coexist with altered metabolism. Interpret functional activity, anatomy and clinical performance as related but distinct measurements (p838–839).

### How changes at a synapse can affect learning

Long-term potentiation strengthens a response after activity, and long-term depression weakens it. Both are forms of plasticity: learning requires the ability to adjust connections, not just to sustain strong signals everywhere. Calcium entering the postsynaptic cell activates signaling pathways, with the timing and amount of the calcium signal helping determine the direction of change. Changes in synaptic shape, receptor signaling and calcium regulation can thus affect memory formation without a dramatic loss of neuronal cell bodies (p836–839, p841–842).

A synapse also needs energy to reset ion gradients after signaling. Excitatory transmission imposes a cost even in a normal brain. Declining energy availability, oxidative damage and disease-associated proteins can make the same activity more stressful in an older neuron. Changes in aged rodents include prolonged calcium-mediated responses and a more pronounced after-hyperpolarization, which can make it harder to initiate subsequent firing. These experiments offer possible links between membrane physiology and less flexible learning. They do not provide a bedside calcium threshold, a blood test for synaptic function or an indication for a calcium-directed dementia treatment (p839, p841–842).

### Cytoskeleton, extracellular amyloid and inflammatory responses

The location of a protein matters. Actin contributes to small-scale structural changes including spine shape; microtubules support long-distance intracellular transport. Tau is normally associated with axonal microtubules. Abnormally phosphorylated tau can accumulate inside neurons as neurofibrillary pathology, while amyloid-beta is a principal component of extracellular plaques. These processes can coexist but are measured differently. A positive amyloid measurement cannot by itself specify how many neurons have lost functional connections, why a new symptom appeared this week or how much help a person needs at home (p838–840).

Amyloid-beta arises through processing of its precursor protein. Production, aggregation and clearance all influence what accumulates. Plaques increase with age in some people, and a small burden can be seen in people who maintained cognition. In the discussion, synapse loss and the number and distribution of tangles often track memory impairment more closely than plaque counts, although very high plaque burdens are rarely observed without impairment. The distinction allows a plaque-sensitive test to identify pathology even though the amount it detects does not closely map onto clinical severity in every person (p837, p839–840, p849–850).

Microglia contribute to synapse remodeling and surveillance; they do not simply mark the presence of a harmful inflammatory response. Aging and disease may alter how they respond to injury, cellular debris and abnormal proteins. Astrocytes also react and help regulate the extracellular environment. The chapter considers chronic inflammation and signaling between peripheral immunity and brain tissue as possible contributions to vulnerability. Observing activated glia near a lesion does not establish whether their overall effect at that stage is protective, harmful or both. That is one reason a general anti-inflammatory strategy cannot be read directly from a histological finding (p838, p840–841, p843).

### Energy metabolism and transmitter systems

Neurons depend on continuous energy supply. The chapter describes modest metabolic changes during typical aging and larger, regionally selective abnormalities in Alzheimer disease. Enzyme changes in Alzheimer brain tissue include the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and cytochrome c oxidase. Mitochondria must generate ATP, handle calcium and respond to oxidative stress while moving to where the cell needs energy. Altered morphology and accumulated damage can expose a loss of reserve under metabolic stress. A metabolic abnormality measured in disease tissue, however, should not be presented as an inevitable change of the same magnitude in every older adult (p840–842).

Cholinergic projections from the basal forebrain support learning and memory. Age can affect choline transport, acetylcholine synthesis and release, or coupling of the receptor to its intracellular signaling system. The deficits described in Alzheimer disease are greater and partly different in quality. Declines in striatal dopamine, transporter levels, receptor binding and downstream coupling may contribute to motor vulnerability and sensitivity to dopamine-blocking drugs. This distinction helps explain why a new medication can have a marked motor effect in an older patient without implying that expected aging is equivalent to Parkinson disease (p842).

Noradrenergic and serotonergic projections from brainstem nuclei influence many cortical and subcortical areas. The amino-acid transmitters glutamate and GABA supply excitatory and inhibitory signaling, respectively. Changes in release, reuptake, receptor function and the ability to buffer the consequences of excitation can disturb their balance. A change in an individual transmitter is only one part of a network response. Motor slowing, impaired attention and memory complaints do not identify the responsible transmitter without other clinical evidence (p842–843).

### Stress hormones, trophic signals and disposal of damaged material

Chronic stress and glucocorticoid exposure are considered possible influences on hippocampal vulnerability. Hormone effects depend on timing, dose and the state of the tissue. Experimental observations about estrogen and synapses have not translated into convincing clinical evidence that prescribing estrogen prevents Alzheimer disease or improves cognition in older women. Distinguish a hormone's role in normal physiology, an experimental response in tissue and the net benefit of treatment in a population with competing risks (p843).

Neurotrophins such as nerve growth factor and BDNF influence survival and plasticity. The same ligand can produce different effects depending on the receptors expressed and how they are combined. The chapter describes interactions between Trk receptors and p75 and distinguishes mature trophic factors from their precursors. A change in receptor balance can alter the direction of signaling even if the measured concentration of a growth factor appears similar. Protection of particular neurons in an experimental model therefore does not amount to a tested dose or a disease-modifying prescription for a patient (p843–844).

Autophagy must deliver the relevant intracellular material to a compartment where it can be degraded. The autophagosome encloses cargo and fuses with a lysosome; problems with selection, transport or degradation can impair clearance. A protein aggregate in one cellular location may not respond to the same intervention as a different aggregate elsewhere. Distinguish increased production of a protein from failed clearance, and increased numbers of visible autophagic structures from successful disposal. The chapter treats improved proteostasis as a research opportunity, with disease and substrate specificity essential to interpretation (p844–845).

### Reading the genetics and emerging mechanisms

Table 56-2 gives overall directions of age-associated pathway expression, not a panel of diagnostic tests. Fatty acid metabolism and transport, mitochondrial beta-oxidation, protein turnover, vesicular transport and synaptic plasticity generally trend down in the studies summarized. Stress-response and inflammatory pathways and microglial activation trend up. Glucose metabolism and hormonal regulation show mixed directions, with some components increasing and others decreasing. A relative increase in expression of a stress-response pathway can reflect a response to accumulating injury. It need not mean the older cell is better protected (p845).

Insulin/IGF signaling, Klotho and other longevity-related pathways connect nutrient sensing, growth and maintenance in experimental organisms. The chapter also asks whether the brain can regulate lifespan through hypothalamic and pituitary control of peripheral hormones. Altered growth hormone or IGF signaling changed survival in some mouse models. The result depends on which pathway, tissue and organism are studied; it is not an instruction to suppress or replace growth hormone in older adults. Human associations with APOE likewise modify probability: the E2 allele has been linked to longevity and lower Alzheimer incidence, while E4 is associated with higher risk. Neither is a clinical diagnosis (p845–847).

Table 56-3 illustrates why the term “accelerated aging” requires care. It lists segmental progeroid syndromes alongside their inheritance and the mean lifespans quoted in the book. Werner syndrome and ataxia-telangiectasia are listed as autosomal recessive, with approximate means of **45–50 years**; Hutchinson–Gilford progeria is listed as autosomal dominant with an approximate mean of **10–15 years**. Down syndrome is distinguished by trisomy 21. These are descriptions of specific conditions at the time of the text. An overlapping feature does not make any one of them a replica of all the processes operating in the normally aging brain (p846).

Epigenetic regulation changes expression without changing the inherited DNA sequence. The chapter considers DNA methylation, histone modification and small regulatory RNAs, noting inconsistent replication of some animal findings across laboratories, species and regions of the brain. The glymphatic hypothesis concerns movement of fluid and solutes through perivascular pathways and their possible contribution to removal of waste including amyloid. Much of the work reviewed was performed in rodents. Distinguish a plausible clearance mechanism, evidence that its efficiency changes in a particular model, and proof that changing it will prevent human dementia (p847–848).

### Counseling about physical and cognitive activity, food and vascular risk

The earlier exam question asks which recommendation fits Table 56-4. The book links **physical exercise** to improved cardiovascular function, reduced volume loss and stimulation of neurogenesis. It also links intellectual and outdoor activity to neurogenesis, plasticity and brain capacity. The correct response in the earlier paper concerned physical activity. The alternative that intellectual activity merely relieves loneliness misses the neural mechanisms considered in the book; the alternative focusing only on cardiovascular death misses the relevance of vascular risks to brain health (p848–849).

Table 56-4 discusses dietary patterns rich in fruit and vegetables and modest energy intake, attention to hypertension, lipids and diabetes, and minimizing environmental stress. Evidence ranges from population associations to controlled animal studies. Dietary restriction preserved selected structural and metabolic measures in some animal models, which does not establish a particular calorie prescription for an older person at risk of weight or muscle loss. Low folate, elevated homocysteine and vascular or cognitive outcomes can be linked epidemiologically while the benefit of giving a supplement to an unselected population remains a different question. Phytochemicals may act through adaptive responses as well as antioxidant chemistry. These mechanisms do not define a therapeutic supplement dose (p848–849).

Physical and cognitive training have both been associated with changes in connectivity. Proposed mechanisms for exercise include vascular adaptation, synaptic change and trophic signaling. The chapter describes some complementary effects but says it remains unclear to what extent one type of training adds to the other. Ask which activities the person can sustain and which health conditions limit participation. The relevant clinical decision is how to support feasible activity and address existing medical risks, without promising a particular gain in brain volume or prevention of dementia for that individual (p848–849).

### Biomarkers: specify the sample, molecule and endpoint

Table 56-5 separates the broad vulnerability associated with aging from Alzheimer-specific pathology. Routine blood measures can be valuable for identifying medical disease yet have low predictive power for general aging in an individual. In the book's comparison, amyloid PET and several CSF measures, including amyloid-beta 42, its ratio to amyloid-beta 40, total tau and phospho-tau, receive higher ratings for Alzheimer disease. CSF neurogranin and neurofilament light are ranked moderate. The table calls the predictive power of tau PET uncertain and ranks its listed blood measurements low, reflecting the evidence and assays considered at publication (p849–850).

A low CSF amyloid-beta 42 concentration and a positive amyloid scan can coexist because they measure soluble peptide and deposited plaque, respectively. The chapter reports greater concordance with amyloid PET for the CSF amyloid-beta 42/40 ratio and discusses sensitivity and specificity around **85%–90%** in the studies cited. Total tau can rise with neurodegeneration from more than one cause, while phosphorylated tau gives different information. In an individual with cognitive symptoms, these measures supplement clinical assessment. Distinguish using a test to support an etiological diagnosis from predicting disability, staging function or screening healthy people (p849–850).

> Current practice (not the book): The FDA cleared a specific plasma pTau217/β-amyloid 1-42 ratio test to aid assessment of amyloid pathology in symptomatic adults aged 55 or older. The result is interpreted with other clinical information; it is not an asymptomatic screening or stand-alone diagnostic test. - [FDA clearance, 2025](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-clears-first-blood-test-used-diagnosing-alzheimers-disease).

## Exam traps

- Ventricular enlargement with age-related atrophy describes volume change, without identifying a particular dementia or degree of impairment (p837).
- Increased bilateral activation during a task may represent compensation and is not synonymous with improved task performance (p838).
- The presence of extracellular amyloid and the severity of intracellular tau or synaptic change are related but distinct observations (p839–840).
- Lower CSF amyloid-beta 42 and a higher plaque burden on imaging can occur together; the two measures sample different compartments (p850).
- A longevity result in a mouse model or a diet association in a cohort does not establish a hormone, nutrient or drug dose for preventing human dementia (p845–849).
- Genes and biomarker ratios change the interpretation of risk or pathology. Neither replaces the assessment of everyday independence (p835–837, p846, p850).


- Gray-matter loss alone does not distinguish normal aging from mild cognitive impairment or dementia (p835–837).
- Synaptic loss is possible without a substantial loss of total neurons in memory-related regions (p838–839).
- Animal results on caloric restriction, neurogenesis or hormonal signaling cannot establish a human prevention prescription (p843, p848–849).

**3. Why can similar autopsy findings accompany different cognitive trajectories?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Resilience varies. Plaques, tangles, Lewy bodies and vascular abnormalities can be present even with preserved cognition (p837).</details>

**4. What changes can occur even when memory-processing regions retain most of their neurons?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Synaptic loss, altered dendrites, glial changes and impaired circuitry can affect function (p838–839).</details>

**5. What is synaptic plasticity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The nervous system can reorganize pathways and alter synaptic strength, including long-term potentiation and depression (p836–837).</details>

**6. What links calcium handling to plasticity in aged rodents?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Age-related changes in calcium channels and cellular buffering can alter the signals that change synaptic strength (p841–842).</details>

**7. Which protein forms neurofibrillary tangles?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Abnormally phosphorylated tau. Tangles and synaptic loss correlate with memory impairment more closely than plaque burden (p839–840).</details>

**8. Why do soluble amyloid species matter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Oligomers may adversely affect neurons even when the quantity of deposited plaques poorly explains cognitive symptoms (p839–840).</details>

**9. What cellular energy problem can increase vulnerability with age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Mitochondrial dysfunction can reduce energy availability and increase oxidative stress (p840–841).</details>

**10. Which transmitter changes have implications for cholinergic and extrapyramidal symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cholinergic deficits are particularly prominent in Alzheimer disease, while dopamine changes may increase susceptibility to extrapyramidal effects of drugs (p842).</details>

**11. What does the chapter conclude about estrogen for cognitive prevention?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The clinical evidence does not convincingly show that treatment prevents Alzheimer disease or improves cognition (p843).</details>

**12. How does autophagy dispose of damaged organelles or aggregates?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The material is sequestered within an autophagosome, which fuses with a lysosome for degradation (p844).</details>

**13. Which exercise effects did the older exam ask about?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter associates exercise with reduced brain-volume loss and stimulation of neurogenesis, as well as cardiovascular benefit (p848).</details>

**14. What role does the chapter give intellectual and outdoor activity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Potential stimulation of neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. Cognitive activity has a broader rationale than relief of isolation alone (p848–849).</details>

**15. How should Alzheimer biomarkers be interpreted in an older adult?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Amyloid and tau combinations may improve prediction, but interpret them with cognitive performance, function and clinical context (p849–850).</details>
