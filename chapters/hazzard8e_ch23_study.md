# Chapter 23 Study: Substance Use and Disorders

## Summary

**Identify unhealthy use**
- Unhealthy use includes risk of harm or established health/social consequences; addiction is a health condition, not a moral failure (p317, p321).
- Aging, multimorbidity and interacting medicines increase harm, sometimes at lower exposure than in younger adults (p320–321).
- A U.S. standard drink contains **14 g** ethanol. The chapter cites daily limits of two drinks for men and one for women on drinking days; contraindicating conditions/medications may require abstinence (p317).
- Reduced tolerance and changed social roles can obscure substance-use diagnostic criteria in older adults (p322).
- Benzodiazepines are disproportionately prescribed to older adults despite contraindications: estimated prevalence of use **13%**, with up to **a third** using them long term. Female gender is consistently associated with misuse of prescription benzodiazepines (p319).
- Prescription misuse in older adults is shaped by overprescription, misdiagnosis (misuse mistaken for depression or dementia), accidentally taking more than intended, mixing up medications and potentially inappropriate prescribing, rather than intentional misuse. Past-year misuse of prescription psychotherapeutics was **2.1%** in 2019, opioids most often (**1.6%**); older adults who misuse rely much more on physician sources (p319).
- Tobacco is the second most commonly used substance; past-year use among adults 65 and older has stayed about **13%** (2019). Past-year cannabis use rose from **0.3%** in 2007 to **5%** in 2019 (p319).

**Screen & assess**
- Ask nonjudgmentally about alcohol, prescriptions, nonprescription drugs, cannabis and other substances; recommended screening includes older adults (p321–322).
- Use AUDIT/AUDIT-C or geriatric tools such as MAST-G and CARET; consider comorbidity and drug interactions, not consumption alone (p322–323).
- Positive screens require assessment of use pattern, consequences, severity, supports and treatment needs (p323).

**Treatment**
- Brief interventions, motivational approaches and psychosocial treatment can help; tailor goals and supports to the person (p323–324).
- Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal may need closely monitored specialty care (p324).
- Naltrexone: oral daily or monthly injection; contraindicated with prescribed opioids, acute hepatitis or liver failure (p324).
- Acamprosate: oral three times daily; reduce for renal insufficiency. Evidence is strongest after initial abstinence in younger-adult trials (p324).
- Disulfiram: generally not recommended in older adults because the alcohol reaction can be harmful; if used, great caution is required (p324).
- Opioid-use disorder has medication options including methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone; combine medication access with supportive care (p324–326).
- The chapter has limited older-adult trial evidence for many medications; use its source dose table and individual precautions (p324–325).
- Continue follow-up and reassessment; recurrence can occur in the course of chronic substance-use disorders (p321).

### Recognizing a hazardous prescription

June 2026 Q10 asks which medicine to review first in an alert older adult taking venlafaxine, mirtazapine, quetiapine and regular clonazepam. The keyed priority is clonazepam. Normal orientation on one examination does not remove the risks of continued benzodiazepine exposure. Ask why it was started, whether it still helps and how it is actually taken. Clarify falls, confusion and other sedative use. Taking prescribed medication can lead to dependence without showing that the patient has a substance use disorder (p318–321).

> Current practice (not the book): When regular benzodiazepine use makes physical dependence likely, plan a supervised taper adapted to symptoms and the original indication. Abrupt discontinuation can cause serious withdrawal. - [Joint Clinical Practice Guideline on Benzodiazepine Tapering, 2025](https://www.asam.org/quality-care/clinical-guidelines/benzodiazepine-tapering).

> Current practice (not the book): US dietary guidance now advises drinking less, without sex-specific daily targets. It advises avoiding alcohol with interacting medicines or conditions and during recovery from alcohol use disorder. - [US Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2026, p5](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA_508.pdf).

### Matching a medicine to an alcohol-treatment goal

Table 23-4 lists naltrexone **50–100 mg orally daily** or **380 mg intramuscularly monthly**. Opioid blockade can precipitate withdrawal and prevent needed opioid analgesia. Avoid it with current opioid use, acute hepatitis or liver failure. Acamprosate **666 mg three times daily** supports abstinence and requires renal assessment and a workable dosing schedule. The chapter's table gives a reduced regimen in moderate renal impairment and advises avoidance below **30 mL/min** (p325).

> Current practice (not the book): US acamprosate labeling includes a creatinine clearance of exactly 30 mL/min in the contraindicated range. For clearance above 30 through 50 mL/min, the reduced dose is 333 mg three times daily. - [Acamprosate prescribing information, 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=91769a96-3182-4e38-90c3-b22c1beae398).

### A history that distinguishes exposure, harm and disorder

Ask what the person uses, how often, how much, by what route and for what reason. Include prescribed sedatives and analgesics, alcohol, cannabis and tobacco. A patient who says they do not use “drugs” may nevertheless take a sleeping tablet every night, drink alcohol to get back to sleep or use cannabis for chronic pain. Asking specifically about the reason often reveals a treatable symptom. Record a typical day and episodes of heavier use rather than treating a weekly average as a complete description. Check whether the product, amount or timing changed after bereavement, retirement, illness or a change of residence (p317–321).

Separate unhealthy use from a substance use disorder. A smaller amount than the patient previously tolerated can become hazardous as balance, kidney or liver function, comorbidity and prescribed medicines change. That risk warrants intervention even if criteria for a disorder are absent. Conversely, a person may continue a pattern of impaired control and recurrent harm when apparently sober in clinic. A clinic visit that documents good orientation need not reproduce the effects of medicines combined with an evening drink at home. Aim to understand what happens between visits and which consequences affect independence (p317–322).

Table 23-1 applies DSM-5 criteria to the realities of later life. At least **two criteria within 12 months** define a disorder; the chapter grades **2–3 as mild, 4–5 as moderate and 6 or more as severe**. The criteria cover impaired control, craving, time spent obtaining or recovering, role or relationship problems, activities given up, hazardous use, continued use despite harm, tolerance and withdrawal. Check concrete examples instead of counting a vague complaint in several domains. Taking longer to recover from a sedative can be pharmacological vulnerability, and withdrawal after appropriate prescribed use can reflect physical dependence without independently establishing a disorder (p318).

Retirement removes workplace clues, while family or caregiving obligations may remain important. Someone may stop going out because of arthritis, substance use, sedation or several causes acting together. Determine the sequence rather than assuming social withdrawal establishes addiction. Increased sensitivity can mean less consumption is now needed to produce the same impairment, obscuring the classic history of escalating tolerance. Withdrawal can be subtle or prolonged, and an older person may have trouble linking falls, missed medicines or memory lapses to use. Ask collateral sources with the person's involvement when safe and appropriate, especially if recall or medication self-management is in doubt (p318–322).

### Who is represented by prevalence estimates?

Alcohol is the most commonly used substance in the US data summarized here. The reported prevalence of past-year drinking in adults aged **65 or older** rose from **52% in 2010 to 56% in 2019**. This is a population trend and does not specify harmful use in an individual. Past-year use, recent binge drinking, nonmedical use of a prescription and a diagnosable disorder are different survey measures. Note the denominator, interval and substance before comparing percentages. The chapter's estimates for benzodiazepine use describe exposure; the estimate for misuse of prescription psychotherapeutics describes a narrower and differently defined outcome (p318–319).

Patient selection can make drinking appear protective in observational work. People who become ill often reduce or stop drinking, while those healthy enough to continue may have fewer comorbidities. Comparing current moderate drinkers with a group that includes these “sick quitters” does not isolate a physiological benefit of alcohol. Ask about prior use when a new patient reports abstinence, both to understand present risk and to avoid drawing a causal conclusion from cross-sectional comparisons. Similarly, higher rates of prescription misuse can reflect more frequent medical exposure or access to multiple prescribers as well as patient behavior (p319–320).

Table 23-2 identifies factors to explore, including insomnia, chronic pain, loneliness, depression or other psychiatric illness, social networks, retirement, bereavement and changes in living arrangements. These are prompts for history, not demographic diagnoses. The older adult who has recently lost a spouse may have begun using a substance to cope, or may already have stopped because of poor health. If a pain medicine is being taken more often than prescribed, ask about both unrelieved pain and impaired control. A change in instructions, duplicate supplies or confusion about a schedule can also explain excessive exposure (p319–321).

### Why the same intake can have a different effect now

Age-related changes in body composition can increase alcohol exposure for a given intake. A lipophilic sedative can have a longer duration of action, particularly if it or its metabolites are long acting. Central nervous system sensitivity, sensory impairment and poorer balance can compound a pharmacokinetic change. Thus a dose taken uneventfully for years can now contribute to a fall, confusion or a driving problem. Add other medicines and substances to the timeline. Alcohol plus a benzodiazepine can increase injury and cognitive risk, while opioids and sedatives can combine to suppress respiration, especially in a person with sleep apnea or pulmonary disease (p320–321, p325–326).

The June 2026 question is a medication-prioritization problem. Venlafaxine, mirtazapine and quetiapine also warrant individual review, but the listed regularly administered benzodiazepine, clonazepam, is the keyed priority for reduction or cessation. Before choosing a plan, establish the present indication, actual schedule, duration, previous attempts to reduce it and use of other sedatives. A benzodiazepine taken several times daily cannot safely be treated as an occasional unused medicine when discussing withdrawal. Avoid assigning an addiction diagnosis merely because physical dependence is possible. Coordinate reduction with treatment of the underlying anxiety, sleep problem or other indication (p318–321, p324; see also Chapter 11, p169).

A medication reconciliation should include where each prescription came from, whether another clinician recently changed it and how the patient measures the dose. Table 23-3 specifically includes antihypertensives, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, CNS depressants, NSAIDs and serotonin reuptake inhibitors among drugs to consider when evaluating substance-related risk. Those classes do not all interact by the same mechanism. For example, worsening balance or hypotension makes a fall more likely, while anticoagulation changes its potential consequences. Ask about the combined effect on the patient's day rather than looking only for a drug interaction that would appear as an abnormal routine laboratory result (p320–321).

For cannabis, ask about purpose and concurrent use rather than assuming that a person who mentions a medical indication has discussed it with every prescriber. The chapter notes rising co-use among older Americans who drank alcohol: in the surveys cited, **2.9% in 2015** and **6.3% in 2018** reported cannabis use in the group aged 65 or older using alcohol. The denominator is people who drank, not all older adults. Data on harms for many substances in older age are less complete than for alcohol or sedating drugs. Discuss the observed difficulty, timing and interacting medicines while acknowledging when evidence comes from younger populations (p319–321).

### Choosing a screen for the clinical question

A useful screen asks something the patient can answer accurately, and its interpretation depends on the population and time frame. Normalize the discussion by asking about substance use alongside other health behaviors. Explain that the question concerns interactions, falls or ability to sleep as well as addiction. Use nonjudgmental wording and sufficient privacy, and determine whether hearing, literacy or cognitive barriers make a written instrument unreliable. A positive screen opens a fuller assessment. A negative screen may miss interaction-related harm if the threshold is geared to younger adults with fewer medical risks (p321–323).

The full AUDIT includes **10 items**, exploring consumption, drinking behavior and alcohol-related problems over the previous year, with a score from **0 to 40**. The chapter gives **8** as a conventional positive threshold for possible AUD and notes evidence for a lower cutoff of **5** to identify potentially unhealthy use in older adults. AUDIT-C uses the consumption questions; the chapter gives **3 or more** as a prompt for assessment. A short instrument helps when visit time is limited but says little about a sleeping medicine, comorbid liver disease or a recent fall. Quantify actual use before deciding on a subsequent intervention (p322).

MAST-G was developed for older adults. At least **5 positive responses among 24 yes/no questions** suggest a problem. Its short form uses **10 items**, with **2 positive responses** warranting assessment. The chapter reports sensitivity **95%** and specificity **78%** for the longer instrument in the work. CAGE addresses a different question: it can identify lifetime problems but performs poorly for binge use and cannot reliably distinguish remote from current difficulties. The chapter suggests a cutoff of **1** for older adults and women. A remote positive answer may be clinically useful history without indicating that the person is drinking now (p322–323).

CARET adds health conditions, interacting medicines and behaviors such as drinking and driving to the quantity consumed. It can uncover risk in a person who would fall below a simple quantity threshold. Broader tools such as ASSIST include tobacco, alcohol and other drugs, and a prescription-specific history can identify a behavior that an alcohol-only tool misses. Select a screen to fit the suspected exposure, and ask about the patient's own view of the benefits and harms. Someone seeking pain relief or sleep may need an alternative treatment alongside a plan to reduce risky use (p322–323).

### Assessment and the intensity of intervention

After a positive result, identify the pattern, criteria met, withdrawal risk, psychiatric or medical comorbidity and prior treatment. Establish immediate hazards first, such as intoxication, interactions, unsafe driving or a planned sudden cessation after longstanding heavy use. Ask about motivation, access to help, housing and ability to attend appointments. Brief advice can be sufficient when the person wants information about lower-risk behavior. A patient with multiple previous failed attempts, substantial withdrawal risk or continuing serious harm may need specialty care. Do not postpone care for depression, chronic pain or insomnia until the substance problem is resolved (p323–324).

Motivational interviewing elicits the person's own reasons for change. A patient may value an evening drink and also worry about losing the ability to drive or live independently after another fall. Examine both sides and agree on one achievable change that can be assessed at follow-up. Cognitive behavioral approaches can identify situations associated with use, anticipate problems and practice alternative responses. Slow the pace, repeat the plan and encourage notes where helpful. Problem-solving therapy makes the next action concrete by selecting a feasible response to a named problem and checking whether it worked (p323–324).

Referral is a clinical handoff. Determine whether the program can accommodate sensory loss, mobility limitations, transportation, medical treatment and caregiver needs. The primary team should know who will manage withdrawal, prescribe ongoing medicines and reassess mood and function. Alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal in older adults can require inpatient or closely monitored specialty care. Brief counseling, peer support or a medication to prevent recurrence is not itself management of acute withdrawal. If a patient declines referral, maintain contact; reassessment may reveal a new reason or opportunity to engage (p323–324, p326).

### Matching alcohol-use treatment to comorbidity and adherence

Naltrexone blocks opioid receptors and can reduce alcohol-related reward or craving. It has been tested in short-term trials in older adults, while geriatric evidence for all three medications remains limited. The monthly injectable formulation removes the need to remember a daily tablet but does not eliminate the need to check for opioid exposure or liver disease. Review prescribed and other opioids, including those required for pain. Starting an antagonist can precipitate withdrawal. Assess hepatic disease and tolerability, discuss nausea or injection-site reactions, and ensure that the patient understands the implications for future analgesia (p324–325).

Acamprosate supports abstinence after withdrawal and is not a rescue treatment for intoxication or acute withdrawal. It can be useful when liver disease or concurrent opioid analgesia makes naltrexone a poor choice, but renal clearance and the three-times-daily schedule are decisive. Ask the patient how doses will fit meals and routine. Disulfiram **250 mg daily**, with **125–500 mg** given as the table's range, works by creating an aversive reaction if alcohol is consumed. The chapter generally discourages its use in older adults because cardiovascular or hepatic disease and drug interactions can make that reaction especially dangerous. It assumes informed, motivated participation; never conceal it in the patient's medication or food (p324–325).

### Opioid use, overdose prevention and smoking

Opioid-use disorder treatment includes agonist, partial agonist and antagonist approaches. Methadone can suppress withdrawal and craving and is associated with lower all-cause mortality in people with OUD. Its long action, potential for QT prolongation, interacting drugs and risk of respiratory depression require careful supervision. Buprenorphine's partial agonism limits respiratory depression relative to full agonists, but combined sedative exposure can still cause overdose. Compare the patient's competing needs, prior response, ability to reach treatment and prescribed opioid use for pain. A pharmacological advantage does not establish that a particular preparation will be acceptable or accessible (p325–326).

Table 23-4 gives an example sublingual buprenorphine/naloxone induction starting with **4/1 mg**, with **8/2 mg total on the first day**, and typical maintenance **16–24 mg/day** of buprenorphine. This is a book regimen under supervision; the timing and method of induction depend on current opioid exposure and withdrawal. Naloxone in the combination discourages injection; it does not eliminate the risks of taking other sedatives. Extended-release naltrexone **380 mg IM monthly** is an antagonist option after an adequate opioid-free period. Starting it while the patient is physiologically dependent can precipitate severe withdrawal. The book's table describes an American waiver for prescribing buprenorphine that is no longer in force (p325–326).

> Current practice (not the book): The United States eliminated the federal DATA/X-waiver requirement for buprenorphine prescribing for opioid-use disorder. Prescribers still need the appropriate DEA registration and must follow applicable state law. - [SAMHSA, MAT Act waiver elimination, 2023](https://www.samhsa.gov/substance-use/treatment/resources/mat-act).

Overdose prevention applies to prescribed exposure as well as diagnosed OUD. The book identifies concurrent benzodiazepines or an opioid dose of **50 morphine milligram equivalents/day or more** as examples of circumstances warranting take-home intranasal naloxone and education. COPD, obstructive sleep apnea and other sedating substances can increase risk. Train family members or caregivers who may actually be present during an overdose. Naloxone supplies a way to respond in an emergency; it does not replace the work of reassessing sedative combinations, providing effective OUD treatment or keeping follow-up accessible (p326).

Tobacco treatment also benefits from an active offer of medication plus support. The chapter discusses varenicline and nicotine replacement, including combining a long-acting form with a short-acting product for breakthrough craving. Its comparisons of efficacy come largely from adult data and should not be represented as trials confined to an older frail population. At follow-up, ask what happened during the most recent attempt, whether the medicine was tolerated and which situations led to renewed use. A return to smoking, drinking or another substance is an opportunity to adjust treatment and reengage the patient rather than discharge them from care (p326).

## Exam traps

- A positive screen prompts assessment; it does not diagnose addiction or specify withdrawal risk (p322–324).
- Prescription-related harm can arise from overprescribing or administration errors even when there is no intentional misuse (p319).
- An antagonist such as naltrexone can precipitate withdrawal in a person using opioids (p324–325).
