# Chapter 57 Study: Cognitive Changes in Normal and Pathologic Aging

## Normal aging — what declines vs what's preserved

<span data-exam-page="854"></span>

- **Crystallized (verbal) intelligence** stays relatively intact with age; **fluid intelligence** (flexible reasoning/problem-solving) declines; reduced speed of information processing has been theorized to account for many of these changes (p853)
- **Sustained attention (vigilance)** does not change significantly with age; **divided attention** may decline, results mixed (p854)
- **"Real-world" executive functions** (planning, executing multi-step tasks) are little affected by normal aging even though performance on *novel* executive tasks (Wisconsin Card Sort, Stroop, Go-No-Go) declines slightly (p854)
- **Remote memory, procedural memory, semantic memory** stay intact; **learning/recall of new information** is what declines — slower learning curve, lower total learned (p854-855)
- Language: comprehension, vocabulary, and syntax are preserved; **spontaneous word finding** ("tip-of-the-tongue") and **semantic fluency** ("name animals") decline, while **phonemic fluency** ("words starting with F") stays stable (p856)
- Visuospatial: speed slows, but mental rotation and complex copy tasks can still worsen even when speed is controlled for (p856)

## Common medical conditions and cognition (Table 57-2, Figure 57-1)

- Prevalence of medical conditions associated with cognitive impairment among US adults aged 65+ (**Figure 57-1**, p856): **hypertension 70%**, **chronic kidney disease 38%**, **diabetes 27%**, **thyroid disease 25%**, **coronary artery disease 17%**, **sleep apnea 14%**, **depression 10-15%**, **COPD 10%**
- **Postoperative cognitive decline (POCD)** after cardiac surgery: seen in **30-70%** of patients at hospital discharge; still present in **15-25%** at 12-month follow-up (p859)
- **T2DM** affects **20-30%** of US adults >65; complex attention and verbal memory encoding are the main deficits (p859-860)
- **CKD** cognitive impairment prevalence **10-40%**, depending on disease severity/duration (p860)
- High-dose **vitamin E (2000 IU/day)** reduced *functional* decline in mild-to-moderate AD in a large trial, though evidence for prevention is weak overall (p860-861)

> Current practice (not the book): Evidence for vitamin E prevention is uncertain. NICE advises against offering vitamin E supplements to treat dementia. Discuss bleeding risk and other medicines before a patient takes high doses. [NICE NG97, 2018](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations).
- Depression prevalence in older adults: **11-30%** (p861)
- Table 57-2's per-condition grid flags **AD, VaD, and PD** risk specifically for coronary artery disease, T2DM, and CKD; hypertension, cardiac surgery, COPD, and sleep apnea are listed for **AD and VaD only** (no PD); nutritional deficiency for **AD and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome**; thyroid disease for **AD** alone (Table 57-2, p857-858)
- Per the table, several of these cognitive effects can improve with treatment (only limited evidence for cardiac rehab; improvement often during year 1 post-surgery; antihypertensives/antidiabetics, dialysis/transplant, O2 therapy, CPAP, thyroid replacement) — but **B12/vitamin D deficits may persist** despite supplementation, and some thyroid patients **don't return to cognitive baseline** (Table 57-2, p857-858)

## Differentiating dementia types (Table 57-3, p863-866)

<span data-exam-page="864"></span>

<span data-exam-page="865"></span>

- Of the major dementia types compared, **AD and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome are the only ones with impaired recognition** (not just recall) on memory testing — the table marks both "Recognition intact?" **N**; PD/PDD, DLB, and VaD are marked variable; DLB is distinguished by **significant fluctuations in attention** plus early hallucinations/delusions; vascular dementia: onset **insidious or acute**, progression **stepwise or gradual**, with executive deficits significantly more impaired than in AD and relative to verbal memory performance
- **Behavioral variant FTLD**: early disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy/sympathy, hyperorality, with recent memory relatively preserved; **PSP**: vertical gaze palsy + postural instability; **CBS**: asymmetric motor signs + alien limb; **NPH**: wide-based gait, urinary incontinence, confusional state
- Table 57-3, bvFTD row: insidious onset, steady and rapid progression, recent memory relatively preserved, executive functions impaired across a range, verbal fluency impaired, significant behavioral change (p864)
- Table 57-3, prion row: insidious onset, steady and rapid progression, nonspecific memory impairment, reduced problem-solving, language generally preserved, visuospatial function generally intact; psychomotor: may exhibit impaired reflexes and coordination; behavior: apathy, emotional lability, impaired sleep, appetite loss (p865)

## MCI due to AD

- NIA/AA criteria: (1) cognitive concern, (2) objective impairment in ≥1 domain, (3) preserved functional independence, (4) no dementia (p868-869)
- In the longitudinal studies summarized by the book, up to **80%** of those diagnosed with MCI progressed to AD within **5-8 years**; conversion rate **~10-15%/year** vs **1-2%/year** in the general population (p869)
- **15-40%** of MCI patients later "revert" to normal on testing — more likely if community-based (not clinic-based), biomarker-negative, younger, fewer comorbidities, more education (p869)

## AD dementia

- Underlying AD neuropathology (β-amyloid, neurofibrillary tangles) typically begins **~20 years or more** before clinical symptom onset (p862)
- NIA/AA dementia criteria require impairment interfering with daily function across **≥2 cognitive domains** (p862)
- Memory (anterograde amnesia/rapid forgetting) is the earliest and most common first symptom; semantic ("category") fluency is impaired disproportionately to phonemic fluency (p867-868)

## FTLD

- Onset typically **late 50s to early 60s** — the **2nd most common** dementia in patients under 65 (p873)
- **30-40%** of FTLD cases are linked to genetic mutations, including GRN and MAPT; C9orf72 expansion is another cause (Ch 63, p990) (p873)
- **Behavioral variant FTLD (bvFTD)** accounts for **50-60%** of all FTLD diagnoses (p873)
- Up to **75%** of pathologically confirmed FTLD patients also meet clinical criteria for probable AD (p873)

<span data-exam-page="873"></span>

- FTLD pathology includes intracellular inclusions of tau or TDP-43. Memory is relatively spared compared with executive and language function, especially with cues; apraxia is more common than in AD (p873)
- bvFTD presents with a prominent decline in social cognition and behavior and/or executive dysfunction, and at least three of: disinhibition, apathy, diminished empathy/sympathy, perseverative/stereotyped/compulsive behavior, hyperorality/dietary change. "Probable" bvFTD needs disproportionate frontal or temporal involvement on imaging, or a pathogenic mutation. The MMSE is not very helpful early: many score within normal limits (p873)
- PPA usually begins in the fifth or sixth decade and needs prominent early language dysfunction with other domains relatively spared. Although not consistent across all cases: agrammatic PPA is often associated with tau, logopenic PPA with AD pathology, semantic PPA with TDP-43 (p873)

<span data-exam-page="874"></span>

- Semantic dementia may show prominent visual agnosia and inaccurate object use; in contrast to PNFA, speech production and repetition remain intact early (p874)
- Logopenic aphasia: impaired word retrieval in spontaneous speech, naming difficulty and impaired sentence repetition, sometimes impaired sentence comprehension. The hypothesized mechanism is a short-term working memory deficit, so single-word repetition is well preserved. Naming is less impaired than in SD, object knowledge is preserved, and speech is usually grammatical without motor speech abnormalities (p874)
- FTD with motor neuron disease: tends to progress rapidly, with death typically **3 to 5 years** after symptom onset; onset age more variable, from **35 to 75** on average. Cognitive and psychiatric symptoms plus upper and/or lower motor neuron signs; early bulbar involvement can cause dysphagia and pseudobulbar affect (p874)
- PSP is frequently misdiagnosed as PD; vertical supranuclear gaze palsy is a core feature but may not appear early. Postural instability with falls shortly after onset; early mental and psychomotor slowing and executive dysfunction; memory impairment is less severe than in AD (p874)
- Corticobasal syndrome is the primary clinical phenotype of corticobasal degeneration: abnormal tau, relatively focal and asymmetric frontal and parietal cortical atrophy. Mean onset in the **early 60s**. Progressive, asymmetric tremor, loss of coordination, rigidity and myoclonus, with a higher prevalence of alien limb syndrome; unilateral apraxia (most commonly ideomotor) is common. Language changes such as slowed verbal fluency and executive dysfunction may occur. In contrast to AD, memory is typically well preserved early (p874)
- Palliative care in FTLD: early family and caregiver education and support; safety measures for impulsive driving, money and scams; early psychiatry (some evidence that SSRIs may help behavioral symptoms); early speech-language pathology for primary or secondary language dysfunction (PSP, CBS, FTD-MND); physical therapy for early motor dysfunction (p874)

## PD / PDD / DLB

<span data-exam-page="870"></span>

- Upwards of **80%** of PD patients who live **≥20 years** are expected to develop PDD (p870)
- **PD-MCI** prevalence **~30-40%**; **10-30%** of newly diagnosed PD patients already show cognitive impairment (p870)
- DLB clue: visual hallucinations with **MMSE >20** are highly suggestive of DLB rather than AD (p871)
- PD→PDD requires dementia onset **>1 year after** established motor symptoms; if dementia precedes or comes within 1 year of motor onset, it's classified **DLB** (p870)

## Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI)

- Co-occurrence of AD + VaD pathology: **20-40%** of dementia patients (p872)

## Other reversible/less common dementias

<span data-exam-page="876"></span>

- Sporadic prion disease (CJD): typically diagnosed **60s**, age range **40-80** (p876)
- Prion disease: early cognitive signs are vague (poor memory, concentration, problem solving) with psychiatric symptoms; frank neurologic signs are not common early, but hyperreflexia, impaired coordination, changes in saccadic eye movements and incontinence may appear as it progresses. Diagnosis: EEG changes, MRI hyperintensities, abnormal 14-3-3 protein in CSF. Given the rapid course and late diagnosis, palliative care often consists largely of hospice care, with social work, end-of-life decisions and bereavement support (p876)

> Current practice (not the book): In a progressive neuropsychiatric disorder, positive RT-QuIC in CSF or another tested tissue can meet probable sporadic CJD criteria. CSF 14-3-3 is an alternative supportive test in the appropriate clinical pathway and is less specific. [CDC sporadic CJD criteria, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/creutzfeldt-jakob/hcp/clinical-overview/).
- **Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH)** makes up **~6%** of dementia cases; classic triad = gait/balance disturbance, urinary incontinence, cognitive deficits; unlike most other dementias, cognitive symptoms often present *later* in the course (p876)
- In one five-year follow-up study of shunted NPH patients, at 6 months, **83%** improved gait and **46%** improved memory; at 5 years, only **39%** of surviving shunt cases remained gait-improved and **<10%** still showed cognitive improvement (p876)

> Current practice (not the book): PENS enrolled patients selected for shunting after a positive CSF-drainage response. Active shunting improved gait velocity at three months compared with a placebo valve setting. The trial did not establish comparable short-term gains in cognition or bladder symptoms. [PENS randomized trial, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12682072/).
- CDC reported that **over half** of people living with diagnosed HIV in the US were aged 50 or older at the end of 2021. Hazzard cites an older 10% figure and reports **2–3%** progression to HIV-associated dementia with combination ART and **50–60%** prevalence of milder cognitive deficits in the cohorts it reviews (p876).
- Neurosyphilis dementia classically emerges **5-25 years** after initial infection (p876)
- Syphilitic dementia may present with hallucinations, delusions, mood disturbance, personality change, strokes, ataxia or cognitive decline. There is only limited evidence of cognitive benefit from penicillin; it is more likely with comorbid HIV and belongs in the differential of dementia of unclear cause (p876–877)

## Recall

**1. In normal aging, which type of intelligence is relatively preserved and which declines?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Crystallized (verbal) intelligence is relatively preserved; fluid intelligence (flexible reasoning/problem-solving) declines (p853).

</details>

**2. Which aspect of attention holds up well with normal aging, and which may decline?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sustained attention (vigilance) is preserved; divided attention (attending to more than one thing at once) may decline, though results are mixed (p854).

</details>

**4. In normal aging, which verbal fluency type declines (semantic or phonemic), and which stays stable?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Semantic fluency ("name all the animals you can") declines; phonemic fluency ("words starting with F") generally remains stable (p856).

</details>

**6. What proportion of patients show postoperative cognitive decline (POCD) after cardiac surgery at hospital discharge, and at 12 months?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

30-70% at discharge; still present in 15-25% at 12-month follow-up (p859).

</details>

**8. What fraction of patients diagnosed with MCI progress to AD, and over what time frame?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 80% progress to AD within 5 to 8 years, converting at roughly 10-15%/year vs 1-2%/year in the general population (p869).

</details>

**14. What percentage of dementia cases does normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) make up, and what are its shunt-surgery gait/memory outcomes at 6 months vs 5 years?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

NPH makes up about 6% of dementia cases. At 6 months post-shunt, 83% improve in gait and 46% improve in memory; of surviving shunt cases at 5 years, only 39% remain gait-improved and fewer than 10% still show cognitive improvement (p876).

</details>

**15. What proportion of people living with diagnosed HIV in the US were aged 50 or older in CDC's 2021 data, and how does the chapter distinguish dementia from milder cognitive deficits?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Over half were 50 or older at the end of 2021 in CDC surveillance. The chapter's treated cohorts had an estimated 2–3% progression to HIV-associated dementia, while milder cognitive deficits were more frequent (50–60%, p876).

</details>

**16. Which features separate corticobasal syndrome from Alzheimer disease early on?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Progressive, asymmetric motor signs (tremor, loss of coordination, rigidity, myoclonus), alien limb, and unilateral, most commonly ideomotor, apraxia; memory is typically well preserved early, in contrast to AD (p874).

</details>

**17. Which PPA variant preserves single-word repetition while sentence repetition fails, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Logopenic aphasia. The hypothesized mechanism is a short-term working memory deficit; single-word repetition needs minimal working memory (p874).

</details>

**18. As prion disease progresses, which neurologic signs may appear, and what supports the diagnosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hyperreflexia, impaired coordination, changes in saccadic eye movements and incontinence. EEG changes, MRI hyperintensities and abnormal 14-3-3 protein in the CSF (p876).

</details>
