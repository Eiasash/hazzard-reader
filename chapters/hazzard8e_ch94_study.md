## Renal anemia and inflammation

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-5967f5970f29d909cde6fe19" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**A low hemoglobin plus an elevated creatinine raises a question about erythropoietin.** Diseased kidneys may fail to increase erythropoietin enough to compensate for anemia. An erythropoiesis-stimulating agent (ESA) can support marrow production over time. In May 2024 Q36 a woman with pneumonia has a hemoglobin of 8.3 g/dL after transfusion, creatinine of 1.9 mg/dL, low iron and transferrin and high ferritin. Of the choices, a renal contribution is plausible. Aortic stenosis alone does not imply a prosthetic valve, LDH is normal, and the other blood counts give little reason to start with a marrow biopsy. The book describes ESA use in end-stage kidney disease. One creatinine during pneumonia does not show that the renal impairment is chronic (p1480).

> Current practice (not the book): Review prior kidney function, address the infection and other reversible causes, and reassess the need for an ESA. Transfusion and ESA treatment serve different time scales. [KDIGO 2026, Figure 5 and ESA initiation, pp. 49–52](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline-Executive-Summary.pdf).

<span id="ch94s-stable-renal"></span>**Stable kidney disease with mild anemia.** In June 2022 Q59 the patient is independent, has established kidney disease, hemoglobin of 11.1 g/dL, normal B12 and folate, and preserved leukocytes and platelets. The low transferrin and elevated ferritin make depleted stores less likely, although transferrin saturation still matters. Monitor the hemoglobin and clinical course. The historical exam cites GRS, Nephrology, Chronic Kidney Disease.

> Current practice (not the book): In nondialysis CKD, consider symptoms, persistent anemia and the risks of transfusion and ESA treatment when deciding whether to initiate an ESA. [KDIGO 2026, Figure 5 and pp. 51–52](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline-Executive-Summary.pdf).

<span id="ch94s-iron-restriction"></span>**Why iron falls during sepsis.** Inflammation increases hepcidin, limiting intestinal iron absorption and release of stored iron to developing red cells. Transferrin can fall and ferritin can rise as part of the inflammatory response. In 2020 Q41 the hemoglobin falls from 12.8 to 9.5 g/dL during severe infection, with CRP of 158 mg/L. Treat the infection and reassess the blood count with recovery. A low serum iron alone cannot justify intravenous iron. Persistent anemia warrants a search for additional causes, which can include coexisting blood loss. The 2020 question concerns the anemia of inflammation (Harrison, pp. 629–630). The corresponding material in the 21st edition is in Chapter 97, pp. 752–753, Figure 97-4 and Table 97-6.

## Anemia after a hip fracture

<span id="ch94s-postoperative"></span>**Start with the operation and the blood-count trend.** In June 2022 Q68 a man previously without known chronic illness reaches rehabilitation after fixation with hemoglobin of 8.5 g/dL, MCV of 88 fL, ferritin of 145 ng/mL and normal vitamin indices. Perioperative blood loss and fluid shifts give a coherent explanation for an isolated normocytic anemia. An unexpectedly large or continuing fall calls for reassessment, including ongoing bleeding. The historical exam cites GRS, Hematology. In a prospective hip-fracture cohort, the mean postoperative fall was 2.8 g/dL. Hazzard's related discussion explains the limited marrow reserve under stress (p1474). [Halm et al., Results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15213502/).

## From the first blood count to a cause

**Reticulocytes answer a different question from MCV.** Reticulocytes reflect the marrow's output. MCV describes average cell size and can still be normal with iron deficiency, recent blood loss, inflammation or renal anemia. Use the differential and platelet count to see whether more than one lineage is affected. Check ferritin and iron indices, B12, red-cell folate, creatinine, thyroid and liver function. A renal diagnosis alone is insufficient reason to skip the nutritional assessment (p1480).

**When to look further.** If the initial work-up is unrevealing, use the history and count pattern to select additional tests. LDH and haptoglobin help investigate suspected hemolysis. Electrophoresis looks for a monoclonal protein. Copper, zinc, CRP or erythropoietin levels can answer more focused questions. Marrow aspiration and biopsy become important when findings suggest myelodysplasia or leukemia. Unexplained anemia describes the residual group after an appropriate evaluation. Some people in this group have an inadequate erythropoietin response despite preserved excretory renal function. Clonal mutations account for only a minority of cases (p1480).

## Matching treatment to the patient

**Why the oral iron schedule matters.** Oral replacement suffices for many stable patients with documented iron deficiency. It raises hepcidin for up to 24 hours, limiting how much of the next day's dose reaches the circulation. The chapter therefore favors every-other-day treatment. If anemia persists despite an adequate course, consider continued losses or impaired absorption. Gastrointestinal intolerance or a poor response can justify intravenous iron. Examples include iron sucrose, ferric gluconate and ferric carboxymaltose (p1480).

**Acute ischemia and an adapted chronic baseline require different decisions.** The chapter describes a general restrictive threshold of 7 g/dL and 8 g/dL in patients with symptoms of end-organ ischemia. New chest pain or dyspnea with hemoglobin of 7.6 g/dL after previously tolerating chronic anemia changes the immediate decision. In the contrasting example of asymptomatic chronic hemolysis at a usual level of 6 g/dL, trying to maintain 7 g/dL adds transfusions without improving daily life. Examine the change from baseline, comorbidities, symptoms and treatment burden each time. New chest pain also warrants urgent assessment for acute coronary syndrome. Weekly injections can be a substantial burden for a frail patient (p1480).

> Current practice (not the book): For hospitalized patients with acute myocardial infarction, AABB conditionally suggests a more liberal transfusion strategy when hemoglobin is below 10 g/dL, with low-certainty evidence. [AABB, 2025 AMI transfusion guideline](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2025/08/20/expert-panel-recommends-liberal-transfusion-strategy-for-hospitalized-patients-with-ami).

## Why the aging marrow matters

**Reserve can fall before resting counts do.** Most blood production at rest comes from downstream progenitors. Quiescent hematopoietic stem cells provide reserve when demand rises. Reduced repopulating capacity can become apparent after hemorrhage, infection or inflammation. Even mild anemia in an older person is associated with falls, lower physical performance, poorer cognition and more hospitalizations. In cohorts that excluded iron deficiency, renal impairment and increased inflammatory markers, average hemoglobin changed little across age groups (pp. 1473–1474, 1479–1480).

**Clones and lineage choice.** A mutation that gives one stem cell a growth advantage produces a clone. Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) becomes more common with age and raises relative risk of a myeloid malignancy, yet most carriers do not develop leukemia. DNMT3A, TET2 and ASXL1 are frequent drivers. The aging stem-cell pool also favors myeloid output. Greater myeloid representation does not ensure normal myeloid function. Fewer lymphoid progenitors and fewer naïve B and T cells limit responses to unfamiliar pathogens and immunization (pp. 1474–1476, Figures 94-1 and 94-2).

**Changes inside the stem cell.** Mutations accumulate in long-lived stem cells despite ongoing DNA repair. Altered DNA methylation and histone marks change which genes are expressed. Mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species can damage these cells. Noncanonical Wnt5a–Cdc42 signaling contributes to loss of cell polarity. Reversal of some aging features in experimental models suggests a component that can be modified. These experiments do not provide a treatment for anemia in patients (pp. 1476–1478, Figures 94-3 and 94-4).

**The marrow environment changes too.** With age, stem cells shift from the relatively quiescent periendosteal arteriolar niche toward the sinusoidal niche that favors myeloid production. Mesenchymal stromal cells increasingly differentiate toward fat and provide less osteoblast support. Altered adrenergic signals also promote myeloid skewing. Inflammaging is persistent, low-grade systemic inflammation. Senescent T cells proliferate poorly yet can produce inflammatory signals. IL-1 favors myeloid differentiation, and IL-6 promotes platelet production. These changes help explain how chronic inflammation and weak protective immunity can coexist (pp. 1478–1480, Figure 94-5 and Table 94-1).

## Summary

**Hematopoietic aging**
- Aging brings impaired stem-cell function, greater clonal hematopoiesis and myeloid skewing with reduced lymphopoiesis; stress responses and immunity deteriorate (p1473).
- Intrinsic DNA/epigenetic/metabolic changes interact with the aging marrow niche and chronic inflammation (p1476–1479).
- Defective stress-induced hematopoiesis may be inapparent at baseline; anemia should prompt investigation for underlying disease (p1479–1480).

**Definition & causes**
- WHO thresholds used here are hemoglobin below **13 g/dL** in men and **12 g/dL** in women (p1479).
- Anemia affects over **10%** of community-dwelling adults at least **65**, and over **20%** at least **85**; even mild anemia is associated with adverse outcomes (p1479).
- Consider iron deficiency, inflammation, kidney disease, folate/cobalamin deficiency, myelodysplasia and other hematologic malignancy (p1480).
- Unexplained anemia accounts for **34–44%** in different studies, but more extensive work-up can reveal causes such as insufficient erythropoietin production (p1480).

**Assessment**
- Initial tests include CBC/differential, reticulocytes, ferritin/iron studies, creatinine, cobalamin, red-cell folate, thyroid and liver tests (p1480).
- If unrevealing, selectively assess erythropoietin, copper, zinc, inflammatory/hemolysis markers and serum electrophoresis (p1480).
- Suspected marrow malignancy requires marrow aspiration/biopsy to assess disorders such as myelodysplasia or leukemia (p1480).

**Management & drugs**
- Oral iron is usually sufficient for iron deficiency. The chapter favors alternate-day dosing because iron-induced hepcidin can inhibit next-day absorption (p1480).
- Intravenous iron is an alternative for intolerance or inadequate response. Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents have established uses in end-stage renal disease and selected myelodysplasia, but limited data in unexplained anemia (p1480).
- For acute symptomatic anemia, the chapter cites transfusion thresholds of **7 g/dL** generally and **8 g/dL** with end-organ ischemia symptoms, individualized for comorbidity and baseline state (p1480).

**Exam traps**
- Clonal mutations do not explain most unexplained anemia; increased frequency in anemia is only a small difference from controls (p1480).
- Treatment burden matters: frequent visits or transfusions without quality-of-life gain can harm frail patients. A threshold is not a universal target for every chronic anemia (p1480).
