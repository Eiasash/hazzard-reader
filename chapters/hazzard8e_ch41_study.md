# Chapter 41 Study: Managing the Care of Patients with Multiple Chronic Conditions

## Summary

**Think across conditions**
- Multiple chronic conditions interact with function, treatment burden and quality of life; counting diagnoses alone misses these effects (p605-606).
- Single-disease guidelines may conflict or create an impractical combined regimen (p609-610).
- Older adults with multimorbidity are underrepresented in trials; check applicability, outcomes meaningful to the person and possible interactions (p611).
- Interpret absolute benefit as well as relative effects, and compare time to benefit with health trajectory and time to harm (p611-612).

**Establish goals and capacity for care**
- Ask what matters to the patient, what outcomes they seek and what treatment workload they are willing and able to accept (p612-613).
- Ask permission and preferences for discussion of prognosis; broad functional expectations may help even without a precise estimate (p613).
- Treatment burden itself can reduce adherence. Approximately **40%** of older adults report some burden in the evidence discussed (p613).
- Identify caregivers, assess their needs and connect them with support while respecting the patient's autonomy and privacy (p612).
- Document and share priorities with the whole team; revisit them after illness, functional decline or major life changes (p613).

**Refine and coordinate the plan**
- Review medicines, appointments and self-management tasks together, not one disease at a time (p613).
- Consider stopping burdensome, harmful or goal-discordant care; weigh lag time to benefit against the expected course (p613).
- Deprescribing candidates include drugs without a valid indication, duplication, interactions, excessive dose, adverse effects or unfavorable benefit/cost (p611).
- Agree a taper/stop plan and monitor the consequences; patient and family preferences guide selection (p611).
- Explicit tools such as Beers and STOPP/START help, but do not replace individualized judgment (p610-611).
- Coordinate across clinicians to reduce redundant testing and conflicting recommendations; evidence for care-management programs is mixed (p612).
- Resolve disagreements around shared patient goals, offer alternatives and revisit decisions when appropriate (p613).

### Make benefits, harms and workload comparable

- Start with the event rate in a patient similar to the one in front of you. In the book's example the risk is **2%** with placebo and **1%** with treatment. That is a **1 percentage point** absolute reduction and a **50%** relative reduction. Both describe the same trial; neither guarantees the same benefit at a different baseline risk (p611).
- Check when the benefit might become apparent. The chapter's approximate examples are **10 years** for many cancer screenings and **2 to 3 years** for antihypertensive therapy. Ask whether that horizon and the current treatment workload fit the patient's expected course and goals (p613).
- The chapter reports averages of **2 hours** per day spent on health-related tasks and **2 hours** per health care visit among older adults and caregivers. A glucose check, transportation and waiting-room time all draw on the same finite capacity; ask which task is hardest to sustain (p613).
- During medication review, check a purported indication against the entire list. A proton-pump inhibitor and an H2 blocker may represent duplication; declining renal or hepatic function may instead call for dose adjustment. Agree what to monitor after any change (p611).

### Define the population before interpreting a statistic

Comorbidity and multimorbidity describe different perspectives on the same chart. In a study organized around heart failure, kidney disease and diabetes may be comorbidities of an index condition. From the patient's perspective there need be no permanent index disease: breathlessness may dominate this week while painful arthritis determines whether the patient can get to the bathroom. The definition used in this chapter requires at least **two concurrent chronic conditions** whose combined effect compromises health, function or quality of life and calls for more complex management or coordination. An incidental diagnosis appears on the list but need not determine today's priorities (p605–606).

A count only has meaning if the conditions eligible to be counted are specified. A survey considering common physical diseases will identify fewer combinations than one that also includes depression, chronic pain or other mental and physical conditions. The age and setting of the population also matter. People in a Medicare sample, community adults responding to a survey and patients who use a particular specialty clinic are different denominators. Before comparing two prevalence estimates, check whether they use the same definition, time period, population and method of ascertainment. An apparent change in the burden of disease can partly reflect these choices (p605–607).

Figure 41-1 describes US adults in **2014**. About **60% had at least one**, **42% more than one**, and **12% five or more** chronic conditions in the source estimates. These are overlapping categories; adding the percentages would count some people more than once. In the chapter's age-stratified estimates, more than **80% of Americans older than 65** had multiple conditions under a broad definition. Kidney disease in Medicare beneficiaries often coexists with diabetes or heart failure. Figure 41-2 asks what proportion of people with a named disease have other conditions; it cannot be used to rank how common each index disease is in the general population (p606–607).

### Social conditions, mental health and functional impact

In a Scottish study illustrated in Figure 41-3, multimorbidity appeared on average **10–15 years earlier** in the poorest areas than in the most affluent. This is a difference between area-level populations, not a prediction of when any given individual will become ill. The gap narrowed in very old age; the authors discuss survival bias as one possible explanation. Someone without reliable transport or money for medicines may have accumulated important constraints long before reaching traditional geriatric age cutoffs. Ask about the context rather than assuming the person first experienced complex care at retirement (p607–608).

Including mental health changes both prevalence and care planning. Among adults aged **50–74**, the chapter cites studies reporting depression in **14%, 21%, 30% and 37%** of groups with one, two, three and four physical conditions respectively. Cross-sectional gradients describe co-occurrence, not the direction of causation in a patient. Depression can also impair the ability to participate in self-management, while pain or reduced independence may shape mood. A management plan that recognizes heart failure, renal disease and arthritis but ignores loss of motivation or social support can miss the reason it has become impractical (p607–608).

Figure 41-4 compares physical, social, cognitive and activity limitations across numbers of chronic illnesses. A diagnosis count can be a marker for overall risk but not a substitute for asking which tasks need help. A person managing several stable diseases independently may find daily life less disrupted than someone with fewer diagnoses who cannot shop, organize tablets or walk to the clinic. Check basic activities separately from more complex activities, and ask about social contact and the ability to plan. A laboratory target cannot show whether the patient can organize a pill box, recognize a warning symptom or reach a specialist (p608–609).

The effect of a combination can differ from the sum of two isolated diseases. Restrictions from one condition or its treatment may frustrate another plan, while a treatment or service may help several conditions at once. Coexisting psychiatric illness can be associated with underrecognition or undertreatment of problems such as arthritis, whereas contact for a physical illness can also create opportunities to recognize mental health needs. Look for a specific mechanism in the patient's history before assuming that every combination leads in the same direction. The aim is to find the interaction that changes the next clinical decision (p607–609).

### Reading Table 41-1 as the patient's day

Table 41-1 reconstructs what single-disease recommendations would ask of one patient with multiple conditions. The early morning includes a bronchodilator, weekly alendronate **70 mg**, a period upright after administration, glucose or foot checks, and efforts to prepare for activity. Later tasks include several oral medicines, inhaler administration throughout the day, nutrition advice and other disease-specific self-care. Some tasks must be done at a particular time; others require supplies, understanding or someone to help. Even a patient who accepts the expected benefits of each treatment may be unable to fit this schedule around meals, sleep, fatigue and ordinary life (p609–610).

The morning medication column alone contains hydrochlorothiazide **12.5 mg**, lisinopril **40 mg**, glyburide **10 mg**, aspirin **81 mg**, metformin **850 mg**, naproxen **250 mg**, omeprazole **20 mg** and a calcium/vitamin D preparation listed at **500 mg**. The latter appears three times on the historical schedule, including noon and with the evening medicines. The evening also repeats metformin and naproxen and adds lovastatin **40 mg**. These are entries in a historical example intended to demonstrate cumulative treatment workload. They are not a prescribing template. Before applying any individual recommendation, reassess its indication, toxicity, interaction with the rest of the regimen and relevance to this person. The point of listing the actual medicines is to reconstruct what the patient has to do (p610).

Work also accumulates outside the medicine cabinet. Table 41-1 includes pulmonary rehabilitation, physical therapy, laboratory and bone-density testing, eye examinations, disease education and competing exercise or diet instructions. Weight-bearing exercise can be desirable for bone health while foot disease can make some forms inappropriate. Repeated clinic visits can each be individually justified but require the same caregiver to leave work and provide transportation. Review all of these demands together. A prescription review that reduces one tablet but adds several visits and home measurements may increase the net burden (p610, p612–613).

Apparent nonadherence can start a self-reinforcing sequence. An overloaded patient misses a medicine or measurement. A clinician sees an out-of-range result and increases treatment without learning which part of the existing plan was not being followed. The new instruction increases complexity again. Ask the patient to describe an actual day, including which instructions are regularly achievable, which are skipped and why. The answer may support a different time of administration, a simpler plan or agreement that a low-value task can stop. It may also reveal a high-value treatment that needs additional practical support (p610–613).

### Assessing external evidence for one patient

A clinical trial can estimate an average effect under its enrollment and follow-up conditions. First compare the participant population with the patient: were people of similar age, function, comorbidity or medication burden included? Then ask whether the measured outcome matters to the patient and how frequently it occurred. Reduction in a laboratory measure, a cause-specific event, hospitalization, loss of independence and overall survival represent different outcomes. If the trial excluded substantial renal disease or functional impairment, there may be little direct evidence about the balance of benefits and immediate harms in a person with those features (p609–612).

The denominator determines how large a change appears. When an event falls from **2% to 1%**, the absolute difference is one event per hundred people over the study period. Describing the result only as a halving of risk conceals how many participants experienced the event in the first place. Conversely, the absolute impact can be larger where the baseline risk is greater, if the relative effect is applicable. Do not assume that everyone with a higher disease risk gains proportionately more: competing risks and limited survival can prevent a patient from reaching the outcome or time point used in the trial (p611–612).

Compare when benefit is expected with when harm and work begin. A preventive intervention may require an immediate procedure or many months of daily tablets for a possible benefit years later. A medicine intended to relieve pain or breathlessness may instead have an effect the patient can evaluate promptly. The chapter's illustrative horizons of about a decade for many cancer screening benefits and a few years for some antihypertensive benefits are approximate. They depend on the intervention, endpoint and population. Using them as an exact survival cutoff would imply more certainty than the evidence or the patient's trajectory supports (p612–613).

Evidence can also be incomplete because the study followed only the disease being treated. Before applying a disease-specific recommendation, look for adverse effects involving the other conditions the patient already has. A fall, treatment-induced symptom or increased need for help may carry more weight for this patient than the research outcome selected by the investigators. When trials do not answer the exact question, explain what is known and what has to be estimated. A time-limited treatment trial with a defined symptom or functional goal can provide information when the response is observable soon; it cannot establish whether a long-lag preventive treatment will avert an event years later (p611–613).

Make the comparison in terms the patient can use. “The laboratory value improved” and “the treatment lets you get to breakfast without stopping” answer different questions. Before increasing treatment because a numerical target was missed, establish whether pursuing it still has a plausible net benefit, whether another condition limits the potential gain and whether the new work fits what the patient wants. This is especially important at transitions, when several clinicians may respond independently to the same abnormal result (p609–613).

### Medication review as a series of decisions

Begin with medicines actually taken, including over-the-counter products, intermittent use and prescriptions from different clinicians. Write the indication in a form that can be checked: which illness, symptom or future event does this medicine address? Finding a diagnosis somewhere in the chart does not prove that a medicine started years ago still meets a current need. Check whether a second medicine duplicates the first, whether current kidney or liver function changes the dose, and whether a new problem could be an adverse effect. The chapter's example of a PPI plus an H2 blocker prompts an indication review rather than an automatic stop (p610–611).

Explicit tools can make a review more systematic. Beers and STOPP flag potentially problematic drugs or situations, and START can identify beneficial treatment that may have been overlooked. A list is a starting point for a conversation; it cannot determine how much a patient values a particular outcome or whether the alternatives are practicable. The Medication Appropriateness Index asks **10 questions** per medicine and relies on clinical judgment; it offers more individualization but requires more time. A patient with many medicines may first need attention to the drug most likely to cause harm, not to whichever one appears first on a checklist (p610–611).

For each candidate for deprescribing, specify the intended change and why. The options include correcting an excessive dose, removing duplicate therapy, stopping a drug without a continuing indication and withdrawing a preventive medicine whose expected benefit is unlikely to be realized. A medicine can be appropriate in principle but unaffordable, difficult to administer or incompatible with a higher priority. Discuss the consequences of continuing as well as of stopping. Agree whether to taper, what symptom or measure will indicate a problem, when it will be checked and who can be contacted. Deprescribing without follow-up is an incomplete intervention (p611, p613).

Simplification can make space for indicated treatment. A patient who no longer needs a burdensome monitoring routine may gain time to attend rehabilitation that directly supports walking. Another may prioritize relief of symptoms even when this adds a tablet. The aim is to improve net benefit across the whole plan, not to reach a target medication count. When stopping a drug originally recommended by another service, communicate the actual reasoning and monitoring plan; otherwise the next specialist may interpret the omission as an error and restart it (p611–613).

### Goal setting when different answers are reasonable

Ask the patient to name an activity or outcome they most want to preserve or regain. Staying at home, walking to a nearby shop, having less pain while dressing, or avoiding an admission can translate broad values into decisions. A priority must be paired with what the person is willing and able to do. Wanting to remain independent does not mean agreeing to every intervention that might reduce future disability. A person may accept exercises but decline another test or medicine. Learn which tradeoffs are unacceptable before proposing a plan based only on disease-specific targets (p612–613).

A goals conversation can begin with the patient's understanding of their illnesses. Determine whether they want a general picture, detailed estimates or a discussion focused on practical changes such as needing more help at home. Prognostic information should address functional trajectory and recurrent hospitalizations as well as survival. Do not substitute the clinician's preference for discussing exact longevity when the patient is more concerned about remaining able to eat or bathe independently. Repeat the conversation when function, health, support or priorities change; an agreement reached during a stable year may not guide decisions after a major illness (p612–613).

Caregivers often perform symptom observation, appointment coordination, transport, treatment administration and communication across settings. Identify who does which task and, with the patient's agreement, invite those people into planning. Ask whether they have the knowledge, time and health to continue it. A daughter willing to help with an occasional appointment may be unable to make daily medication visits; identifying her as “the caregiver” without checking availability obscures a real gap. At the same time, a patient's own priorities remain central. Distinguish disagreement about the desired outcome from disagreement about which intervention is likely to achieve it (p612–613).

Approximately **40%** of older adults in the chapter's cited evidence report some degree of treatment burden. Its time estimates include both daily self-management and the hours spent on each visit. These averages are useful prompts, not a measure of how demanding any particular patient finds an activity. A finger-stick test can be quick but distressing; a clinic appointment can cost a full day when transport is unreliable. Ask which activity causes the most difficulty, what might be safely combined or stopped, and what symptoms or outcomes the patient fears losing control of if the plan changes (p613).

### Coordination and evidence for care programs

In the Medicare analysis, people with one condition saw an average of **4 physicians per year**, compared with **14** among those with **5 or more**. Each clinician may see only one part of a treatment sequence. Clarify who is responsible for interpreting tests, discussing adverse effects and deciding whether to continue a trial of therapy. Share a concise account of the patient's priority, the agreed action and the reason a competing recommendation was deferred. A list of referrals without this information can leave the patient responsible for reconciling incompatible instructions (p612).

When clinicians disagree, identify the common goal and verify that everyone is working from the same clinical facts. A renal concern and a cardiac concern can lead to different recommendations for a patient with both diseases. Discuss the outcome each clinician is trying to prevent, its probability and time horizon, then consider alternatives the patient can actually manage. If a patient understands the tradeoff and declines the clinician's proposal, document the decision and the circumstances under which it can be revisited. Insisting on consensus about every possible disease objective can delay agreement on the care that matters now (p605–606, p613).

A Cochrane review discussed in the chapter included **17 interventions**: **12** used organizational or system-level approaches such as case management, and the other **5** emphasized education and engagement. Some studies reported modest mental-health improvement and possible functional benefit. Clinical measurements, service use, adherence, behavior and costs did not consistently improve. This difference matters when selecting an intervention. A program that helps a patient manage depression or regain a meaningful activity may be worthwhile even without reducing total visits; a case-manager hire alone cannot be assumed to produce financial savings. Match the intervention to a specific difficulty and evaluate the outcome it was designed to change (p614).

## Exam traps

- A large relative reduction can accompany a small absolute benefit when baseline risk is low (p611).
- An individually reasonable recommendation can become impractical when combined with the rest of the regimen (p609–610, p613).
- A deprescribing list helps identify candidates; the actual taper, monitoring and decision depend on goals and the medicine (p610–611).
