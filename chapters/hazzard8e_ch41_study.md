# Chapter 41 Study: Managing the Care of Patients with Multiple Chronic Conditions

## Summary

### Think across conditions
- Multiple chronic conditions interact with function, treatment burden and quality of life; counting diagnoses alone misses these effects (p605-606).
- Single-disease guidelines may conflict or create an impractical combined regimen (p609-610).
- Older adults with multimorbidity are underrepresented in trials; check applicability, outcomes meaningful to the person and possible interactions (p611).
- Interpret absolute benefit as well as relative effects, and compare time to benefit with health trajectory and time to harm (p611-612).

### Establish goals and capacity for care
- Ask what matters to the patient, what outcomes they seek and what treatment workload they are willing and able to accept (p612-613).
- Ask permission and preferences for discussion of prognosis; broad functional expectations may help even without a precise estimate (p613).
- Treatment burden itself can reduce adherence. Approximately **40%** of older adults report some burden in the evidence discussed (p613).
- Identify caregivers, assess their needs and connect them with support while respecting the patient's autonomy and privacy (p612).
- Document and share priorities with the whole team; revisit them after illness, functional decline or major life changes (p613).

### Refine and coordinate the plan
- Review medicines, appointments and self-management tasks together, not one disease at a time (p613).
- Consider stopping burdensome, harmful or goal-discordant care; weigh lag time to benefit against the expected course (p613).
- Deprescribing candidates include drugs without a valid indication, duplication, interactions, excessive dose, adverse effects or unfavorable benefit/cost (p611).
- Agree a taper/stop plan and monitor the consequences; patient and family preferences guide selection (p611).
- Explicit tools such as Beers and STOPP/START help, but do not replace individualized judgment (p610-611).
- Coordinate across clinicians to reduce redundant testing and conflicting recommendations; evidence for care-management programs is mixed (p612).
- Resolve disagreements around shared patient goals, offer alternatives and revisit decisions when appropriate (p613).

### Make benefits, harms and workload comparable

- Start with the event rate in a patient similar to the one in front of you. In the book's example the risk is **2%** with placebo and **1%** with treatment. That is a **1 percentage point** absolute reduction and a **50%** relative reduction. Both describe the same trial; neither guarantees the same benefit at a different baseline risk (p611).
- Check when the benefit might become apparent. The chapter's approximate examples are **10 years** for many cancer screenings and **2 to 3 years** for antihypertensive therapy. Ask whether that horizon and the current treatment workload fit the patient's expected course and goals (p613).
- The chapter reports averages of **2 hours** per day spent on health-related tasks and **2 hours** per health care visit among older adults and caregivers. A glucose check, transportation and waiting-room time all draw on the same finite capacity; ask which task is hardest to sustain (p613).
- During medication review, check a purported indication against the entire list. A proton-pump inhibitor and an H2 blocker may represent duplication; declining renal or hepatic function may instead call for dose adjustment. Agree what to monitor after any change (p611).

## Exam traps

- A large relative reduction can accompany a small absolute benefit when baseline risk is low (p611).
- An individually reasonable recommendation can become impractical when combined with the rest of the regimen (p609–610, p613).
- A deprescribing list helps identify candidates; the actual taper, monitoring and decision depend on goals and the medicine (p610–611).

## Recall drill

**1. Why can adherence to several single-disease guidelines create a problem?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Combined treatments can interact, impose excessive work and lead to goals or instructions that conflict (p609–610).</details>

**2. What should be checked before applying trial evidence to a patient with multimorbidity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Enrollment criteria, baseline risk, outcomes meaningful to the patient, adverse effects and interactions with other treatments (p611).</details>

**3. If event risk falls from 2% to 1%, what are the absolute and relative reductions?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The absolute reduction is <strong>1 percentage point</strong> and the relative reduction is <strong>50%</strong> (p611).</details>

**4. Why might the trial's absolute benefit differ for another patient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Multimorbidity may alter baseline risk and the size of the treatment effect (p611).</details>

**5. What is time to benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The interval before a treatment's expected benefit appears. Compare that interval with the person's likely health trajectory (p611–613).</details>

**6. What are the book's approximate time-to-benefit examples?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>About <strong>10 years</strong> for many cancer screenings and <strong>2 to 3 years</strong> for antihypertensive therapy (p613).</details>

**7. How should a prognosis discussion begin?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask permission and how much detail the person wants; use functional expectations as well as survival if meaningful (p613).</details>

**8. What does treatment burden mean?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The adverse effect of managing treatments and health-related tasks on quality of life (p613).</details>

**9. How common was some treatment burden in the evidence cited?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Approximately <strong>40%</strong> of older adults reported it (p613).</details>

**10. What information should accompany the patient's health goals?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Which activities and outcomes matter most, and what care tasks the patient is willing and able to take on (p612–613).</details>

**11. What can trigger a medication dose review even if its indication remains sound?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Changed kidney or liver function, adverse effects or an interaction may require a dose change or substitution (p611).</details>

**12. Which medications may be candidates for discontinuation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Those without a valid indication, duplicative therapy, harmful interactions or drugs with little likely benefit relative to time, burden or cost (p611).</details>

**13. What follows selection of a drug for deprescribing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Agree how to taper or stop it and how to monitor for consequences; incorporate patient and family priorities (p611).</details>

**14. How can the caregiver's role be addressed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Identify what the caregiver does, their capacity and support needs, while respecting patient privacy and choice (p612).</details>

**15. What should the team do when disease-specific recommendations conflict?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Return to documented patient priorities, discuss feasible alternatives and share the agreed plan across clinicians (p612–613).</details>
