# Chapter 28 Study: Anesthesia

## Summary

**Choose and monitor anesthesia**
- Match general, regional/neuraxial or monitored anesthesia care to the procedure, comorbidities and patient preferences (p384).
- Older adults are more sensitive to anesthetics and opioids; age-adjust dosing and monitor depth, ventilation, circulation and temperature (p383-385).
- Avoid excessive depth and hypotension while preventing awareness; processed EEG may supplement clinical monitoring (p383-385).
- In general, keep BP within **20%** of baseline and MAP at least **65 mm Hg**; older patients, especially with hypertension, may need substantially higher targets (p388).
- Regional techniques can improve analgesia and reduce opioid use, but the book finds no conclusive superiority for major postoperative outcomes (p388).
- Anticoagulants require coordinated planning for neuraxial placement, catheter removal and medication resumption (p388).

**Cognition and medicines**
- Delirium and longer-term cognitive dysfunction can coexist but are distinct; their relationship and anesthetic causation remain uncertain (p390-391).
- Prevention emphasizes orientation, mobilization, sleep, sensory aids and adequate pain control; no preventive pharmaceutical is recommended in the chapter (p384, p396).
- For monitored sedation, the book discusses reduced doses of propofol, ketamine or dexmedetomidine and avoidance of benzodiazepines; continuously monitor breathing because light sedation can deepen rapidly (p388).
- Review anticholinergic and other high-risk drugs; dose details and agent-specific cautions are in the source tables (p386-387, p392).

**Cardiac procedures and goals**
- Cardiac operations have procedure-specific hemodynamic and monitoring needs; off-pump benefits remain uncertain overall, with possible benefit in geriatric subgroup analyses (p384, p392-393).
- TAVR may use sedation or general anesthesia; inability to lie still/flat, imaging needs or complications may require conversion (p393-394).
- Clarify the device indication and whether the patient is pacemaker-dependent. Plan for ICD interference and rescue during the procedure (p396).
- Do not automatically suspend an Allow Natural Death order. Discuss and document acceptable interventions, goals and the period of any modification with the patient and perioperative team (p395-396).

### Why the same anesthetic dose can have a different effect

An older adult may have a greater and later hemodynamic response to an induction dose. Table 28-1 advises smaller, incremental doses with time to assess effect. Propofol can cause hypotension. Etomidate depresses cortisol production; the chapter flags sepsis as a concern. Morphine can depress respiration and its active glucuronide metabolite is cleared less effectively in renal failure. The table gives agent-specific cautions rather than one dose for every patient (p385–387).

During general anesthesia, use ventilation, oxygenation, circulatory and temperature monitoring. A nerve stimulator helps assess neuromuscular blockade and its reversal. Even a small residual block can impair pharyngeal muscles and raise aspiration risk. During monitored anesthesia care the patient may still breathe spontaneously, but supplemental oxygen does not replace assessment of respiratory rate and end-tidal carbon dioxide (p385, p388).

### What changes with the route of anesthesia

A peripheral block acts around a nerve. A spinal block is subarachnoid, whereas an epidural can be maintained through a catheter. Regional techniques may spare opioids and provide continuing analgesia. For anticoagulated patients, the question includes timing of catheter removal and medication restart, drug clearance and the consequences of bleeding into a noncompressible space. Modality alone does not reliably predict major postoperative outcomes (p388).

The term perioperative neurocognitive disorders spans pre-existing cognitive illness and new postoperative syndromes. In the terminology summarized here, delayed neurocognitive recovery covers decline up to **30 days**, while postoperative neurocognitive disorder describes decline persisting or identified up to **12 months**. Acute postoperative delirium has an acute, fluctuating course and needs prompt evaluation (p389–390).

## Exam traps

- Spontaneous breathing during sedation does not ensure adequate ventilation; monitor the patient throughout (p388).
- The chapter's general blood-pressure floor needs interpretation relative to baseline, especially in chronic hypertension (p388).
- An established limit on resuscitation should prompt a perioperative discussion of particular interventions and timing (p395–396).

## Recall drill

**1. Which factors determine the choice between general, regional and monitored anesthesia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The procedure, comorbidities, risks and patient or surrogate preferences, discussed with the anesthesia team (p384, p388).</details>

**2. Why should induction drugs be given slowly in smaller increments?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Changes in distribution and clearance increase sensitivity; the hemodynamic response may be accentuated or delayed (p385–386).</details>

**3. What is a major concern with propofol induction in an older patient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Hypotension. Titrate to effect while monitoring circulation (p386).</details>

**4. Why does kidney function affect the choice or dosing of morphine?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Its active metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide can accumulate when renal clearance is impaired, increasing risk of prolonged opioid effects (p386).</details>

**5. Why verify recovery from a neuromuscular block before extubation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Residual blockade can impair pharyngeal function and predispose to aspiration (p385).</details>

**6. What can processed EEG add to routine anesthetic monitoring?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An additional estimate of depth when usual signals are difficult to interpret, supporting titration while avoiding both excessive anesthesia and awareness (p385).</details>

**7. What general BP targets does the book describe?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Within <strong>20%</strong> of baseline, MAP at least <strong>65 mm Hg</strong> and systolic pressure at least <strong>100 mm Hg</strong>. Chronic hypertension may call for higher targets (p388).</details>

**8. How do spinal and epidural blocks differ anatomically?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A spinal block delivers local anesthetic into the subarachnoid space; an epidural acts in the epidural space (p388).</details>

**9. Which decisions require coordination for an anticoagulated patient receiving a neuraxial catheter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When to stop medication, place the block, maintain or remove the catheter, and restart therapy in light of agent and clearance (p388).</details>

**10. What must be followed in a spontaneously breathing patient receiving monitored anesthesia care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide and other standard vital signs; light sedation can deepen rapidly (p388).</details>

**11. What advantage of a regional technique is well supported in the chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Analgesia with reduced opioid requirements. Studies cited do not establish broad superiority over general anesthesia for mortality or delirium (p388).</details>

**12. Which postoperative syndromes use the 30-day and 12-month windows?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Delayed neurocognitive recovery covers decline up to <strong>30 days</strong>; postoperative neurocognitive disorder covers a persisting or identified decline up to <strong>12 months</strong> (p389).</details>

**13. Which preventive measures matter for postoperative delirium?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Multicomponent care including orientation, sleep protection, sensory aids, mobilization and adequate pain treatment (p384, p390, p396).</details>

**14. What may necessitate general anesthesia or conversion during TAVR?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Inability to cooperate or lie flat, specific imaging needs or a procedural complication can make sedation insufficient (p393–394).</details>

**15. How should an existing Allow Natural Death order be addressed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Discuss permissible measures and goals with patient or surrogate and team, and document any modification and when it ends (p395–396).</details>
