# Chapter 28 Study: Anesthesia

## Summary

**Choose and monitor anesthesia**
- Match general, regional/neuraxial or monitored anesthesia care to the procedure, comorbidities and patient preferences (p384).
- Older adults are more sensitive to anesthetics and opioids; age-adjust dosing and monitor depth, ventilation, circulation and temperature (p383-385).
- Avoid excessive depth and hypotension while preventing awareness; processed EEG may supplement clinical monitoring (p383-385).
- In general, keep BP within **20%** of baseline with systolic BP at least **100 mm Hg** and MAP at least **65 mm Hg**; older patients, especially with hypertension, may need substantially higher targets (p388).
- Regional techniques can improve analgesia and reduce opioid use, but the book finds no conclusive superiority for major postoperative outcomes (p388).
- Anticoagulants require coordinated planning for neuraxial placement, catheter removal and medication resumption (p388).

**Cognition and medicines**
- Delirium and longer-term cognitive dysfunction can coexist but are distinct; their relationship and anesthetic causation remain uncertain (p390-391).
- Prevention emphasizes orientation, mobilization, sleep, sensory aids and adequate pain control; no preventive pharmaceutical is recommended in the chapter (p384, p396).

> Current practice (not the book): Neither spinal nor general anesthesia has shown a consistent delirium advantage for hip fracture surgery. Consider perioperative dexmedetomidine selectively, weighing bradycardia and hypotension. Maintain the non-drug prevention measures. [ASA perioperative care advisory for older adults, 2025](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/anesthesiology/fulltext/10.1097/aln.0000000000005172~2025-american-society-of-anesthesiologists-practice-advisory).
- For monitored sedation, the book discusses reduced doses of propofol, ketamine or dexmedetomidine and avoidance of benzodiazepines; continuously monitor breathing because light sedation can deepen rapidly (p388).
- Review anticholinergic and other high-risk drugs; dose details and agent-specific cautions are in the source tables (p386-387, p392).

**Cardiac procedures and goals**
- Cardiac operations have procedure-specific hemodynamic and monitoring needs; off-pump benefits remain uncertain overall, with possible benefit in geriatric subgroup analyses (p384, p392-393).
- TAVR may use sedation or general anesthesia; inability to lie still/flat, imaging needs or complications may require conversion (p393-394).
- Clarify the device indication and whether the patient is pacemaker-dependent. Plan for ICD interference and rescue during the procedure (p396).
- Do not automatically suspend an Allow Natural Death order. Discuss and document acceptable interventions, goals and the period of any modification with the patient and perioperative team (p395-396).

### Why the same anesthetic dose can have a different effect

An older adult may have a greater and later hemodynamic response to an induction dose. Table 28-1 advises smaller, incremental doses with time to assess effect. Propofol can cause hypotension. Etomidate depresses cortisol production; the chapter flags sepsis as a concern. Morphine can depress respiration and its active glucuronide metabolite is cleared less effectively in renal failure. The table gives agent-specific cautions rather than one dose for every patient (p385–387).

During general anesthesia, use ventilation, oxygenation, circulatory and temperature monitoring. A nerve stimulator helps assess neuromuscular blockade and its reversal. Even a small residual block can impair pharyngeal muscles and raise aspiration risk. During monitored anesthesia care the patient may still breathe spontaneously, but supplemental oxygen does not replace assessment of respiratory rate and end-tidal carbon dioxide (p385, p388).

### What changes with the route of anesthesia

A peripheral block acts around a nerve. A spinal block is subarachnoid, whereas an epidural can be maintained through a catheter. Regional techniques may spare opioids and provide continuing analgesia. For anticoagulated patients, the question includes timing of catheter removal and medication restart, drug clearance and the consequences of bleeding into a noncompressible space. Modality alone does not reliably predict major postoperative outcomes (p388).

The term perioperative neurocognitive disorders spans pre-existing cognitive illness and new postoperative syndromes. In the terminology summarized here, delayed neurocognitive recovery covers decline up to **30 days**, while postoperative neurocognitive disorder describes decline persisting or identified up to **12 months**. Acute postoperative delirium has an acute, fluctuating course and needs prompt evaluation (p389–390).

### Designing the anesthetic around the patient and procedure

Describe what the operation requires before comparing techniques. Will the patient need to remain immobile for a prolonged period? Must the team control ventilation or obtain transesophageal images? How much postoperative pain is expected, and which interventions would the patient accept if a complication occurred? Then ask how the person functioned before this illness. Baseline cognition, hearing, mobility and ability to lie flat affect both the anesthetic plan and the interpretation of postoperative changes. Geriatric surgical programs bring this information into the operative handoff so that a patient who is normally attentive and independent is recognized when they return to the ward quiet and inattentive (p383–385, p389–392).

Figure 28-1 distinguishes monitored anesthesia care, regional anesthesia, general anesthesia and a combined regional/general approach. General anesthesia may be maintained with an inhaled agent such as isoflurane or sevoflurane, with an intravenous technique, or with a combination. Regional techniques include spinal and epidural blockade, peripheral nerve blocks for a limb and blocks for the trunk. A block can be part of a general anesthetic or provide continuing postoperative analgesia. Knowing the name of a technique is not enough to predict how sedated the patient was, what the airway required or how much postoperative opioid they will need (p384–385, p388).

### Intravenous agents: relate the adverse effect to organ reserve

Table 28-1 emphasizes that the initial distribution of hypnotic drugs changes with age and clearance of some hepatically extracted drugs falls as liver blood flow decreases. A hemodynamic effect may be delayed. Repeating a dose before observing its full effect can overshoot the intended depth. The table consequently describes reduced, incremental induction dosing for older patients. It does not give a fixed milligram dose for each hypnotic. Compare agents by the feature relevant to this patient's circulation, respiration and postoperative cognition rather than by onset speed alone (p385–386).

Propofol permits rapid induction and maintenance and has antiemetic properties, but hypotension is important when perfusion reserve is limited. Etomidate is discussed for patients with impaired ventricular function, with cortisol suppression a concern in sepsis; involuntary movements, nausea and vomiting may occur. Ketamine can provide analgesia and dissociative anesthesia but produces sympathetic effects, secretions and sometimes distressing emergence phenomena. Methohexital has a role in electroconvulsive therapy and can produce excitatory movements. Thiopental can provoke cough or laryngospasm. Midazolam provides anxiolysis and amnesia, but Table 28-1 advises avoiding it in older patients because it contributes to postoperative delirium and identifies flumazenil as short acting. An apparent recovery after reversal still requires observation (p386).

Opioids differ in kinetics while sharing respiratory and central nervous system effects. Morphine can produce histamine-related effects, constipation and urinary retention, and its active glucuronide metabolite can persist with renal failure. Fentanyl has rapid onset and can cause chest-wall rigidity at high doses. Sufentanil also depresses respiration. Remifentanil is ultra-short acting and can be titrated by infusion. Alfentanil is short acting, but coadministration of erythromycin reduces its clearance. The book gives a class-level suggestion to reduce opioid dosing by **50% in patients aged 80 or older** and titrate to effect. Apply this as the chapter's monitored perioperative principle, not an equipotency rule between drugs or routes. Naloxone reverses opioid effects (p386–387).

### Residual weakness and respiratory recovery

Neuromuscular blockade improves surgical conditions but is distinct from hypnosis, amnesia and analgesia. Consider both the intended intraoperative effect and recovery of the muscles needed to keep the upper airway open, swallow and cough. Older adults can be clinically vulnerable to residual pharyngeal weakness even after visible limb movement returns. Determine the depth of block, allow for prolonged action when clearance is reduced, and check recovery after reversal rather than relying solely on elapsed time (p385–388).

Pancuronium is long acting, with **30%–70%** excreted unchanged in urine according to Table 28-1. Table 28-1 advises avoiding pancuronium in renal disease because impaired filtration can prolong its action; tachycardia may also occur. The intermediate-acting nondepolarizers include rocuronium and vecuronium. The table advises avoiding nondepolarizing agents in neuromuscular disease such as myasthenia gravis because their effects can be prolonged. Cisatracurium is highlighted for its limited dependence on organ clearance and comparatively preserved recovery with aging. Succinylcholine is a short-acting depolarizing agent with a different role. Neostigmine or sugammadex is listed for reversal of vecuronium or rocuronium. The chapter's practical endpoint is recovery adequate for the planned airway management, not the mere fact that a reversal agent has been given (p387).

> Current practice (not the book): For patients receiving neuromuscular blockers, ASA recommends quantitative monitoring at the adductor pollicis and confirmation of a train-of-four ratio of at least 0.9 before extubation. [ASA monitoring and antagonism practice guideline, 2023](https://pubs.asahq.org/anesthesiology/article-pdf/138/1/13/682670/20230100.0-00011.pdf).

### Following perfusion, ventilation and temperature

An older person with longstanding hypertension may lose adequate cerebral or coronary perfusion at a pressure that looks acceptable in isolation. The authors use a general target within **20%** of baseline pressure and a mean arterial pressure of at least **65 mm Hg**, with higher individual targets sometimes needed. Studies cited associate even relatively brief hypotension with adverse outcomes; the depth and duration of the change matter. Interpret a low reading in relation to the baseline, bleeding, anesthetic depth, vascular tone, rhythm and ventricular function rather than assuming a single intervention fits each cause (p388).

Pulse oximetry answers a different question from capnography. Supplemental oxygen can maintain saturation during impaired ventilation. Observe the respiratory pattern and airway and use end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring as appropriate, including during sedation outside the operating room. With age-related sensitivity, a patient who began a procedure breathing comfortably can become obstructed or hypoventilate as additional medication takes effect. Temperature deserves similar attention: active warming and maintaining normothermia are part of protecting an older patient from perioperative stress. Communicate episodes of hypotension, hypoxia or delayed recovery to the postoperative team (p385, p388–389, p391–392).

The level of block and the timing of anticoagulant therapy affect regional choices. A peripheral technique can reduce systemic analgesic requirements and an epidural may provide ongoing relief through a catheter. Bleeding into a confined space can have serious consequences. Consider not only placement but catheter removal, timing of drug resumption, formulation and the patient's kidney function. Anticoagulant clearance may be prolonged in an older patient. The chapter does not offer one safe interval applicable to all antithrombotic agents and procedures (p388).

### Counselling about postoperative outcomes

Table 28-2 gives approximate ranges for older surgical patients. Delirium is listed at **12%–24%** and cognitive dysfunction at **10%–16%**, compared with **2%–4%** for pneumonia, **0.4%–2%** for myocardial infarction and **0.3%–1%** for stroke. The other estimates are **0.2%–0.5%** for pulmonary embolism, **0.4%–0.6%** for acute renal failure, **0.6%–6%** for heart failure and **2%–5%** for death. These outcomes differ in definition and can overlap. Adding the percentages is not a way to estimate the risk for an individual facing a particular operation (p389).

Ask about pre-existing changes in concentration, learning and daily function before surgery. Postoperative delirium develops acutely, often fluctuates and requires a search for precipitating causes. Delayed neurocognitive recovery and postoperative neurocognitive disorder describe cognitive changes assessed on longer time scales. An older person can have both cognitive impairment at baseline and a superimposed postoperative episode. Without baseline information it is difficult to separate a pre-existing limitation from a new decline. Tell the recovery team what the patient could do before admission and what constitutes a meaningful change to them (p389–391).

Studies asking whether a particular technique prevents delirium must be read with their comparators. In ENGAGES, older adults undergoing major surgery received EEG-guided anesthesia or usual care. The book reports delirium in **26%** versus **23%**, respectively, without a significant benefit from the guided strategy. Other studies the chapter discusses raised the possibility of benefit from avoiding excessive depth. A monitor can inform titration and reveal a cerebral response to anesthetic without guaranteeing prevention of delirium. Similarly, regional anesthesia and general anesthesia are broad labels: sedation depth, underlying risk and postoperative care can differ within either arm of a comparison (p389–391).

### Constructing a prevention and recovery plan

In Table 28-3 the panels agree on preoperative cognitive screening. Several support multicomponent care and avoiding high-risk medicines. Their recommendations are less consistent for processed EEG monitoring, regional anesthesia and cerebral perfusion measures. Some entries indicate no recommendation or an inability to recommend a strategy. This represents an evidence limitation, not evidence that every useful component must succeed as a stand-alone intervention. Maintain hearing and vision aids, protect sleep, support orientation, mobilize where feasible and address pain, oxygenation and other physiological disturbances (p390–392).

Pain itself can impede sleep, breathing and mobility; excessive sedative or opioid exposure can also impair attention and ventilation. Review what the patient actually received in theatre, recovery and on the ward before adding another agent. Table 28-4 includes diclofenac, ibuprofen, etodolac, meloxicam and naproxen among NSAIDs with gastrointestinal bleeding concerns, particularly at age **75 or older** or with anticoagulants, antiplatelets or corticosteroids. Table 28-4 advises avoiding ketorolac because of risks of gastrointestinal bleeding, peptic ulcer disease and acute renal injury, while indomethacin has particular CNS adverse effects. Gabapentin requires dose reduction with a GFR below **60** in the table and should be avoided in end-stage renal disease; avoid meperidine, particularly in chronic kidney disease because of its metabolite (p392).

For postoperative nausea and vomiting, distinguish the proposed agent's antiemetic benefit from its cognitive and motor effects. The table lists ondansetron **4 mg IV every 6 hours**, haloperidol **0.5–1 mg every 6 hours** and metoclopramide **5 mg IV once** as examples, along with propofol and aprepitant for selected circumstances. These are the printed table entries, not an instruction to combine or repeat all of them. The table advises avoiding dexamethasone for postoperative nausea and vomiting, especially doses above **4 mg**, and avoiding or using antipsychotics cautiously because of delirium risk. It advises avoiding diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine, scopolamine, promethazine, prochlorperazine and tricyclic agents because of central anticholinergic effects and oversedation. Review the entire exposure when an older patient becomes inattentive (p392).

> Current practice (not the book): The book does not specify a route for haloperidol in this row. US labeling does not approve IV haloperidol lactate; higher doses and IV administration increase the risk of QT prolongation and torsades. If administered IV, monitor the ECG. [Haloperidol lactate prescribing information, 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=e2eed126-cb82-42b1-9b48-9cbeb6873b50).

### Cardiac procedures: why the anesthetic decision changes with the anatomy

Coronary disease is common in older surgical patients, but a diagnosis of coronary disease is not the same as evidence that every patient needs the same invasive monitoring. The chapter describes using an arterial line where rapid pressure changes or limited reserve would make intermittent readings inadequate. The question is what information a measurement adds and what the team could change in response. During coronary procedures consider the balance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand, baseline ventricular function, rhythm and the hemodynamic consequences of anesthesia or manipulation of the heart (p392–393).

Conventional coronary bypass uses cardiopulmonary bypass. Off-pump surgery avoids the pump but has its own technical and hemodynamic demands. A minimally invasive thoracotomy uses a smaller incision; the authors caution that pain can nevertheless be greater than with sternotomy. The chapter does not establish a general superiority of off-pump surgery for all patients. Subgroup findings in older people and an individual patient's coronary anatomy, comorbidity and surgical expertise can inform the discussion. Percutaneous coronary intervention avoids some surgical stress but still requires a plan for cooperation, access complications and cardiovascular instability (p392–393).

Degenerative aortic stenosis is common in later life. TAVR can be performed with monitored sedation or general anesthesia. A patient who cannot lie flat, tolerate imaging or remain still may require a different plan from one who can cooperate comfortably. Access problems, bleeding or cardiac instability can require conversion during a planned sedated procedure. The chapter cites complication rates up to **20%** in its TAVR material, including approximately **2%** stroke, **3.6%** significant postprocedural leak and **6%** major bleeding. The figures describe the experience at the time of the book, not a contemporary risk calculator for a particular device or center (p393–394).

Mitral regurgitation can reflect primary valve disease or an effect of ventricular dysfunction. The chapter contrasts surgical repair or replacement with catheter approaches in selected patients. Imaging can be central to assessing the lesion and the result of repair; the need for transesophageal imaging and patient immobility affects the anesthetic plan. For atrial fibrillation ablation, the chapter describes general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation and arterial monitoring; systemic heparin is used during left-atrial instrumentation (p394). Evaluate the patient's ability to lie still, antithrombotic therapy and baseline rhythm. Device implantation and electrophysiologic procedures may be less invasive than open surgery while still requiring readiness for sudden cardiovascular or respiratory events (p394–395).

### Pacemakers, defibrillators and acceptable rescue

Identify why the cardiac device was implanted, what it is programmed to do and whether the patient depends on pacing. A high proportion of ventricular pacing in a resynchronization device can be expected rather than proof of malfunction. Electromagnetic interference and the potential for an inappropriate shock matter during surgery. Obtain a device-specific perioperative plan, clarify how rhythm will be monitored and how pacing or defibrillation would be provided if needed, and arrange restoration of the intended postoperative settings. Distinguish a discussion about disabling shocks from one about ongoing pacing support (p395–396).

A pre-existing order limiting resuscitation deserves a conversation before anesthesia. Airway support, a vasoactive medicine or a brief reversible rhythm disturbance in theatre may not mean the same thing to the patient as prolonged ventilation or intensive care. Explain the likely perioperative circumstances, identify treatments the patient accepts or declines, agree when any modification ends, document it and communicate it to the recovery team. If capacity is impaired, involve the appropriate decision-maker and use the patient's known priorities. In a US study cited, **32%** underwent inpatient surgery in the last year of life, **18%** in the last month and **8%** in the last week. That frequency makes a specific perioperative conversation relevant even for an apparently routine procedure (p395–396).
One hemodynamic consequence of correcting mitral regurgitation deserves special attention. Before repair, part of the ventricular stroke volume can leave through the regurgitant valve. After repair the effective load against which the left ventricle ejects may increase substantially. Preoperative ventricular function and the ability to tolerate that change are therefore relevant to patient selection and postoperative support. The operative mortality estimate below **2%** cited in the chapter came from patients whose **average age was 60**. Using that figure as an individual estimate for a frail, much older person would discard the population on which it was based (p394).

The patient's experience of an implanted defibrillator can also influence preparation. The chapter reports that 30%–40% of ICD recipients may experience depressive or posttraumatic stress symptoms related to needing the device, and describes anxiety about shocks. Explain who will monitor the rhythm and what support is available if device therapies are temporarily changed. Review the plan for electrocautery with the procedural team and confirm the postoperative handoff. This discussion can reduce anxiety and reveal a preference about future shocks that the usual device interrogation cannot answer. Incorporate that preference into the wider goals-of-care conversation rather than relying on the device's default programming (p395–396).

## Exam traps

- A broader or combined anesthetic block may reduce analgesic requirements without guaranteeing protection from delirium (p388–391).
- Oxygen saturation can remain reassuring during hypoventilation on supplemental oxygen. Observe airflow and ventilation as well (p388–389).
- A patient moving a limb after anesthesia can still have residual pharyngeal weakness (p385–387).
- A shorter incision in off-pump bypass does not establish less pain after thoracotomy (p393).
- Do not infer pacemaker dependence solely from a high percentage of ventricular pacing in a resynchronization device (p396).
- Limits on postoperative life support may differ from the patient's wishes for a brief, reversible intraoperative complication (p395–396).
- Spontaneous breathing during sedation does not ensure adequate ventilation; monitor the patient throughout (p388).
- The chapter's general blood-pressure floor needs interpretation relative to baseline, especially in chronic hypertension (p388).
- An established limit on resuscitation should prompt a perioperative discussion of particular interventions and timing (p395–396).
