# Chapter 93 Study: Skin Cancer

## Summary

**Definition & risk**
- Skin cancer is the most common US malignancy; cumulative ultraviolet exposure is a major risk. Most cases in older adults are basal or squamous cell carcinomas (p1455).
- Actinic keratoses are precancerous epidermal lesions on sun-exposed skin. Not every lesion progresses to squamous cell carcinoma (p1456, p1458).
- Melanoma and Merkel cell carcinoma are more aggressive, with greater nodal and distant metastatic potential (p1455).

**Assessment**
- Biopsy lesions suspicious for keratinocyte carcinoma; assess site, size, histology and recurrence risk when choosing treatment (p1460–1462).
- Melanoma clues: asymmetry, irregular borders, varied color and evolution; an “ugly duckling” unlike the patient's other lesions deserves attention (p1464).
- Diameter above **6 mm** alone is relatively insensitive. Small lesions can still be concerning if dark or changing (p1464).
- Suspected melanoma needs complete full-thickness excision with narrow **2-mm** margins or deep saucerization; superficial shaving can prevent accurate depth measurement (p1467).

**Management & drugs**
- Isolated actinic keratoses: liquid-nitrogen cryotherapy. High-risk keratinocyte cancers commonly require Mohs surgery with margin control (pp1460–1462). Table 93-2 includes the **eyelid** among high-risk facial sites for both basal and squamous cell carcinoma.
- Topical fluorouracil: for actinic keratoses, once daily for up to **4 weeks**; imiquimod: twice weekly for **16 weeks**. Both can cause irritation, dryness and burning (p1461).
- The **5%** formulations can treat selected biopsy-confirmed low-risk superficial basal cell cancers; their cancer dosing differs from actinic-keratosis dosing (p1461). For advanced basal cell carcinoma, the book lists **vismodegib and sonidegib** as systemic options (p1462).

> Current practice (not the book): PD-1 or PD-L1 blockade is an option for adults with metastatic or locally advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma who are not candidates for curative surgery or radiation. Assess autoimmune disease, transplant status and immune-related adverse effects. [FDA cosibelimab prescribing information, 2026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/761297s004lbl.pdf).
- Definitive melanoma management incorporates depth and nodal assessment; sentinel node biopsy is ideally coordinated with definitive excision when indicated (p1467–1468).
- Explain surgical recovery, reconstruction, treatment visits and functional demands; age alone should not determine treatment (p1470–1471).


### Choosing biopsy, local surgery or systemic treatment

- A typical actinic keratosis can be assessed clinically. Induration, thickening or failure to respond to treatment raises concern for invasive squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and warrants biopsy. A firm, sometimes tender hyperkeratotic nodule that bleeds can represent SCC. Even a keratoacanthoma that appears capable of regressing is often excised because its behavior is unpredictable (p1457, p1459–1460).
- Nodular basal cell carcinoma (BCC) often begins as a pearly papule with surface vessels and a rolled edge. Superficial BCC can resemble eczema. Reassess a persistent plaque that fails usual therapy and biopsy a suspicious lesion or persistent ulcer before choosing a destructive or topical treatment (p1457–1460).
- Eyelid location and recurrence both raise concern for occult extension. Assess extent, surgical feasibility and suitability for radiation. These clinical details determine whether margin-controlled surgery or systemic treatment is appropriate. The chapter discusses the hedgehog inhibitor vismodegib for metastatic or locally advanced inoperable BCC when other modalities are unsuitable. A difficult site alone does not establish that indication (p1460, p1462).
- Too few older adults received vismodegib for an age-specific comparison; in sonidegib trials 54% were at least 65, with similar efficacy but more serious and grade 3–4 adverse events. Muscle spasms and altered taste can limit oral intake or treatment tolerance, particularly in a patient already losing weight (p1462–1463).
- For a pigmented lesion, compare its two halves, inspect the border and the colors within the lesion, compare it with the patient's other nevi, and ask about change in size, shape, color or itching. Evolution or an outlying appearance can matter even in a small lesion. Biopsy depth affects whether the pathologist can measure invasion accurately (p1464, p1467).

### Begin with exposure, immune status and the patient's other skin

A personal history of skin cancer changes what a new lesion might mean. Establish the histology and previous treatment. A new plaque at the edge of a surgical scar raises a different question from a new papule at a distant site, and someone cured of an earlier cancer remains at increased risk of a new primary. The chapter attributes approximately **79%** of skin cancers to BCC, **14%** to SCC and **5%** to melanoma. Frequency is a poor guide to the harm of delay: BCC accounts for the largest number of lesions, but melanoma causes a disproportionate share of skin cancer deaths. A slow BCC beside the eyelid can still cause substantial local damage (p1455, p1457–1458).

Table 93-1 is an exposure history rather than a laboratory screen. Ask about work and recreation outdoors, indoor tanning, blistering sunburns, protective clothes, hats, shade and sunscreen. Ask specifically about transplant or other immunosuppression when considering SCC, and a family history when considering melanoma. UVB accounts for much erythema and burning; longer-wavelength UVA penetrates more deeply and also contributes to photoaging and cancer. A patient who rarely burns may still have important cumulative exposure. In one population of White women, at least five blistering burns between ages 15 and 20 were associated with higher subsequent risks of both keratinocyte carcinoma and melanoma. Adult exposure also contributes, so the interview should extend beyond childhood (p1455–1456).

The distribution of risk affects the examination. A scalp newly exposed by hair loss, the ears, hands and neck may show a broad field of sun damage. In immunosuppressed solid-organ transplant recipients, SCC occurred **65 to 250 times** more often than in the general population in the evidence reviewed. Tumors can be numerous and aggressive. In a transplant recipient with darker skin, inspect covered skin and sites of scarring or chronic inflammation as well. An ulcer that changes within an old burn or a chronic wound deserves direct assessment even if its site has had little sun (p1458–1459).

### What the photographs teach about keratinocyte lesions

Figure 93-1 illustrates a relatively ill-defined, erythematous scalp plaque. Surface scale or roughness, often easier to appreciate by touch than from across the room, is a clue to actinic keratosis. In an area with diffuse photodamage there may be many such lesions, which favors treatment of the whole affected field. An unusually thick or indurated focus is a different problem: an invasive SCC may be hiding underneath. If a focal lesion persists despite otherwise effective field treatment, return to the question of diagnosis before giving another prolonged course (p1456–1457, p1460–1461).

Compare the lower-lid BCC in Figure 93-2 with the chest lesion in Figure 93-3. The first is a small shiny papule with a conspicuous fine surface vessel. The second is flat, pink and slightly scaly at its raised edge. Only one looks like the classic pearly nodule, yet both are BCC. An apparently eczematous area that never resolves deserves consideration for biopsy. In Figure 93-4 the indurated plaque on the nose is ulcerated, and the authors warn of tumor beyond the visible edge. The clinical boundary becomes especially unreliable in morpheaform or infiltrating disease. A narrow area of surface ulceration can therefore conceal a much larger reconstructive problem (p1457–1458).

Figures 93-5 through 93-7 show three routes to the same diagnostic concern. One SCC is an ulcerated cheek nodule with a central keratin core, another is a crusted lesion on the dorsum of a sun-damaged hand, and another is an ulcerated scalp plaque with raised edges. Look for firmness, tenderness, bleeding, ulceration and rapid growth; a chronic wound is not reassurance. Figure 93-8 shows a keratoacanthoma: a flesh-colored nodule with a central crater of keratin. The growth can take only **4–6 weeks**. Some involute, but the course of an individual lesion cannot be predicted and its resemblance to invasive SCC explains why excision is commonly chosen (p1459–1460).

September 2024 Q8 tests the next step when an older adult presents with a new suspicious lesion. Choosing a definitive local treatment from a photograph alone would discard the information that changes management: whether cancer is present, the histologic subtype and the likelihood of occult extension. Arrange referral for a biopsy adequate to establish what the lesion is. Neither the patient's advanced age nor uncertainty about a future reconstruction resolves the diagnostic question. Discuss definitive treatment, including the possibility of observation for a suitable low-risk lesion, after its biology and the person's wishes are clear (p1457–1462, p1471).

### Read the risk table before choosing a local treatment

Table 93-2 applies to both BCC and SCC. Start with recurrence, poorly defined borders, immunosuppression and a site of previous radiation, scarring or chronic inflammation. Perineural or vascular involvement raises concern even if the primary lesion is small. The highest-risk locations include the central facial mask and periorbital region, temples, lips, ears, genitalia, hands and feet. Size depends on the site: the table flags **over 5 mm** in high-risk areas, **over 9 mm** on cheeks, forehead, scalp or neck and **over 19 mm** at low-risk trunk and extremity sites. Hands, feet, ankles and pretibial skin should not be assigned the general low-risk extremity category (p1460).

Histology and behavior refine this estimate. Morpheaform, fibrosing, sclerosing, infiltrating and micronodular BCCs have growth patterns in which visible borders underestimate disease. For SCC the table emphasizes poor differentiation, certain aggressive histologic subtypes, invasion **at least 2 mm** deep or Clark level IV or V, rapid growth and neurologic symptoms. These features change how much reassurance a small clinical diameter can provide. With SCC, examine the regional nodal basin as well as the primary site. BCC rarely metastasizes, but a new primary or a local recurrence is still a reason for ongoing skin surveillance (p1458–1460, p1463).

A single typical AK can be frozen with liquid nitrogen. The book describes freezing for about **10 seconds**, allowing thawing and repeating the cycle. This destroys tissue without supplying histology. Multiple thin AKs in a field make topical therapy more practical. Fluorouracil inhibits thymidylate synthase; imiquimod stimulates an immune response via toll-like receptor 7. Irritation, burning and dryness can interfere with adherence. The **5%** formulations have an additional role in selected biopsy-confirmed superficial low-risk BCCs; the chapter refers readers to cancer-specific labeling for that regimen. A topical treatment that clears AKs is not adequate margin assessment for an invasive eyelid tumor (p1460–1461).

Photodynamic therapy separates drug delivery from activation: apply a photosensitizer such as aminolevulinic acid, allow porphyrins to accumulate, then expose the area to the treatment light. The attraction for someone unable to manage daily creams is a supervised treatment with potentially good cosmesis. The trade-offs are lower clearance than excision in the comparisons described and pain or burning during illumination, especially with extensive scalp or facial treatment. The book also describes methyl aminolevulinate as another photosensitizer and notes that it was no longer available in the United States. Electrodessication and curettage uses a different property: the friable tumor is removed with a curette and the base and periphery are treated. Several passes may be needed. It can suit low-risk disease in a low-risk site, with healing by granulation and sometimes a pale or depressed scar (p1461).

Standard excision provides a specimen for formal margin evaluation. The chapter describes usual lateral margins of **4–10 mm**, depending on risk, and reports five-year freedom from disease of **98%** in low-risk BCC and above **91%** in low-risk SCC. Mohs micrographic surgery maps the tumor and examines the peripheral and deep margins in successive stages. If tumor remains at one mapped edge, only that area is removed again. This tissue conservation is particularly valuable on a nose or eyelid, where an unnecessarily wide defect may compromise function. With extensive disease the major burden may be the repair after clearance, rather than removing the layers. Discuss the anticipated time in the unit, possible flap or graft, wound care and what the patient can manage at home (p1462).

A worked example on p1470 is a **6-mm** ulcerated nodular BCC of the lower nose. The chapter estimates less than **50%** cure with electrodessication and curettage in that setting. Radiation offers approximately **90%** control but involves repeat visits. A one-stage excision can exceed **95%** control with an adequate deep plane and at least a **4-mm** margin, at the price of reconstruction. Mohs is cited at about **98%** for this lesion and may leave a more conservative defect. These are estimates for the example, not interchangeable cure rates for all skin cancers. Fractionated radiation can preserve function when surgery would cause excessive morbidity, but travel, late pigment or skin changes and the quality of local circulation matter in a frail person (p1461–1462, p1470).

### When a biopsy-proven BCC needs systemic assessment

June 2023 Q42 links the histology to the drug class. Vismodegib and sonidegib inhibit the hedgehog pathway in advanced BCC. A lower-eyelid BCC is the keyed case because of the type of tumor and its difficult location. A readily resectable eyelid BCC can still be managed locally. Systemic assessment becomes relevant for metastatic disease or locally advanced BCC for which suitable surgery or radiation is unavailable. The reported vismodegib response rates of approximately **33%** in metastatic and **48%** in locally advanced disease are objective responses in different groups. They are neither complete clearance rates nor outcomes of Mohs surgery (p1460, p1462–1463).

Taste disturbance, muscle spasm and weight loss can make treatment especially burdensome when reserve is limited. Ask how symptoms affect meals, weight, walking and usual activities rather than recording toxicity solely as present or absent. Review concomitant medication, particularly interacting CYP3A inhibitors when considering sonidegib. The population studied does not permit confident estimates for every frail older patient. Follow-up after keratinocyte carcinoma has more than one purpose: look for local recurrence, a new primary and, particularly for SCC, regional lymphadenopathy. The chapter estimates **30%–50%** develop another primary keratinocyte carcinoma within five years. SCC recurrence often occurs during the first two years, whereas BCC recurrence can be later (p1458, p1463).

### Recognizing melanoma across the photographed variants

June 2025 Q29 asks what makes a lesion suspicious for melanoma. In Table 93-3 asymmetry means that folding the lesion along an imaginary center line would leave unlike halves. An irregular, ragged or poorly defined outline, uneven pigment and evolution strengthen concern. Compare the lesion with the patient's own pattern of nevi: a single outlier can matter more than size alone. Figure 93-9 shows the classic combination of asymmetric shape, irregular border, several colors and a diameter exceeding the usual **6-mm** warning value. That threshold has limited sensitivity. Waiting for every small changing lesion to exceed it risks delaying evaluation (p1464).

Figure 93-10 provides the corrective to relying on brown or black pigment: this amelanotic melanoma is a pink nodule on the leg. Figures 93-11 through 93-13 show superficial spreading melanomas. One small early lesion already has an irregular shape and edge, while the following examples have Breslow depths of **3.5 mm** and **1.1 mm** respectively. Breslow thickness is a microscopic measurement of vertical invasion. It is not the width of the surface patch, and comparing two photographs cannot establish which is deeper. A history of change and a full-thickness biopsy resolve different parts of the problem (p1465, p1467).

Figures 93-14 and 93-15 show the slow, flat and irregularly pigmented patches of lentigo maligna on chronically sun-damaged facial skin. One spreads around a lateral canthus onto the cheek; the other had enlarged slowly on the nose over years. Slow enlargement can characterize melanoma in situ at these sites. Figure 93-16 shows the opposite morphology: a large protruding, ulcerated nodular melanoma with **11.5 mm** Breslow thickness. A growing nodule warrants attention even when it lacks the radial shape changes of a superficial spreading lesion. The nodular and lentiginous patterns therefore pose different pitfalls for recognition (p1465–1467).

A complete skin examination extends below the ankles and includes the nails. Figures 93-17 and 93-18 illustrate acral lentiginous melanoma as a dark irregular macule on the sole or lateral foot. A lesion at a pressure-bearing site should not automatically be attributed to friction. In Figure 93-19 the longitudinal nail streak is associated with pigment at the proximal nail fold and dystrophy of the nail plate. The matrix biopsy site is shown. A history confined to sun-exposed skin or a quick look at the upper body could miss all three examples. The physical location also affects the feasibility of an initial complete biopsy and subsequent reconstruction (p1466–1467).

### Stage melanoma with a specimen that retains its depth

When the lesion can be removed completely, the chapter describes narrow full-thickness excision or complete deep saucerization. A superficial shave may transect the base and obscure the true Breslow thickness, compromising subsequent staging. If a large lesion cannot be removed in its entirety initially, obtain a sufficiently deep incisional sample; an incisional biopsy does not itself increase metastatic risk. Orient the excision with future lymphatic mapping in mind. The **2-mm** margin here is a diagnostic biopsy margin. Definitive wide-excision margins are determined after the measured depth is available (p1467).

In situ melanoma remains in the epidermis. Stages I and II describe local disease, with higher-risk features or deeper invasion in stage II, stage III includes regional nodal or skin spread and stage IV indicates distant metastasis. The definitive excision specimen is assessed for clearance and histologic stage. Lentigo maligna can spread farther across facial skin than its visible pigmentation suggests. Even a **5-mm** margin may leave tumor, making staged margin control relevant. When invasion has been excluded, the chapter also discusses imiquimod cream **5 times a week for 12 or more weeks**, radiation or carefully documented observation when limited life expectancy favors it. These options do not substitute for staging an invasive nodule (p1467–1468).

Sentinel node biopsy identifies the first draining node or nodes using tracers injected at the primary site. It is ideally coordinated with wide excision so drainage can be mapped accurately. The book describes considering it above **1 mm** or in selected **0.76–1.0 mm** lesions with adverse features, and discusses regional lymphadenectomy following a positive sentinel biopsy. Wound problems, seroma, hematoma and lymphedema are relevant trade-offs. Nodal staging offers prognostic information and can influence access to adjuvant therapy; fitness for the procedure and the likely use of that information belong in the discussion (p1467–1468).

> Current practice (not the book): Discuss sentinel biopsy for pT1b melanoma, defined by thickness of 0.8–1.0 mm or a thinner ulcerated lesion. ESMO recommends sentinel biopsy for clinically node-negative tumors thicker than 1.0 mm and recommends against completion lymph node dissection after a positive sentinel biopsy. [ESMO melanoma guideline, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.annonc.2024.11.006).

Systemic melanoma therapy depends on tumor biology. Confirm the relevant BRAF mutation before using a BRAF inhibitor such as vemurafenib or dabrafenib. MEK lies downstream, providing the rationale for combining dabrafenib with trametinib. In the age comparison cited, peripheral edema and anorexia were more common in older people on the combination. Checkpoint treatment instead acts on immune regulation; the chapter discusses ipilimumab, nivolumab and pembrolizumab. These are distinct mechanisms from hedgehog inhibition for advanced BCC. Follow-up must also address new primary melanoma and recurrence, including examination of the skin and regional nodes (p1468–1469).

### Merkel cell carcinoma and the cost of delaying biopsy

Figures 93-20 and 93-21 show red or violaceous nodules. The lower-leg lesion in the first photograph is **2.3 cm**. A painless lesion that enlarges rapidly over a few months may initially resemble a cyst, boil or inflamed follicle. Older age and immunosuppression increase concern, and the tumor can spread to nodes when the primary still appears small. Studies cited in the chapter describe at least **15%–20%** occult regional nodal disease even with small tumors. Tissue diagnosis and assessment of extent are therefore more consequential than a further period of empiric treatment for presumed inflammation (p1469–1470).

For Merkel cell carcinoma, distinguish local from regional nodal or distant disease when discussing prognosis. The chapter describes wide local excision and radiation to the tumor bed or involved nodes; narrow excision with radiation or radiation alone may suit some patients. Distant disease may call for systemic treatment, including checkpoint inhibition. For an asymptomatic superficial low-risk BCC near the end of life, time until benefit may support observation. A bleeding or painful lesion can instead justify palliative surgery even when survival is limited. The decision follows the specific threat posed by the lesion, the patient's function and priorities, and the practical burden of the treatment (p1470–1471).

> Current practice (not the book): For unresectable or metastatic Merkel cell carcinoma, PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is preferred when appropriate. Responses to cytotoxic chemotherapy can be brief, and treatment fitness and immune contraindications influence selection. [ESMO–EURACAN Merkel cell carcinoma guideline, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11145756/).

## Exam traps

- The diameter clue is neither a biopsy threshold nor a reliable exclusion criterion. Assess asymmetry, contrast with other nevi and change over time (p1464).
- The lower-eyelid BCC that points toward hedgehog inhibition in the exam still needs assessment of operability and radiation options (p1460–1463).
- Do not confuse the narrow initial diagnostic margin for suspected melanoma with the definitive excision margin determined by Breslow depth (p1467).
- A solitary red nodule can represent amelanotic melanoma or Merkel cell carcinoma, and a black patch on a sole can be acral melanoma (p1465–1470).
- Basal cell carcinoma rarely metastasizes but can cause local morbidity; morpheaform lesions may extend beyond visible borders and recur (p1458).
- Near the end of life, active nonintervention may be reasonable for asymptomatic low-risk disease; palliative surgery may still help painful, bleeding or malodorous lesions (p1471).

## Recall drill

**1. When does a presumed actinic keratosis require biopsy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When it is thick or indurated, remains suspicious or fails appropriate treatment. The concern is underlying keratinocyte carcinoma. (p1457)</details>

**2. What is the usual clinical appearance of nodular BCC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A shiny or pearly papule or nodule, often with branching surface vessels and a rolled border. (p1457)</details>

**3. Why reassess a persistent eczema-like plaque?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Superficial BCC can resemble an inflammatory rash and may be overlooked if treatment is repeatedly continued without reassessment. (p1458)</details>

**4. Which appearance should raise concern for cutaneous SCC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A firm hyperkeratotic pink or red papule, nodule or plaque, sometimes tender, bleeding or ulcerated. (p1459–1460)</details>

**5. Why might a crateriform lesion that grows over weeks be excised?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Keratoacanthomas may regress, but their behavior is difficult to predict and they resemble other SCCs. (p1459)</details>

**6. What makes an eyelid BCC or a recurrent BCC clinically important?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Both are higher-risk features for subclinical extension or recurrence and affect selection of a margin-controlled treatment. (p1460–1462)</details>

**7. When is systemic vismodegib relevant?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>For metastatic or locally advanced inoperable BCC when usual local approaches are unsuitable. Site and recurrence prompt careful assessment of these conditions. (p1462)</details>

**8. What toxicities can limit hedgehog inhibitors?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Muscle spasms, dysgeusia and weight loss are important examples. Monitor functional and nutritional consequences. (p1463)</details>

**9. What do the topical fluorouracil and imiquimod regimens on p1461 treat?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>They describe actinic keratosis. Selected biopsy-confirmed low-risk superficial BCC can also be treated topically, with a different regimen. (p1461)</details>

**10. What does melanoma asymmetry mean at the bedside?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The two halves are not mirror images. Examine border irregularity, variation in color and change over time as well. (p1464)</details>

**11. Can a small changing pigmented lesion be melanoma?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Diameter alone is insensitive. Evolution, darkness and difference from the patient's other nevi may identify a concerning lesion. (p1464)</details>

**12. Why avoid a superficial sampling shave in suspected melanoma?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It may transect the lesion and prevent accurate determination of Breslow depth. The book describes complete full-thickness excision or deep saucerization. (p1467)</details>

**13. Why coordinate sentinel-node biopsy with definitive excision when indicated?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Node assessment provides staging information and is ideally planned with definitive surgery to preserve mapping of the primary lesion's drainage. (p1467–1468)</details>

**14. What distinguishes invasive SCC from BCC in metastatic behavior?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Regional nodal metastasis is more common with SCC. BCC can still cause major local damage despite rarely metastasizing. (p1458, p1460–1462)</details>

**15. When might palliative surgery be worthwhile near the end of life?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When a painful, bleeding or malodorous lesion substantially affects comfort, even if observation would be appropriate for asymptomatic low-risk disease. (p1471)</details>
