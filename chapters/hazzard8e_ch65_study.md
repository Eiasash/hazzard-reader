# Major Depression

Interpret symptoms and impairment together. A screening score prompts assessment of severity, safety and the treatment the person can realistically sustain.

## Symptoms, screening and diagnosis

### Reading a PHQ-9 when the score and the story differ

The PHQ-9 asks about nine symptoms over two weeks. For severity, add the frequency points for all nine responses (0–3 each). For the provisional diagnosis in <a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-2">Figure 65-2</a>, count questions 1–8 only when present on more than half the days or nearly every day. Count any endorsement of item 9 and ask about safety directly. A report of marked impairment on the separate functional question warrants investigation even when few symptoms meet the frequency threshold (<a href="?chapter=65#p1009">p1009</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1013">1013</a>).

**Social isolation** (objective — network size, contact frequency) vs **loneliness** (subjective — perceived inadequacy of connection) are distinct but closely related constructs; loneliness is fundamentally related to depression, whereas some isolation (living alone, selectivity) may reflect capability and preference and need not signify a problem (<a href="?chapter=65#p1009">p1009</a>).

Ask which core symptom qualifies. Five or more qualifying symptoms including low mood or loss of interest, with impaired function, support evaluation for a major episode. The minor-depression row uses two to four qualifying symptoms with a core symptom and impairment. The chronic-depression row specifically requires low mood (item 2), a two-year history and impairment. Severity scores and diagnostic counts serve different purposes. A total of 10 or more generally warrants assessment for major depression, and following the score over visits can reveal which symptoms have improved (<a href="?chapter=65#p1010">p1010</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">1012</a>).

PHQ-9 scoring: **0–4** = no/insignificant symptoms, **5–9** = mild/subsyndromal depression, **≥10** = typically correlates with major depression and warrants further evaluation and clinical intervention (<a href="?chapter=65#p1009">p1009</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>).

<a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-2">Figure 65-2</a> combines symptom count, depressed mood or loss of interest, duration and functional impairment to guide diagnostic assessment. Its chronic-depression row specifies mood symptoms for two years. For standardized **PHQ-9 severity**, 10–14 is moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, and 20–27 severe (<a href="?chapter=65#p1011">p1011</a>, Kroenke et al.). The score supports evaluation and does not independently diagnose major depression.

Item 9 asks about death wishes or more intensive suicidal ideation — clearly an important part of the assessment (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>).

In May 2024 Q16, reduced interest, fatigue and appetite change each occurred on several days. Total 3, zero qualifying symptoms, daily tasks very difficult. In June 2025 Q57, interest, mood, energy and appetite each scored one. Total 4, again zero qualifying symptoms and marked interference. Both exams credited minor depression as well as a response compatible with the low count. In clinic, explore impaired function, sleep, illness, medication and distress, and arrange follow-up. Neither image meets the printed minor-depression algorithm (<a href="?chapter=65#p1011">p1011</a> <a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-2">Figure 65-2</a>).

### Mild symptoms, cognitive complaints and major depression

Ask what the person has stopped enjoying and why. Apathy, pain, illness and isolation can all reduce activity. Loss of pleasure and hopelessness can interfere with eating, obtaining essentials and sustaining relationships. Compare mood, cognition and function with the prior baseline and listen to the patient as well as the family. In June 2022 Q19, new concentration and recall problems after bereavement and a change from MMSE 24 to 28 are compatible with a depressive contribution. Neither improvement nor a single test identifies the medication taken or excludes later cognitive disease (<a href="?chapter=65#p1008">p1008</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">1012</a>).

Two core distinguishing features of geriatric depression: **anhedonia** and **hopelessness** — they may be important clues when deficits in basic needs (Maslow's hierarchy, <a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-1">Fig 65-1</a>) are more apparent than depressed mood (<a href="?chapter=65#p1008">p1008</a>).

In one presentation, crying followed a loss of family authority, while enjoyment of grandchildren and the day center continued. A PHQ-9 of 5, no reported suicidality and a preference against medication favor a conversation about her role, activities she values, and planned follow-up. Distress deserves help even when it falls below a major episode. In dementia, combine a careful review of medical contributors with caregiver support and realistic activities. Complex depression with cognitive impairment is a reason to involve geriatric mental health expertise (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1014">1014</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">1015</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1018">1018</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1019">1019</a>).

Two weeks of yearning after a spouse’s death can occur in acute grief. Ask whether self-worth and the capacity to enjoy contact are preserved or whether worthlessness, pervasive anhedonia, hopelessness or suicidal thoughts suggest an additional depressive episode. Female sex is associated with vulnerability to prolonged grief. It does not ask for a diagnosis after two weeks. In adults DSM-5-TR requires at least 12 months after the death for prolonged grief disorder (ch66 <a href="?chapter=66#p1024">p1024</a>; American Psychiatric Association, [Prolonged Grief Disorder](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/prolonged-grief-disorder/)).

**~5%** of older adults meet criteria for major depression — lower than in other age groups; new-onset depression peaks in middle age; among older adults, greater age brings a similar likelihood of becoming depressed but a **higher likelihood of remaining depressed** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1007">p1007</a>).

Depression is **not** a normal part of aging; normalizing it in older patients is misguided and unproductive (<a href="?chapter=65#p1007">p1007</a>).

**Pitfall.** The PHQ-9 diagnostic table's dysthymia row requires Q2 (depressed mood) specifically, sustained **2 years** — not just any 3–4 symptoms (<a href="?chapter=65#p1011">p1011</a>).

## Treatment selection and response

Begin with the indication. People with major depression may choose a drug, structured psychotherapy or another suitable intervention. Trials in mild or subsyndromal depression show little evidence of a drug benefit. In depression with dementia, check for a measurable improvement before adding to long-term polypharmacy. Placebo-group improvement can be considerable, especially in less severe episodes. Improvement after a prescription can reflect time, clinical contact, expectation and a drug effect. The book cites about six to eight weeks for a measurable antidepressant effect. Continue to check symptoms and safety during that interval (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1014">1014</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">1015</a>).

Even in "gold standard" collaborative care: **<50%** of patients achieve a depression response (≥50% symptom reduction) at 6 months, **<1/3** achieve remission — **two-thirds remain effectively depressed**; between-clinic response rates ranged from **<10%** to **>80%** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>).

Structured psychotherapy trials run **~3 months**; if no improvement by **1–2 months**, consider switching modality (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

Antidepressants do **not** show benefit for minor/subsyndromal depression despite it having similar health/QOL impact to major depression — don't reflexively medicate mild cases (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

Review adherence, time on treatment, adverse effects, interacting medicines and the diagnosis before calling a drug ineffective. Sertraline 50 mg had been tolerated for months without benefit. A gradual trial toward 100 mg with reassessment was reasonable. Two weeks at escitalopram 5 mg had produced some improvement. If tolerated, a move to 10 mg allows a fuller trial before adding a second medicine. Neither dose is a universal therapeutic threshold. A lower initial dose and planned reviews can improve adherence even if the eventual dose is the same. Ask explicitly about sexual effects because patients may otherwise stop treatment without explaining why (<a href="?chapter=60#p929">p929</a> <a href="?chapter=60#book-table-60-9">Table 60-9</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">1015</a>).

Slow up-titration gave much better adherence than a higher starting dose, even though both reached the same eventual dose; separately, dose-response research does not support a **minimum effective dosage** or narrow therapeutic window (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

Patients often don't volunteer side effects (especially sexual dysfunction) unless asked directly (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

June 2026 Q87 concerns [depression in later life](?chapter=65#cited-chapter). The printed <a href="?chapter=7#p114">pages 114</a>–<a href="?chapter=7#p115">115</a> concern a different chapter. The [placebo discussion on p1014](?chapter=65#exam-p1014-mcq-d06be14df3b5c357b755243d) and the [individualized-dose paragraph on p1015](?chapter=65#exam-p1015-mcq-d06be14df3b5c357b755243d) supply the depression teaching. Benefit in dementia is uncertain, and response within placebo groups can be substantial (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">1015</a>).

Antidepressants do **not generally work in dementia**-associated depression; favor behavioral/caregiver-support interventions instead (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

In some nursing home populations, **almost half** of residents are prescribed an antidepressant — far exceeding actual depression prevalence estimates (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

**Pitfall.** Subsyndromal depression has real health/QOL impact but antidepressants show no proven benefit for it — don't assume "some symptoms = start a drug" (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

**Pitfall.** No trial has found medications more effective than ECT, and older patients seem even more responsive to it than younger adults — contrary to its negative-press reputation (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

## Drug effects and discontinuation

Match the adverse effect to the agent. Paroxetine adds anticholinergic burden in someone with memory impairment, delirium risk or impaired bladder emptying. Citalopram has less anticholinergic activity but can prolong QT in a dose-related fashion. In a patient taking citalopram, the ECG shows wide-complex tachycardia with changing amplitude, making citalopram the important association. Both citalopram and escitalopram can cause SIADH. Tramadol plus venlafaxine raises concern for serotonin toxicity because both enhance serotonergic signaling. Both delirium with paroxetine and serotonin toxicity with citalopram are possible. Its published comparison is [chapter 60, Table 60-9](?chapter=60#exam-p929-mcq-4641bad3c4f4169ea022ed51), alongside the Beers Criteria (<a href="?chapter=60#p929">p929</a>; FDA prescribing information, citalopram and venlafaxine).

Ask what will happen if the drug helps, and when it should be reviewed if it does not. Repeated episodes or clear recurrence following prior discontinuation favor longer treatment. Review benefit, falls, hyponatremia and other adverse effects even after years of stability. Taper in steps when discontinuation is appropriate and assess symptoms as the dose falls. Vivid dreams, insomnia, vertigo, gastrointestinal upset or sensory disturbances emerging after a reduction suggest withdrawal. Compare their timing and character with the previous depressive episode before deciding to restart long-term therapy (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">1016</a>).

DSM-5 defines major depression as an "episode" — expected to improve over time even without treatment, which complicates decisions about indefinite maintenance therapy (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>).

Hazzard gives **2–4 weeks** as a rough taper for most SSRIs, SNRIs and mirtazapine. It gives **several months** for tricyclics (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>).

Hazzard states that **fluoxetine does not require tapering** because of its long half-life. If withdrawal symptoms occur despite a long taper of an antidepressant, the chapter suggests considering **a single 20 mg dose of fluoxetine** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>).

> **Current practice: Individualized antidepressant taper.** reduce doses in stages, tailoring the pace to the medicine and symptoms. For fluoxetine 20 mg/day, alternate-day dosing can be a step down. At 40–60 mg/day, withdraw gradually — [NICE NG222, recommendations 1.4.16–1.4.18, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng222/chapter/Recommendations#stopping-antidepressant-medication).

Discontinuation symptoms (sweating, chills, dizziness, **flu-like symptoms**, GI upset, **insomnia**, vivid and excessive dreaming, **depressed mood**, vertigo, numbness, shock-like sensations) can be confused with relapse/lack of efficacy — misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary tests, useless treatments, and increased costs (<a href="?chapter=65#p1015">p1015</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">1016</a>).

**Pitfall.** Fluoxetine is the one antidepressant that breaks the "always taper slowly" rule (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>).

## Suicide risk, support and referral

### ECT counseling and continuation treatment

For a severely depressed patient losing weight, becoming psychotic or failing adequate medication trials, discuss ECT early. Hazzard gives an average initial course of 3–6 treatments per week for 2–4 weeks (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>). Response means substantial clinical improvement, whereas remission means few or no remaining symptoms. In most studies summarized here, over 80% responded and over 60% remitted. Older adults often responded particularly well. Assess medical and anesthetic suitability. Hazzard says short-term memory loss almost always resolves within a few weeks after the course (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

> **Current practice: ECT frequency and duration.** ECT is commonly given **2–3 times weekly**, often for **6–12 treatments**. Some patients experience longer-lasting gaps in autobiographical memory — [American Psychiatric Association, What Is Electroconvulsive Therapy?](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/ect/).

ECT should be considered early for severe or psychotic depression, or after limited medication response (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

Plan beyond the acute response because benefit can diminish, even during medication treatment. Follow symptoms and discuss continuation medication or maintenance ECT where appropriate. For patients who prefer psychotherapy, offer an accessible structured intervention with explicit goals. The chapter describes cognitive behavioral, interpersonal and group approaches, including reminiscence. Reconsider the chosen modality if there is no improvement after roughly one to two months (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

### Suicide risk and immediate protection

Ask a caregiver with insomnia about exhaustion and suicidal thinking. <a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-3">Figure 65-3</a> lists insomnia and caregiving stress among the possible risks. It also lists reasons for living, supports and responsibility to others as possible protective factors. Ask what caring for her spouse means to her, how she sleeps and who can give her respite. A protective factor does not cancel an expression of suicidal intent. Clinical context appears on <a href="?chapter=65#p1017">p1017</a> and risk assessment on <a href="?chapter=65#p1018">p1018</a>; the two examined factors appear together on the <a href="?chapter=65#p1013">p1013</a> checklist (<a href="?chapter=65#p1013">p1013</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1017">1017</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1018">1018</a>).

A suicidal patient introduces medicolegal concerns — protecting the patient's safety and ensuring the provider **documents fully** (**<a href="?chapter=65#book-table-65-1">Table 65-1</a>**, <a href="?chapter=65#p1017">p1017</a>).

Ask directly about thoughts, intent, a plan, prior behavior and access to means. A wish to die may express unbearable pain, hopelessness, a desire for contact or imminent intent. Take the statement seriously and explore it during this encounter. Seek collateral information, reduce access to lethal means with appropriate support and arrange emergency evaluation for imminent danger. Treat pain and isolation at the same time. In an older person with cancer, more severe pain and depressive symptoms can coexist. A discussion of care goals and symptom relief can proceed alongside the safety assessment (<a href="?chapter=65#p1013">p1013</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1017">1017</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1018">1018</a>).

Suicide/safety checklist (<a href="?chapter=65#book-figure-65-3">Fig 65-3</a>) is triggered by **PHQ Q9 ≥1**, **MADRS Q10 ≥3**, or **QIDS-SR Q12 ≥1** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1013">p1013</a>).

Structured assessment: suicidal thoughts, intent, plan, behavior; risk factors (hopelessness, impulsivity, isolation, prior attempts, recent psychiatric discharge, access to means) vs protective factors (reasons for living, supports); document firearm status and an action plan (<a href="?chapter=65#p1013">p1013</a>).

Assess whether a plan is formulated/rehearsed and whether means (especially firearms) are accessible — removing access lowers immediate risk (<a href="?chapter=65#p1018">p1018</a>).

Simple human presence (sitting down, eye contact, asking "what else is going on?") is probably the most important way to ensure older patients can express themselves (<a href="?chapter=65#p1017">p1017</a>).

### Social context, longitudinal care and referral

Depression is less prevalent in later life than in some younger age groups, yet an older person who becomes depressed may remain so longer. A loss of role, function or connection can be clinically important without meeting criteria for major depression. Ask about basic needs, security, belonging and what still feels worthwhile. Depression and disability can reinforce one another when poor motivation makes self-care harder and worsening illness restricts participation (<a href="?chapter=65#p1007">p1007</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1009">1009</a>).

Living alone describes a social arrangement. Loneliness describes how well a person’s relationships meet their needs. Someone may choose solitude and feel connected, or be lonely despite frequent contact. One practical approach is Act–Belong–Commit: identify something the patient can enjoy, someone to do it with and a meaningful role worth sustaining. Physical activity is associated with lower onset or recurrence, and a realistic activity plan can support health alongside depression treatment (<a href="?chapter=65#p1009">p1009</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1014">1014</a>).

Collaborative care uses a designated care manager for contact and symptom tracking, a prescribing primary clinician and psychiatric consultation. Response and remission remain distinct: in real-world collaborative care, fewer than half achieved a 50% symptom reduction at six months and fewer than one-third remitted. Selected trial participants, intensive check-ins and short follow-up can make research outcomes look better than routine practice. Agree on what improvement should look like and make time to review it (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>, <a href="?chapter=65#p1014">1014</a>).

During follow-up, ask about stressors and arrange practical help, respite or a call between visits. Clinicians may feel drained when responses are slow or the family’s concerns seem endless. Reflecting with a colleague can preserve the capacity to listen. In long-term care, offer choices, reduce unwanted noise, identify valued activities and consider reminiscence work before escalating drug treatment. Palliative discussions can address pain, social and spiritual support, and what quality of life means throughout serious illness. Worsening symptoms, psychosis, bipolar features, complex drug combinations or consideration of ECT justify psychiatric referral (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1019">1019</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=65#book-table-65-1">Table 65-1</a>** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1017">p1017</a>) catalogs challenges clinicians face treating depressed older patients — emotional toll (feeling drained, like a failure), practical friction (patients' "pulling teeth" pace, bottomless needs, family members wanting to explain at length what they've observed), and slow response (weeks to months) — the chapter frames "caring for the caregiver" (including the clinician) as part of good care (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>).

Medically complex, cognitively impaired, or bipolar/psychotic-feature depression is best suited for geriatric psychiatry subspecialty referral; most general psychiatrists should feel comfortable evaluating and treating older adults (<a href="?chapter=65#p1018">p1018</a>–<a href="?chapter=65#p1019">1019</a>).

## Key numbers

- In primary care, **~5%** of older adults have clinically significant depression; about half are recognized/diagnosed; of those, only **1 in 5** get guideline-concordant care — so only **~10%** of depressed older adults get "adequate" care (<a href="?chapter=65#p1012">p1012</a>).

- Largest geriatric depression treatment trial: **51%** of patients preferred counseling/psychotherapy vs **38%** preferring antidepressants (<a href="?chapter=65#p1014">p1014</a>).

<details class="study-self-check" data-check-key="65s:recall-13"><summary>How should antidepressant tapering be individualized, and how does fluoxetine&#x27;s long half-life affect dose reduction?</summary>

Hazzard gives **2–4 weeks** for most SSRIs, SNRIs and mirtazapine and **several months** for tricyclics. It states that **fluoxetine does not require tapering**. For symptoms despite a long taper, the chapter suggests considering **one dose of fluoxetine 20 mg** (<a href="?chapter=65#p1016">p1016</a>).
