# Chapter 34 Study: Hearing Loss: Assessment and Management

## Summary

**Definition & epidemiology**
- Presbycusis is heterogeneous age-related peripheral and central auditory dysfunction; it usually begins at high frequencies and progresses into the speech range (p489–490).
- Significant loss affects approximately **44%** in their sixties, **66%** at **70–79**, and **90%** after **80** in the chapter's cited estimates (p490).
- Genetic susceptibility interacts with noise, ototoxic drugs, smoking and comorbidity; diabetes and vascular disease can promote cochlear injury (p489–491).

**Assessment**
- Ask about speech understanding, especially in noise, rather than only whether sounds are audible. A hearing-handicap questionnaire can identify everyday effects (p489, p493, p500).
- Pure-tone audiometry, tympanometry, acoustic reflexes and word-recognition testing characterize different aspects of impairment (p489).
- One cited professional definition uses threshold loss of at least **25 dB** at any frequency between **250 and 8000 Hz**; functional impact also depends on frequency pattern (p493).
- Sensorineural loss often begins with high-frequency hair-cell damage; a flat audiogram can reflect metabolic/supporting-structure dysfunction (p495).

**Management**
- Tailor digital hearing aids to the audiogram; they can improve hearing and speech discrimination but do not regenerate lost hair cells (p499).
- Address acceptance and continued use, not just device provision; many eligible older people never try an aid or stop using it (p499).
- When aids cannot provide sufficient correction, consider cochlear implantation. The chapter cites benefit when sentence scores in the poorer ear are below **60%** (p499).
- Hearing aids amplify sound using remaining sensory cells; implants bypass those cells to stimulate the auditory nerve electrically (p499).

**Prevention, drugs & exam traps**
- Reduce noise, ototoxin and smoking exposure; exercise and healthy diet may help, but the degree to which specific interventions delay presbycusis remains uncertain (p498).
- Antioxidant results are inconsistent; a small low-frequency benefit from folate in a Dutch study cannot be generalized to restoration of high-frequency hearing (p499).
- “I hear but cannot understand” may reflect central auditory dysfunction that amplification alone cannot resolve (p499–500).
