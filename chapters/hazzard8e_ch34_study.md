## Family history and assessment

<span id="ch34-study-genetics" data-exam-ids="mcq-db9b505b636e803c1bb580ce" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>The Framingham comparison found that blood relatives had more similar hearing thresholds than spouses. Key Clinical Point 2 gives **over 55%** (p489): it describes the proportion of variation in the study population attributable to genetic differences, and cannot count how many individual cases are inherited. A patient can inherit susceptibility and also sustain cochlear injury from diabetes, vascular disease, noise or ototoxic medication. Men generally develop deficits earlier, and sex differences narrow later in life (pp489–491).

Ask whether conversation becomes unintelligible in company and whether the patient avoids social situations. The short Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly measures perceived functional difficulty. A typical gradual bilateral history supports a presumptive clinical diagnosis after checking for other causes. A bedside whisper test has variable sensitivity. Otoscopy can reveal obstructing wax, and audiometry measures each ear’s thresholds and helps distinguish sensorineural and conductive causes. Both B and D received credit in September 2024; a clinic screen alone cannot confirm the type or degree of loss (p493).

> Current practice (not the book): Sudden unilateral hearing loss warrants urgent assessment. [Lin, NEJM 2024, pp1506–1507](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp2306778).

## Recognizing speech in noise

<span id="ch34-study-speech" data-exam-ids="mcq-ca570710752bad38efb708ba" data-exam-read-page="495" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>A voice can be loud enough to hear while its words are indistinct. Reduced sensitivity at high frequencies obscures consonants that distinguish one word from another. In June 2025, **ספר (sefer)** contains both /s/ and /f/. Table 34-2 places /sh/ in **שלום (shalom)** at **1000–2000 Hz**, whereas /f/, /k/, /s/, /t/ and /th/ are **3000–8000 Hz** (p494). Vowels and context can carry a quiet conversation until competing voices mask those remaining cues (pp494–495, Table 34-2).

The audiogram in Figure 34-5A slopes downward in both ears as pitch rises. In panel B a flatter pattern spans low and high frequencies; panel C shows a unilateral notch typical of noise exposure. At the same pure-tone threshold, two people can have different speech-in-noise performance. The QuickSIN test measures the signal advantage a listener needs over competing noise. Slower delivery and a quieter room can immediately improve communication (pp495–497).

## Hearing aids and referral for an implant

<span id="ch34-study-implant" data-practice-ids="mcq-4c92ab23f4ca6e471480cf69" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**June 2026 Q39.** PTA4 averages thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz in one ear. A better-ear average of 70 dB falls in the severe range. Speech remains hard to understand even in quiet with properly fitted aids. Refer to a cochlear implant centre for aided speech testing and an individual assessment. Hazzard discusses a different measure, sentence recognition below 60% in the poorer ear. Decibels quantify the sound level needed to detect tones; percent correct measures speech recognition. [Hazzard pp499–500](?chapter=34#p499).

> Current practice (not the book): Lin describes PTA4 generally at least 60 dB in one or both ears with insufficient functional benefit from hearing aids as a reason to consider implant referral. [NEJM, 2024, pp1509–1511](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp2306778).

A digital hearing aid supplies frequency-specific acoustic gain to a functioning cochlea. Figure 34-6 shows improved thresholds through about 3000 Hz in one listener, with limited recovery at the highest frequencies. A cochlear implant encodes sound as electrical stimulation of the auditory nerve. Understanding speech after implantation takes practice; benefit varies with neural function and other factors, and chronological age alone does not rule out benefit (pp499–500).

## Inner ear, brain and cognition

The basal cochlea encodes high frequencies and the apex low frequencies. Outer hair cells amplify and sharpen mechanical responses; inner hair cells convert movement into signals for auditory nerve fibres. The stria vascularis maintains the ionic gradient that powers transduction. Damage to basal hair cells can produce a sloping audiogram, whereas loss of this gradient can raise thresholds across frequencies. Synaptic or neural loss can impair discrimination even with relatively preserved tone detection. Several lesions can coexist (pp492–497, Figures 34-1 to 34-5).

Hearing loss is associated with faster cognitive decline. Listening effort, social isolation and common pathology are plausible explanations. The chapter describes observational data (p500).

> Current practice (not the book): The later ACHIEVE trial found no difference in three-year cognitive change across its full cohort, with slower decline in a prespecified comparison of higher-risk participants. Offer rehabilitation for communication and participation; the evidence does not promise prevention of dementia for each patient ([ACHIEVE trial, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)01406-X); Lin, NEJM 2024, p1511).

## Prevention and emerging treatments

Permanent hair-cell loss limits recovery. In mice, apparently transient noise damage can leave lasting synaptic injury, and different strains lose hearing at different ages. Oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury and vascular changes are candidate mechanisms. Their importance differs between animal models and human ageing. Reducing avoidable noise and ototoxic exposures is practical; vitamin supplementation and hair-cell regeneration have no established role in routine treatment of presbycusis. Experimental nerve or brainstem stimulation and regeneration are discussed separately from everyday rehabilitation (pp491, 497–501).

## Summary

**Definition & epidemiology**
- Presbycusis is heterogeneous age-related peripheral and central auditory dysfunction; it usually begins at high frequencies and progresses into the speech range (p489–490).
- Significant loss affects approximately **44%** in their sixties, **66%** at **70–79**, and **90%** after **80** in the chapter's cited estimates (p490).
- Genetic susceptibility interacts with noise, ototoxic drugs, smoking and comorbidity; diabetes and vascular disease can promote cochlear injury (p489–491).

**Assessment**
- Ask about speech understanding, especially in noise, rather than only whether sounds are audible. A hearing-handicap questionnaire can identify everyday effects (p489, p493, p500).
- Pure-tone audiometry, tympanometry, acoustic reflexes and word-recognition testing characterize different aspects of impairment (p489).
- One cited professional definition uses threshold loss of at least **25 dB** at any frequency between **250 and 8000 Hz**; functional impact also depends on frequency pattern (p493).
- Sensorineural loss often begins with high-frequency hair-cell damage; a flat audiogram can reflect metabolic/supporting-structure dysfunction (p495).

**Management**
- Tailor digital hearing aids to the audiogram; they can improve hearing and speech discrimination but do not regenerate lost hair cells (p499).
- Address acceptance and continued use, not just device provision; many eligible older people never try an aid or stop using it (p499).
- When aids cannot provide sufficient correction, consider cochlear implantation. The chapter cites benefit when sentence scores in the poorer ear are below **60%** (p499).
- Hearing aids amplify sound using remaining sensory cells; implants bypass those cells to stimulate the auditory nerve electrically (p499).

### Reading an audiogram alongside the history

The horizontal axis moves from low to high frequencies. The vertical axis runs downward as more intense sound is needed for detection. In the book's bilateral sloping example, low-frequency tones remain audible and higher-frequency thresholds are poor. A person can follow a voice in a quiet room yet miss the consonants that distinguish similar words in a group. A flat loss suggests a different pattern; a unilateral notch calls for an exposure history rather than attribution to age alone (p494–495).

Pure-tone detection and word recognition answer different questions. Tympanometry and acoustic reflexes assess middle-ear function. Speech-in-noise assessment challenges auditory and cognitive processing under competing signals. Ask what listening situations the person wants to recover before choosing and fitting a device; then assess how it works in those situations (p493–497, p499–500).

### From a pressure wave to a conversation

Figures 34-1 through 34-3 follow sound from the cochlear fluid to the auditory nerve. The eardrum and ossicles transmit vibration to the cochlea, producing a traveling wave and motion of the basilar membrane. In Figure 34-1, scala vestibuli and scala tympani contain perilymph; scala media contains endolymph. The stria vascularis and spiral ligament along the lateral wall maintain the ionic conditions that allow sound to be converted into a signal. The figure describes an endolymphatic potential of about **+70 to +100 mV**. A vascular or metabolic injury may therefore impair auditory sensitivity even when surviving hair cells still look relatively intact (p491–493).

Figure 34-2 distinguishes the one row of inner hair cells from the three rows of outer hair cells. Inner hair cells perform most of the sensory transduction communicated to afferent spiral ganglion neurons. Outer hair cells amplify the mechanical response and receive predominantly efferent innervation. In the surface view in Figure 34-3 the inner bundles are linear, whereas the outer bundles form a W-like pattern. Relative motion of basilar and tectorial membranes bends the stereocilia, opens transduction channels and allows potassium influx from the endolymph. The resulting receptor potential triggers glutamate release onto the auditory nerve. Mechanical amplification, transduction and neural signaling are therefore three places a sound may lose information (p492–493).

Figure 34-4 compares surface preparations from mice aged **3** and **23 months**. Orderly rows of outer hair cells in the younger animal become irregular, with gaps in the older animal. In mammals, lost sensory cells ordinarily leave scars rather than functional replacements. Loss concentrated in the basal cochlea disproportionately affects high frequencies. The photograph demonstrates a structural change in an animal preparation. It cannot identify which cellular lesion is responsible for an individual older patient's audiogram or establish a human age equivalent for the animal findings (p493–495).

### Read sound level and frequency separately

Tables 34-1 and 34-2 answer different questions. Sound level in decibels describes how intense a stimulus is. Frequency in hertz describes its rate of vibration and relates to pitch. A whisper is roughly **35 dB**, and ordinary conversation about **50–60 dB** in Table 34-1. Increasing the level of a voice does not restore the consonant information if the listener's high-frequency processing remains poor. From the same table, a vacuum cleaner produces about **60–70 dB** while a lawn mower or chain saw is around **105–110 dB**. Occupational or recreational noise exposure depends on duration as well as peak intensity; the table contrasts **85 dB for an 8-hour workday** with **100 dB for 15 minutes** (p494).

Table 34-2 places spoken vowels around **500–750 Hz**, the /sh/ group at **1000–2000 Hz**, and /f/, /k/, /s/, /t/ and /th/ at **3000–8000 Hz**. Fundamental frequencies and their overtones together give speech and music their distinctive character. A listener with high-frequency loss can hear syllable timing and much of a speaker's voice through lower-frequency cues yet confuse words with similar vowels. In the Hebrew exam example, the two high-frequency consonants in the keyed word make it particularly vulnerable. The competing sounds in a busy room can remove contextual cues that made the same word intelligible one to one. Clarify the word or phrase in a quieter setting and face the listener so facial information can supplement the acoustic signal (p494–495).

In Figure 34-5A an audiogram from a man aged **62** slopes downward in both ears. The downward direction means a more intense stimulus was required for detection. Responses around 250–500 Hz are comparatively preserved; the high-frequency thresholds reach levels at which ordinary conversation cannot supply the necessary cues. Panel B, from a man aged **64**, is much flatter: thresholds are elevated over a broad range. The book associates this pattern with loss of metabolic support, including the endocochlear potential. Panel C, from a man aged **32**, shows an approximately **45-dB** notch at **4 kHz** on the left, while the right is near normal. The focal asymmetry suggests noise exposure such as target shooting. These are worked patterns, not a rule that every sloping loss is caused exclusively by hair-cell loss or every flat loss by strial disease (p495–496).

### Choosing tests for the complaint

Start with the patient's actual communication difficulty. Someone may report that others mumble, respond to the wrong word at dinner, or stop attending gatherings long before saying that sounds are too quiet. Ask about time course, differences between ears, noise and drug exposures, and effects on everyday life. Examine the canal and tympanic membrane. Wax or a middle-ear problem can produce a conductive component, while gradual bilateral impairment is more compatible with age-related sensorineural loss. The family history and a screening inventory can uncover a problem; neither finding localizes the lesion. The book notes that even a result described as normal for age may leave substantial disability that merits rehabilitation (p489–493, p495).

Use the test that addresses the patient's difficulty. Pure-tone testing measures the least intense sound detectable at selected frequencies in each ear. Tympanometry and reflex testing address middle-ear function and reflex pathways. Otoacoustic emissions give information about outer hair cells, whereas an auditory brainstem response assesses neural transmission. Word-recognition testing adds the demand to identify a spoken signal, and a speech-in-noise task measures performance against competing sound. These tests can diverge without any inconsistency in the patient's account. A person who hears a pure tone reliably may still require a much larger speech-to-noise advantage to extract words (p493, p495–497).

Central processing adds a time dimension. Following rapid or compressed speech requires the listener to separate brief sounds, connect them to words and retain enough context to interpret the next phrase. The chapter links performance on some complex listening tasks with working memory. Slowing delivery and reducing noise can improve immediate communication without reversing a cochlear lesion. When evaluating possible cognitive impairment, make sure the instructions were heard; otherwise the assessment may partly measure access to the test. A hearing-adapted cognitive assessment can help when standard spoken instructions disadvantage the patient (p496–497).

### Risks, models and the limits of prevention evidence

Heritability estimates concern variation within the population examined. The Framingham comparison of relatives with spouses supports a genetic contribution but does not assign a percentage of one patient's hearing loss to genes. Exposure to noise and ototoxic agents accumulates across the same years. Diabetes can affect cochlear vascular supply and central auditory processing; cardiovascular disease may compromise the highly vascular stria. The energy requirements for maintaining the endocochlear potential make a vascular contribution plausible. Most human evidence is observational, however, and common risk factors tend to cluster. Ask for a timeline of changes and exposures alongside family history, rather than assigning the loss to one diagnosis by default (p490–491, p498).

Different animal models reproduce different components. The gerbil can model a strial pattern with lower endocochlear potential, reduced blood flow and metabolic changes. Mouse strains vary greatly in the age at which sensory loss begins. Noise may injure connections between hair cells and nerve fibers even when the temporary increase in pure-tone thresholds recovers. Findings in one species, strain or exposure setting do not settle whether a treatment modifies human presbycusis. In the aging cochlea, altered oxidative defenses, mitochondrial DNA damage, vascular change and altered synapses can interact. Evidence of oxidative damage in human tissue is evidence of association, not proof that supplementing an antioxidant will preserve hearing (p496–499).

Prevention studies illustrate the distinction. Some supplemented animal groups had better outcomes, but study size, strain, age at treatment and even kennel noise complicated interpretation. In one long-term mouse comparison an antioxidant-enriched diet did not improve age-related hearing loss. A Dutch trial found a small low-frequency benefit after **3 years** of folic acid, while the high-frequency losses most characteristic of presbycusis continued. That result does not establish broad clinical protection. A history of harmful sound levels or potentially ototoxic drugs is still actionable: identify exposures that can reasonably be reduced and reassess function over time (p490–491, p498–499).

### Evaluate benefit from amplification, then the need for a different device

Figure 34-6 plots aided and unaided thresholds for the same listener. The open-circle curve worsens at progressively higher frequencies. The filled-circle curve with amplification approaches normal through about **3000 Hz** in that example; there is little reason to boost a low-frequency region already functioning well. Nor does the aid restore all the highest frequencies, where surviving sensory capacity may be too limited. Fitting a digital aid to the audiogram is the beginning of rehabilitation. Return to the situations the person named at the start: understanding a spouse across the table, following several voices, answering a telephone or recognizing an alarm. Lack of use may reflect a mismatch between those needs and the device as fitted rather than absence of disability (p499–500).

When well-fitted aids still leave speech unintelligible, ask how much additional acoustic gain could accomplish. A cochlear implant converts incoming sound into a pattern of electrical stimulation delivered through a cochlear electrode to the auditory nerve. The result depends partly on the surviving nerve and on learning to interpret this input. Many recipients can understand speech better and use the telephone, while unfamiliar contexts and music can remain difficult. The percentage of sentences understood through the poorer ear and the decibel threshold averaged across tones are different measurements; each belongs with the specific book or article criterion cited above. The appropriate answer to the article-based exam question is referral for a candidacy evaluation, not a guarantee of implantation or a precise postoperative score (p499–500).

Improved hearing has immediate value for participation, confidence and communication with clinicians regardless of its possible effect on long-term cognition. Observational studies can associate hearing loss with cognitive decline and hearing-aid use with later dementia diagnosis without eliminating differences between people who obtain and use aids and those who do not. The chapter discusses possible pathways including listening effort, reduced social engagement and shared disease. Distinguish these hypotheses from the randomized intervention evidence described above. Research on regeneration faces an additional hurdle: replacement cells must occupy the correct cochlear architecture, transduce sound and establish functional neural connections. It remains investigational (p499–501).

## Exam traps

- A preserved low-frequency threshold can coexist with disabling high-frequency and speech-in-noise loss (p494–497).
- A 4-kHz unilateral notch calls for an exposure history; it is not the bilateral, diffuse aging pattern in the adjacent panel (p496).
- Aided sentence recognition is measured in percent correct, while PTA4 is a pure-tone average in decibels. Do not substitute one referral measure for the other (p499–500; separately assigned NEJM review).

- Population heritability describes variation among people; it does not assign an individual's hearing loss to a single cause (p489–491).
- Increased speaking volume alone may leave high-frequency consonants indistinct; reduce competing sound and allow time for processing (p494–497).
- The book's implant discussion uses a poorer-ear sentence-recognition percentage. A pure-tone threshold is expressed in decibels (p495, p499).

**Prevention, drugs & exam traps**
- Reduce noise, ototoxin and smoking exposure; exercise and healthy diet may help, but the degree to which specific interventions delay presbycusis remains uncertain (p498).
- Antioxidant results are inconsistent; a small low-frequency benefit from folate in a Dutch study cannot be generalized to restoration of high-frequency hearing (p499).
- “I hear but cannot understand” may reflect central auditory dysfunction that amplification alone cannot resolve (p499–500).
