## Family history and assessment

<span id="ch34-study-genetics" data-exam-ids="mcq-db9b505b636e803c1bb580ce" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>The Framingham comparison found that blood relatives had more similar hearing thresholds than spouses. Heritability was estimated at about 55%: it describes the proportion of variation in the study population attributable to genetic differences, and cannot count how many individual cases are inherited. A patient can inherit susceptibility and also sustain cochlear injury from diabetes, vascular disease, noise or ototoxic medication. Men generally develop deficits earlier, and sex differences narrow later in life (pp489–491).

Ask whether conversation becomes unintelligible in company and whether the patient avoids social situations. The short Hearing Handicap Inventory for the Elderly measures perceived functional difficulty. A typical gradual bilateral history supports a presumptive clinical diagnosis after checking for other causes. A bedside whisper test has variable sensitivity. Otoscopy can reveal obstructing wax, and audiometry measures each ear’s thresholds and helps distinguish sensorineural and conductive causes. Sudden unilateral loss needs urgent assessment. Both B and D received credit in September 2024; a clinic screen alone cannot confirm the type or degree of loss (Hazzard p493 and Lin, NEJM 2024, pp1506–1507).

## Recognizing speech in noise

<span id="ch34-study-speech" data-exam-ids="mcq-ca570710752bad38efb708ba" data-exam-read-page="495" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>A voice can be loud enough to hear while its words are indistinct. Reduced sensitivity at high frequencies obscures consonants that distinguish one word from another. In June 2025, **ספר (sefer)** contains both /s/ and /f/. **שלום (shalom)** also has a high-frequency consonant, so the distinction is relative. Vowels and context can carry a quiet conversation until competing voices mask those remaining cues (pp494–495, Table 34-2).

The audiogram in Figure 34-5A slopes downward in both ears as pitch rises. In panel B a flatter pattern spans low and high frequencies; panel C shows a unilateral notch typical of noise exposure. At the same pure-tone threshold, two people can have different speech-in-noise performance. The QuickSIN test measures the signal advantage a listener needs over competing noise. Slower delivery and a quieter room can immediately improve communication (pp495–497).

## Hearing aids and referral for an implant

<span id="ch34-study-implant" data-practice-ids="mcq-4c92ab23f4ca6e471480cf69" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**June 2026 Q39.** PTA4 averages thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000 and 4000 Hz in one ear. A better-ear average of 70 dB falls in the severe range. Speech remains hard to understand even in quiet with properly fitted aids. Refer to a cochlear implant centre for aided speech testing and an individual assessment. Lin describes a PTA4 generally at least 60 dB in one or both ears plus insufficient functional benefit from aids as a reason to consider referral. Hazzard discusses a different measure, sentence recognition below 60% in the poorer ear. Decibels quantify the sound level needed to detect tones; percent correct measures speech recognition. [Lin, NEJM 2024, pp1509–1511, Table 2 and Figure 2](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp2306778) · [Hazzard pp499–500](?chapter=34#p499).

A digital hearing aid supplies frequency-specific acoustic gain to a functioning cochlea. Figure 34-6 shows improved thresholds through about 3000 Hz in one listener, with limited recovery at the highest frequencies. A cochlear implant encodes sound as electrical stimulation of the auditory nerve. Understanding speech after implantation takes practice; benefit varies with neural function and other factors, and chronological age alone does not rule out benefit (pp499–500).

## Inner ear, brain and cognition

The basal cochlea encodes high frequencies and the apex low frequencies. Outer hair cells amplify and sharpen mechanical responses; inner hair cells convert movement into signals for auditory nerve fibres. The stria vascularis maintains the ionic gradient that powers transduction. Damage to basal hair cells can produce a sloping audiogram, whereas loss of this gradient can raise thresholds across frequencies. Synaptic or neural loss can impair discrimination even with relatively preserved tone detection. Several lesions can coexist (pp492–497, Figures 34-1 to 34-5).

Hearing loss is associated with faster cognitive decline. Listening effort, social isolation and common pathology are plausible explanations. The chapter describes observational data. The later ACHIEVE trial found no difference in three-year cognitive change across its full cohort, with slower decline in a prespecified comparison of higher-risk participants. Offer rehabilitation for communication and participation; the evidence does not promise prevention of dementia for each patient (Hazzard p500 and Lin, NEJM 2024, p1511).

## Prevention and emerging treatments

Permanent hair-cell loss limits recovery. In mice, apparently transient noise damage can leave lasting synaptic injury, and different strains lose hearing at different ages. Oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury and vascular changes are candidate mechanisms. Their importance differs between animal models and human ageing. Reducing avoidable noise and ototoxic exposures is practical; vitamin supplementation and hair-cell regeneration have no established role in routine treatment of presbycusis. Experimental nerve or brainstem stimulation and regeneration are discussed separately from everyday rehabilitation (pp491, 497–501).

## Summary

**Definition & epidemiology**
- Presbycusis is heterogeneous age-related peripheral and central auditory dysfunction; it usually begins at high frequencies and progresses into the speech range (p489–490).
- Significant loss affects approximately **44%** in their sixties, **66%** at **70–79**, and **90%** after **80** in the chapter's cited estimates (p490).
- Genetic susceptibility interacts with noise, ototoxic drugs, smoking and comorbidity; diabetes and vascular disease can promote cochlear injury (p489–491).

**Assessment**
- Ask about speech understanding, especially in noise, rather than only whether sounds are audible. A hearing-handicap questionnaire can identify everyday effects (p489, p493, p500).
- Pure-tone audiometry, tympanometry, acoustic reflexes and word-recognition testing characterize different aspects of impairment (p489).
- One cited professional definition uses threshold loss of at least **25 dB** at any frequency between **250 and 8000 Hz**; functional impact also depends on frequency pattern (p493).
- Sensorineural loss often begins with high-frequency hair-cell damage; a flat audiogram can reflect metabolic/supporting-structure dysfunction (p495).

**Management**
- Tailor digital hearing aids to the audiogram; they can improve hearing and speech discrimination but do not regenerate lost hair cells (p499).
- Address acceptance and continued use, not just device provision; many eligible older people never try an aid or stop using it (p499).
- When aids cannot provide sufficient correction, consider cochlear implantation. The chapter cites benefit when sentence scores in the poorer ear are below **60%** (p499).
- Hearing aids amplify sound using remaining sensory cells; implants bypass those cells to stimulate the auditory nerve electrically (p499).

**Prevention, drugs & exam traps**
- Reduce noise, ototoxin and smoking exposure; exercise and healthy diet may help, but the degree to which specific interventions delay presbycusis remains uncertain (p498).
- Antioxidant results are inconsistent; a small low-frequency benefit from folate in a Dutch study cannot be generalized to restoration of high-frequency hearing (p499).
- “I hear but cannot understand” may reflect central auditory dysfunction that amplification alone cannot resolve (p499–500).
