# Chapter 105 Study: Bacterial Pneumonia and Tuberculosis

## Definition & Epidemiology
- Pneumonia (clinical definition) = symptoms (fever, chills, cough, pleuritic pain) + signs (hyper-/hypothermia, raised RR, dullness, crackles, wheezing, pleural rub) + an opacity on chest x-ray; research definitions require ≥2 symptoms, ≥1 sign, and a new opacity not due to other conditions (p1679).
- Incidence **2-10** cases/1000/yr overall; in patients >65, European/US registries report **25-40**/1000/yr with **7%-35%** mortality (p1680).
- US burden: **>4 million** annual outpatient visits, **>1 million** hospitalizations/yr (**600,000** in adults >65); **10-20%** of hospitalized patients need ICU; mean length of stay **5 days**; overall 30-day mortality **23%** (p1680).
- Mortality **1%-2%** in young patients without comorbidity vs **25%-50%** in older adults with severe comorbidity; 30-day mortality **3x higher** in patients >85 vs 65-74; **>50,000** US deaths/yr, **85%** in patients >65; 30-day readmission **~20%**; annual US cost **>$17 billion**, **80%** driven by hospitalization (p1680).

## Etiology & Resistant-Organism Risk
- *Streptococcus pneumoniae* remains the single most common agent, **20%-65%** of cases (p1681).
- Viruses implicated in **12%-18%** of cases; influenza, RSV, and human metapneumovirus together account for **8%-14%** (p1681).
- Hazzard defines hospital-acquired pneumonia as pneumonia arising **more than 48 hours** into a hospital stay or during the **10–14 days after hospital discharge** (p1681).
  > Current practice (not the book): ATS/IDSA defines HAP as pneumonia arising **≥48 hours after admission** that was not incubating on admission. Recent discharge alone does not meet that definition — [ATS/IDSA HAP/VAP guideline, 2016](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/hap_vap/).
- HAP pathogen-specific risk factors (Table 105-1, p1681): **MDR pathogens** — prior antibiotics, hospitalization **>5 days** in the prior 90 d, high hospital resistance rate, nursing-home residence, home IV therapy, chronic dialysis, active wound care, contact with an MDR-infected patient, or immunosuppressive disease/therapy; ***Pseudomonas*/MDR bacilli** — recent ICU admission, prior antibiotics, chronic corticosteroids, structural lung disease, or late-onset nosocomial pneumonia; ***Legionella*** — chronic corticosteroids, hematologic cancer, contaminated-water contact, or prior nosocomial *Legionella* cases; ***S aureus*** — recent ICU admission, prior influenza, colonization/prevalence of methicillin-resistant strains, TBI, coma, diabetes, or renal failure; **anaerobes** — poor oral hygiene, decreased consciousness, airway manipulation, or recent abdominal surgery.

<span data-exam-page="1682"></span>

- Health care–associated pneumonia (HCAP) can be defined by one of: hospitalization for **2 days** or more in the preceding **90 days**; nursing home or extended care residence; home infusion therapy; chronic dialysis within **30 days**; home wound care; or a family member with a multidrug-resistant pathogen. Up to **50%** of emergency department pneumonia patients may meet HCAP criteria, but only **10% to 30%** of them have resistant bacteria, so many authors favor an approach based on individual risk factors and severity (p1682).

> Current practice (not the book): For community-onset pneumonia, nursing-home residence alone does not justify broad-spectrum therapy. Choose empiric MRSA or Pseudomonas coverage from locally validated risk factors, especially prior respiratory isolation or hospitalization with parenteral antibiotics in the preceding 90 days. Severity and cultures determine initial coverage and de-escalation. [ATS/IDSA CAP guideline, 2019, Table 4 and question 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6812437/).
- Shorr MDRO risk score: recent hospitalization **4 pts**, admission from long-term care **3 pts**, hemodialysis **2 pts**, critical illness **1 pt**; a score of **0** carries **84%** negative predictive value for MDRO (p1682).
- One study: clinical signs of severe pneumonia plus two risk factors (immunosuppression, hospitalization in the prior 90 d, Barthel <50, antibiotics in the prior 6 mo) were associated with higher MDRO frequency (**2% vs 27%**). Ewig / Brito-Niederman approach: ≥2 multidrug-resistance factors (severe pneumonia, hospitalization in the prior 90 d, nursing-home residence, severe basal dependence, immunosuppression, antibiotics in the prior 6 mo) → empiric MDRO coverage warranted (p1682).
- ESBL-producing *Klebsiella* found in **20%** of US and **35%** of European pneumonia isolates (p1683).
- *Pseudomonas* is uncommon in older adults overall (**1%-2%**), but should be suspected with severe COPD (FEV1 <35%), multiple prior antibiotic courses, ICU admission and/or bronchiectasis, or prior colonization (p1683).
- Risk factors for less-common pathogens (Table 105-2, p1682): **ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae** — prior antibiotics, hemodialysis, long-term indwelling urinary catheter, long-stay-center residence, recent hospitalization, diabetes, or recurrent UTIs; **aspiration-prone Enterobacteriaceae/anaerobes** — functional impairment, dysphagia, GERD, history of vomiting, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, periodontal disease, or poor oral hygiene; **MRSA** — wound/bedsore care, serious illness plus recent hospitalization plus prior IV antibiotics plus institutionalization, previous colonization, or superinfection during an influenza epidemic.
- Exposure-specific pathogens (Table 105-3, p1683): bird/fowl/farm-animal contact → *Chlamydophila psittaci*; rabbit contact → *Coxiella burnetii*; bat contact or residence in an endemic area → *Histoplasma capsulatum*; air-conditioning/cooling-tower exposure → *Legionella pneumophila*; travel to Southeast Asia → *Burkholderia pseudomallei*, avian influenza; alcohol intoxication → *S pneumoniae*, *K pneumoniae*, anaerobes, *S aureus*; prison inmates → *S pneumoniae*, *M tuberculosis*; advanced HIV → *S pneumoniae*, *H influenzae*, *P jiroveci*, *M tuberculosis*.

> Current practice (not the book): Table 105-3 prints the farm-animal and rabbit associations [sic]. In the CAP guideline’s exposure table, birds suggest psittacosis, contact with farm animals or parturient cats suggests Q fever (Coxiella burnetii), and rabbit exposure suggests tularemia (Francisella tularensis). [IDSA/ATS CAP guideline, Table 5, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1086/511159).
- Shorr MRSA prediction score: low risk if total score **≤1** (p1683).

## Diagnosis & Severity Stratification

<span data-exam-page="1686"></span>

- Chest x-ray can be falsely negative in up to **30%** of cases (more so with dehydration/neutropenia); repeat the film at **24-48 h** if the first is negative and suspicion remains (p1684).
- Serum lactate **≥2 mmol/L** predicts 30-day mortality (p1685).
- Pneumococcal urinary antigen: sensitivity **>60%**, specificity **>90%**; stays positive for **~3 months** after resolution, limiting its use to judge relapse or treatment response (p1685).
- Legionella urinary antigen detects only serogroup I but has sensitivity **>90%** for that serogroup (p1685).
- Admission criteria: **PSI ≥ III** or **CURB-65 ≥ 2**; a short-stay unit is a safe, cost-effective alternative for patients with CURB-65 of 2 or PSI of III (p1687).
- CURB-65 scoring (Table 105-4, p1686): 1 pt each for confusion, urea **>44 mg/dL** (BUN >20), RR **≥30**, SBP **<90** or DBP **≤60**, age **≥65**; mortality by score — **0-1** low (0.7%-2.1%, consider outpatient), **2** intermediate (9.2%), **3** high (15%, admit), **4-5** **>40%** (admit, consider ICU).
- SCAP severity score (Table 105-5, p1686): major criteria — pH **<7.30** (13 pts), SBP **<90 mm Hg** (11 pts); minor criteria — confusion (5 pts), BUN **>30 mg/dL** (5 pts), RR **>30** (9 pts), multilobar/bilateral CXR (5 pts), PaO2 **<54 mm Hg** or PaO2/FiO2 **<250** (6 pts), age **≥80** (5 pts); risk — low **0-1** (<10 pts), intermediate **2** (10-19 pts), high **3-4** (**>20 points** [sic]; the printed footnote leaves exactly 20 unassigned).
- SMART-COP score (Table 105-6, p1687): SBP **<90** (2 pts), multilobar involvement (1 pt), albumin **<3.5 g/dL** (1 pt), RR age-adjusted (1 pt: **<50y ≥25/min**, **≥50y ≥30/min**), tachycardia **≥125** (1 pt), confusion (1 pt), oxygenation age-adjusted (2 pts: **<50y** PaO2 **<70** or SpO2 **≤93%** or PaO2/FiO2 **<333**; **≥50y** PaO2 **<60** or SpO2 **≤90%** or PaO2/FiO2 **<250**), pH **<7.35** (2 pts); risk for needing vasopressor/intensive respiratory support — **0-2** low, **3-4** moderate, **5-6** high, **≥7** very high [sic, printed age boundary].

  > Current practice (not the book): The original SMART-COP rule places a patient aged exactly 50 in the younger group. [Charles et al., 2008, SMART-COP](https://doi.org/10.1086/589754).
- Discharge readiness: HR **<100 bpm**, RR **<24/min**, axillary temperature **<37.2°C (<98.96°F)**, systolic BP **>90 mm Hg**, SpO2 **>90%**, plus baseline awareness and oral tolerance; most patients stabilize by hospital day **3-4**, up to **7 days** if frail (p1687).

## Management Framework & Treatment
- Two questions should drive antibiotic choice: (1) severe pneumonia or risk factors for unusual pathogens? (2) does the patient meet criteria for frailty? Both "no" → standard younger-adult regimens; severe pneumonia and/or risk factors for less common pathogens → consider broadening; mildly frail → same antibiotics as independent patients plus functional/cognitive monitoring; moderate-severe frailty → broader coverage in addition to routine pathogens (p1687-1689).

<span data-exam-page="1688"></span>

- In Table 105-8 (p1688), the non-frail outpatient row lists amoxicillin/clavulanate or a second-generation cephalosporin with azithromycin/fluoroquinolone; the nonsevere inpatient row lists amoxicillin/clavulanate or ceftriaxone/cefotaxime/ceftaroline with azithromycin/fluoroquinolone; the severe row lists ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftaroline or ertapenem plus azithromycin or a fluoroquinolone, with linezolid/vancomycin, an antipseudomonal beta-lactam or oseltamivir added only if indicated. In prefrailty the table lists the nonsevere inpatient regimen; in frailty it lists ertapenem, amoxicillin/clavulanate, or ceftriaxone plus clindamycin.

> Current practice (not the book): For an adult inpatient with nonsevere CAP and no MRSA or Pseudomonas risk factors, ATS/IDSA recommends either a beta-lactam plus a macrolide or respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy. Hazzard’s printed table puts a plus sign before the azithromycin/fluoroquinolone alternatives. [ATS](https://doi.org/10.1164/rccm.201908-1581ST).

> Current practice (not the book): For suspected aspiration pneumonia, avoid routinely adding anaerobic coverage unless a lung abscess or empyema is suspected. [ATS/IDSA CAP guideline, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6812437/).
- Directed treatment (Table 105-9, p1688): *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — piperacillin/tazobactam or imipenem or meropenem or cefepime, plus levofloxacin or ciprofloxacin or amikacin or tobramycin; ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae — ertapenem; MRSA — add linezolid or vancomycin. Empirical late HAP (≥5 days) or HAP with risk factors (Table 105-10): ceftazidime or cefepime or imipenem or meropenem or piperacillin/tazobactam, plus ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin or amikacin or tobramycin, ± linezolid or vancomycin (p1688).

> Current practice (not the book): For ESBL-producing Enterobacterales infections outside the urinary tract, IDSA prefers a carbapenem. In critical illness or hypoalbuminemia, meropenem or imipenem-cilastatin is preferred to ertapenem. Tailor definitive therapy to susceptibility, source and clinical response. [IDSA antimicrobial-resistance guidance, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39108079/).
- Quinolones should not be prescribed when pulmonary tuberculosis is suspected, because of their tuberculostatic activity and the possibility of delaying the TB diagnosis (p1689).
- Treatment failure = no clinical stability by **3-4 days**, or deterioration/respiratory failure/septic shock in the first **72 h**; associated with a **fivefold** increase in mortality. However, stability may take longer **without implying therapeutic failure** in older patients with severe pneumonia or serious comorbidity (eg, severe COPD, untreated heart failure) (p1690).
- A **5-7 day** course is sufficient for most pneumonia even in older adults; extend for *Legionella*, resistant GNB, or MRSA, or for persistent fever beyond **72 h**, persistence of >1 instability criterion, inadequate initial coverage, or complications (p1690).

> Current practice (not the book): The 2025 ATS guideline conditionally supports fewer than five days of antibiotics, with a minimum of three days, in selected outpatients and nonsevere inpatients once stable. Severe CAP generally requires at least five days. Organism, complications and clinical response can require longer treatment. [ATS CAP guideline update, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40679934/).

<span data-exam-page="1690"></span>

- In older adults at risk of aspiration pneumonia, oral care may reduce pneumonia, although efficacy in the highest-risk groups (eg, nursing home residents) was not shown in a high-quality randomized study (p1690).
- PCV13 should be given first, with PPSV given at least **8 weeks** later, per ACIP (p1690).
  > Current practice (not the book): prior vaccination determines the schedule: PCV15, PCV20 or PCV21 for adults ≥50 who never received a PCV or whose history is unknown; after PCV15 give PPSV23 if not previously administered, one year later (8 weeks for certain conditions); no PPSV23 after PCV20/PCV21 — CDC, 2026.

## Tuberculosis — Epidemiology & Pathophysiology
- **>1.7 billion** people (about **25%** of the world's population) are estimated to be infected with *M tuberculosis*; high-burden regions run **≥100/100,000**, while the US, Western Europe, Canada, Japan, and Australia run **<25/100,000** (p1691).
- TB incidence is bimodal: one peak at **15-35 years**, a second after **65 years** (p1691).
- Healthy people infected with TB carry a **5%-10%** lifetime risk of active disease; **50%-80%** of those who progress do so within the first **2 years** (p1692).
- HIV coinfection without antiretroviral treatment carries a **10%** annual risk of active disease (p1692).

## Tuberculosis — Diagnosis & Treatment

<span data-exam-page="1693"></span>

- A false-positive TST occurs with cross-reaction with nontuberculous mycobacteria and after BCG vaccination. IGRA is far more specific with the same sensitivity (lower in immunosuppressed patients); it cannot discriminate previous from current infection or latent from active TB (p1693).
- Two-step tuberculin test for initial geriatric screening (older adults have increased cutaneous anergy): a negative first test (induration **<10 mm**) is followed by a repeat test at **2 weeks**; positive = **≥10 mm** and an increase of **≥6 mm** over the first test (p1693).
- Isoniazid causes hepatotoxicity in up to **4%-5%** of patients >65 y, versus **<1%** in those <65 y (p1694).
- Chemoprophylaxis is generally reserved for a Mantoux induration increase **≥15 mm** over a prior negative test; in close contacts of active TB and other high-risk individuals **with negative TST or IGRA**, preventive treatment should also be considered, unless contraindicated (p1694).

> Current practice (not the book): CDC interprets TST induration by risk category. Thresholds are ≥5 mm in highest-risk groups, ≥10 mm in additional defined risk groups, and ≥15 mm in people with no known risk factors. Exclude active disease before treating latent infection. [CDC TST clinical testing guidance, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/tuberculin-skin-test.html).
- Hazzard usually treats latent TB with **9 months of isoniazid** unless resistance is suspected. **4 months of rifampin** is the alternative for isoniazid resistance or intolerance, and **12 weeks of isoniazid plus rifapentine** was also effective (p1694).
  > Current practice (not the book): when suitable, short rifamycin-based courses are preferred, including 3 months of weekly isoniazid plus rifapentine or 4 months of daily rifampin. Daily isoniazid for 6 or 9 months is an alternative — [NTCA/CDC guideline, 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/rr/rr6901a1.htm).
- Standard active-TB regimen: 4-drug intensive phase **2 months**, then 2-drug continuation phase **≥4 months** (total **6 months**); extend the continuation phase to **7 months** (9 months total) for cavitary disease plus a positive culture at month 2, or if the intensive phase omitted pyrazinamide (p1694).

> Current practice (not the book): For eligible people aged 12 or older with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB, the updated ATS/CDC/ERS/IDSA guideline conditionally recommends the four-month rifapentine–moxifloxacin regimen. Confirm susceptibility and suitability with the TB programme. [ATS/CDC/ERS/IDSA TB guideline, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11755361/).
- The book describes older **18–24 month** regimens for multidrug-resistant TB and explains that the resistance pattern determines treatment (p1694).
- First-line regimen exceptions (Table 105-12, p1695): give pyridoxine **10-50 mg/day** with isoniazid if alcoholic, malnourished, diabetic, or has kidney failure (peripheral neuropathy risk); Table 105-12 includes “toxicity or infection by a resistant strain” in its substitution rows [sic]. The p1696 prose limits its first-line substitutions to susceptible disease with drug intolerance.

> Current practice (not the book): Manage rifampin-resistant pulmonary TB on the drug-resistant pathway with specialist input and rapid susceptibility testing. For eligible patients aged 14 years or older with fluoroquinolone-susceptible MDR/RR pulmonary TB, the guideline recommends the 6-month bedaquiline–pretomanid–linezolid–moxifloxacin regimen (BPaLM). If fluoroquinolones cannot be used because of resistance or intolerance, six months of bedaquiline–pretomanid–linezolid (BPaL) is recommended for eligible patients in this age group who have received bedaquiline and linezolid for less than one month or have never received them. [ATS/CDC/ERS/IDSA, 2025, recommendations 3–4](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/treatment-of-drug-susceptible-tb/treatment-of-drug-resistant-and-drug-susceptible-tb-2025-update/).
- Asymptomatic AST elevation occurs in **~20%** of patients on the standard 4-drug regimen and usually resolves spontaneously (p1696).
- Stop hepatotoxic TB drugs if bilirubin **≥3 mg/dL** or transaminases **>5x** the upper limit of normal (or **>3x** ULN with symptoms of hepatitis) (p1696).

*Exam trap: a quinolone looks like a reasonable empiric choice for undifferentiated CAP, but the book says not to prescribe it when pulmonary TB is suspected — its tuberculostatic activity can delay the TB diagnosis (p1689).*

## Recall

**1. What four items make up the Shorr MDRO risk score, and what does a score of zero mean?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-2"><summary>Answer</summary>

Recent hospitalization (4 pts), admission from long-term care (3 pts), hemodialysis (2 pts), critical illness (1 pt); a score of 0 carries an 84% negative predictive value for MDRO (p1682).

</details>

**2. What rates of ESBL-producing *Klebsiella* were found among pneumonia isolates in the US vs Europe?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-3"><summary>Answer</summary>

In the cited surveillance study, 20% of isolated Klebsiella spp. in the United States and 35% in Europe were ESBL-producing (p1683).

</details>

**3. In what fraction of cases can the initial chest x-ray be falsely negative, and what should be done if suspicion for pneumonia remains?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-4"><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 30% (more often with dehydration or neutropenia); repeat the film at 24-48 hours (p1684).

</details>

**4. What are the sensitivity/specificity of the pneumococcal urinary antigen test, and what limits its use in relapse or treatment-response assessment?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-6"><summary>Answer</summary>

Sensitivity >60%, specificity >90%; it stays positive for about 3 months after resolution of the pneumonia (p1685).

</details>

**5. What PSI and CURB-65 thresholds define criteria for hospital admission?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-7"><summary>Answer</summary>

PSI ≥ III or CURB-65 ≥ 2 (p1687).

</details>

**6. What vital-sign criteria define readiness for hospital discharge, and by what day do most patients stabilize?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-8"><summary>Answer</summary>

HR <100 bpm, RR <24/min, axillary temperature <37.2°C (<98.96°F), systolic BP >90 mm Hg, SpO2 >90%, good awareness and oral tolerance; most stabilize by hospital day 3-4 (up to 7 days if frail) (p1687).

</details>

**7. How is antibiotic treatment failure defined, and what mortality risk does it carry?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-9"><summary>Answer</summary>

No clinical stability by 3-4 days, or deterioration/respiratory failure/septic shock in the first 72 hours; associated with a fivefold increase in mortality. But in older patients with severe pneumonia or serious comorbidity (eg, severe COPD, untreated heart failure), stability may take longer without implying therapeutic failure (p1690).

</details>

**8. What duration of antibiotic therapy is sufficient for most older adults with pneumonia, and when should it be extended?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-10"><summary>Answer</summary>

5-7 days; extend for infection by *Legionella*, resistant gram-negative bacilli, or MRSA, or for persistent fever beyond 72 hours, persistence of more than one criterion of clinical instability, inadequate initial coverage, or complications (p1690).

</details>

**9. Roughly what fraction of the world's population is estimated to be infected with *M tuberculosis*, and what is the bimodal age pattern of TB incidence?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-12"><summary>Answer</summary>

About 25% (>1.7 billion people); incidence peaks at 15-35 years and again after 65 years (p1691).

</details>

**10. What is the two-step tuberculin test protocol in older adults, and why is it used?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-13"><summary>Answer</summary>

Older adults have increased cutaneous anergy; if the first TST is negative (induration <10 mm), a second test is done at 2 weeks. A positive result is induration ≥10 mm and an increase of ≥6 mm over the first test (p1693).

</details>

**11. What isoniazid hepatotoxicity rate is seen in patients over 65, compared with those under 65?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-14"><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 4%-5% in patients >65 years, versus <1% in those <65 years (p1694).

</details>

**12. At what bilirubin or transaminase thresholds should hepatotoxic TB drugs be stopped?**

<details data-recall-key="105s:recall-15"><summary>Answer</summary>

Bilirubin ≥3 mg/dL, or transaminases >5x the upper limit of normal (>3x ULN if the patient has symptoms of hepatitis) (p1696).

</details>
