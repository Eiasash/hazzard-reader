# Chapter 40 Study: Applied Clinical Geroscience

## Summary

**The geroscience hypothesis**
- Shared biological aging mechanisms may drive multiple chronic diseases and geriatric syndromes; targeting them could extend healthspan (p593-595).
- Hallmarks interact: altered metabolism, macromolecular damage, epigenetics, inflammation, stress responses, proteostasis, stem-cell changes and senescence (p594).
- Geriatric syndromes share functional risk factors and multifactorial causes. No single molecule explains aging (p595-597).
- An upstream target may influence several downstream processes; pathway plausibility alone does not prove clinical benefit (p597, p602-603).

**Evidence and trial design**
- Rapamycin, metformin and senolytics are investigational geroscience approaches; much efficacy evidence remains preclinical (p597-599).
- Dasatinib plus quercetin has early human pilot evidence in specific conditions; this does not establish routine treatment for aging (p599).
- CALERIE studied **25% calorie restriction** in nonobese adults, reporting improvements in disease-risk measures and aging biomarkers (p599).
- Healthspan trials target gradual accumulation of disease and disability; resilience trials target recovery after stressors such as surgery or myocardial infarction (p599-600).
- Measure clinically meaningful function, independence, disease accumulation and survival; biomarkers may be secondary outcomes (p600).
- Include heterogeneous older adults, chronic conditions and care settings. Limited life expectancy and severe impairment require careful consideration of the chance to benefit within the study period (p600-601).

**Biomarkers and counseling**
- No single clinical-trial endpoint or aging biomarker is universally accepted; a marker should relate to outcomes and respond meaningfully to intervention (p601-602).
- Multisystem indices and biomarker panels may be more informative than a single pathway; telomere measures have substantial variability (p602).
- DNA-methylation clocks estimate aging-related patterns; evidence that interventions change them remains an evolving research area (p602).
- Unregulated clinics are not controlled trials. Most investigational therapies fail, and older adults may be especially vulnerable to harms (p602-603).
- Some age-related changes may be adaptive or consequences of aging rather than causes. Clinical geroscience needs combined biological, geriatric and trial expertise (p603).

### Read the endpoint before interpreting the intervention

- In mice, low-dose rapamycin can extend both survival and the duration of health. Human studies of a rapamycin analogue in community-dwelling older people examined vaccine responses and reported infections. Improved immune measurements are evidence about that outcome; they do not by themselves show that life is longer (p597–599).
- The open-label dasatinib–quercetin pilot involved people with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Tolerability in a small group with a particular disease is a different question from efficacy for otherwise healthy older people. The chapter presents this work as an early translational step (p599).
- After an acute stress such as surgery, measure the patient's return toward baseline function. A trial aimed at delaying multimorbidity would instead follow the accumulation of chronic conditions or disability over time. Decide which question the trial addresses before interpreting a short-term laboratory change (p599–600).
- In a post-hoc analysis of CALERIE, a composite index estimated that physiologic age advanced less over **2 years** in participants assigned to dietary restriction. This was a model-based result. Different combinations of clinical measurements can estimate biological age, and a change on one index is distinct from an observed gain in years lived independently (p602).

### What evidence would support the geroscience hypothesis?

Aging is the strongest shared risk factor for many chronic diseases, yet different people of the same chronological age have very different combinations of disease and disability. Geroscience asks whether some of that shared vulnerability arises from biological processes that participate in several conditions and can be modified. Figure 40-2 illustrates this relationship with age on the horizontal axis and deaths per **100,000 people per year** on the vertical axis, despite the caption calling age the vertical axis (p595). The claim has testable consequences. Changing a proposed mechanism should do more than make a measurement look younger: it should alter risk, function, recovery or another outcome that matters to patients. A drug that treats only one disease effectively may still be valuable without establishing the broader hypothesis (p593–595).

Table 40-1 separates lifespan, ordinarily expressed as a duration of survival, from healthspan, the period of healthy active life before marked functional limitation or dependence. An intervention could postpone disability without changing the date of death. It could also extend survival without improving the period lived independently. A greater average age at death, a longer median lifespan, fewer new diagnoses, more years walking independently and faster recovery from an illness are related but distinct outcomes. Ask which of them a study measured before deciding what it has established (p593–595, p599–601).

Table 40-2 summarizes the rationale. Several interacting biological processes change with age; experimental interventions in animals can alter some of those processes and in some settings extend survival or function. Many chronic diseases cluster as people age, and geriatric syndromes involve shared vulnerabilities. None of those observations by itself proves that a given age-associated change causes disease in older humans or that modifying it will help. A useful human experiment needs a credible biological rationale, a population in whom benefit could reasonably occur, tolerability and a clinical endpoint capable of distinguishing benefit from a laboratory change alone (p594–596, p599–602).

### Distinguishing the hallmarks

Macromolecular damage refers to accumulated injury to proteins, lipids and DNA. Proteostasis concerns the ability to form, maintain and renew proteins and remove those that are damaged or misfolded. These concepts overlap but are not synonyms: an intervention might reduce damage, improve the handling of damaged material or alter the cellular response to it. Altered metabolism includes nutrient sensing, energy balance and signals regulating growth. Epigenetic change concerns the regulation of gene expression, including methylation and chromatin organization, rather than a simple change in the DNA sequence. A measurement in any one category may also reflect activity in several others (p594).

Inflammatory signals can arise within one tissue but affect the function of distant organs. They can both follow existing disease and contribute to its progression. Stress adaptation spans responses to short-lived challenges and the failure of compensation during persistent stress. Senescence describes a state that includes cell-cycle arrest and can include a proinflammatory secretory phenotype. Declining stem-cell or regenerative capacity concerns the ability to renew or repair tissue. A fall or hospitalization is a clinical stressor, not itself a molecular hallmark. It can reveal the consequences when these biological changes and pre-existing disease have already narrowed reserve (p594–596, p599–600).

Figure 40-1 places the hallmarks in an interacting framework. With age, the same tissue can exhibit altered metabolism, accumulated damage, reduced repair and inflammation. A marker associated with several diseases may be a signal of shared injury rather than a tractable common cause. Conversely, altering one upstream process could affect several downstream changes. The distinction is why a mechanistic study may appropriately examine a panel while still requiring a prespecified functional or disease outcome. A list of hallmarks names plausible questions; it does not rank therapies or establish efficacy (p594–598, p601–603).

### Why a geriatric syndrome is a demanding test

A one-to-one model from a single molecular abnormality to a single disease can work for some inherited conditions. The chapter contrasts that model with the many-to-many relationships involved in falls, delirium, incontinence and frailty. Several modest risk factors can combine to produce one presentation, while the same vulnerability can contribute to several different syndromes. A person with reduced mobility, cognitive impairment and limited physiological reserve may fall during an acute illness and may also be vulnerable to delirium or loss of continence. Identifying one immediate precipitant does not explain why recovery and downstream consequences differ between people (p595–597).

Figures 40-3 and 40-4 extend the familiar clinical framework backward toward biological aging. Clinical risks such as reduced function act between the underlying biology and the event. Treatment aimed at a precipitating cause can still be essential even if an upstream mechanism eventually becomes treatable. A patient with delirium needs assessment for acute illness; the possibility of shared aging biology is not an alternative bedside diagnosis. The research question is whether altering common vulnerability could reduce the incidence or severity of more than one syndrome or improve resilience to several stresses (p595–597).

Exercise illustrates the distinction between a multicomponent outcome and proof of a unique mechanism. Improvements in mobility, physical capacity or recovery can matter regardless of whether researchers can attribute them to one pathway. Several biological and behavioral mechanisms may change together. A drug with a plausible single target requires separate evidence that the target is reached and that the expected outcome improves. Similar-looking functional improvements from two interventions do not show that the same molecular change caused them, and failure to affect a single disease marker does not settle the effects on function or independence (p596–598).

### How to read a preclinical intervention table

Table 40-3 separates reported extension of healthspan and lifespan from evidence that addresses only one of the two. Its examples with preclinical evidence for both include caloric restriction, exercise, rapamycin, selected renin–angiotensin system drugs, metformin and certain senolytic compounds. The healthspan group includes some flavonoids, sirtuin activators and inhibitors of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. Other interventions have evidence primarily about survival; some effects are specifically described for median survival. Placement in an experimental category does not mean that a human dose, favorable safety profile or general anti-aging indication is known (p597–598).

Read the footnotes before interpreting a blank outcome. In some studies lifespan was not assessed, and in others healthspan was unavailable. Missing evidence is different from evidence that no change occurred. A treatment tested in one sex, strain, age or stage of life may also yield different results in another group. If an intervention is started early in life, its effect on long-term development and disease accumulation need not predict the result of starting it after disability is established. The need to distinguish animal conditions from the clinical circumstances of older adults is one reason the chapter devotes a separate section to translation (p597–600).

The same agent can appear under more than one mechanistic heading. Rapamycin affects nutrient-signaling pathways and can influence inflammatory or secretory responses, while metformin has established clinical use for diabetes and is discussed as a candidate affecting several aging-related processes. Evidence supporting an appropriate established indication does not validate its use to prevent aging in otherwise healthy people. A potential candidate can also carry adverse effects that matter disproportionately in frail patients or when treatment would be taken for years. No patient dosing regimen for extending human lifespan follows from the table (p597–599, p602–603).

### Senotherapeutics as a worked example of translation

Distinguish the target, the strategy and the claim. Senescent cells can persist and influence nearby or distant tissues through secreted signals. A senolytic aims to reduce the burden of such cells, while another class may suppress components of their secretory phenotype. Evidence of a change in a tissue marker after exposure supports the possibility that the biological target was affected. It leaves open whether the change is durable, whether another biological function is impaired and whether the person experiences a benefit. Some changes associated with aging may also be adaptive, so reversing a marker cannot automatically be equated with improving health (p597–599, p601–603).

The chapter describes an early open-label study of intermittent dasatinib plus quercetin in people with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The study asked whether a candidate approach could be given in a specific disease context and provided preliminary human information. Open-label tolerability in selected participants cannot establish comparative efficacy for mobility, dementia, lifespan or general wellness. Different questions would require a comparator, adequate follow-up, an outcome appropriate to the target population and systematic assessment of adverse effects. The chapter's mention of fisetin and other candidates describes a research direction at publication, rather than completed proof for prescribing them (p598–599).

Another early human example is a rapamycin analogue studied in community-dwelling older adults. Vaccine response and reported infections provided clinically relevant tests of aspects of immune function. They do not by themselves measure a reduction in multimorbidity or longer survival. CALERIE studied dietary restriction in nonobese adults, again a different population and intervention. A signal in a metabolic or immune measure can justify a larger trial when the mechanism and safety make sense. It cannot be transferred without further evidence to a frail older adult with involuntary weight loss or to a patient using a commercial product of different composition (p598–599, p602).

### Three clinical questions that require different trial designs

Figure 40-5 groups clinical tests of aging-directed interventions by the outcome they aim to change: geriatric syndromes, multimorbidity or resilience after a stressor (p598). One question is whether an intervention delays accumulation of chronic disease. Figure 40-6 illustrates a hypothetical shift toward later morbidity at a given incidence, rather than a measured treatment effect (p599). Here meaningful outcomes include time to a newly diagnosed age-related condition, time to a second or third disease, or survival without disease or disability. Follow-up must be long enough for these events to occur. If an intervention is already effective against one component of a composite, inspect results for the individual components. A favorable composite driven entirely by diabetes, for example, would not establish comparable effects on mobility, cancer or cognitive decline. The chapter describes TAME as a trial design intended to address the translational and regulatory problem, not as completed efficacy evidence (p599–601).

A second question asks whether an intervention prevents or improves a geriatric syndrome. Choose direct measures of the relevant domain, such as mobility, gait speed, cognition, frailty or ability to perform daily activities. A patient may notice improved endurance without crossing a threshold for a new diagnosis. If the person already has severe disability, the endpoint might need to capture meaningful improvement or preservation rather than first onset of impairment. Conversely, a very healthy participant may have little room for measured improvement during short follow-up. Match the measurement to baseline function and to the aspect of healthspan that the intervention is expected to change (p599–601).

A third question concerns resilience after a defined stress. The same operation or acute illness can produce a modest temporary decline in one person and prolonged dependence in another. Assess function before the stress where possible, the extent of the decline, the time to recover and how close the person comes to their prior baseline. A single postoperative observation cannot distinguish a smaller initial decline from a faster recovery. Mortality alone misses an important difference between survivors returning home independently and survivors who require ongoing assistance. Figure 40-7 shows why serial assessment is informative even when the groups start at similar levels (p599–601).

### Which people should participate, and what constitutes benefit?

Including older adults is necessary but insufficient if a study systematically excludes the very patients seen in geriatric practice. Sex, race and ethnicity, socioeconomic conditions, multimorbidity, function and residence affect generalizability. Stratification by functional status or a validated risk measure may help disclose a benefit that differs across groups. Selecting people at appreciable baseline risk can make events observable in a feasible time. Excessive selection for illness can instead expose people with limited time to benefit to harm. Record why a particular group was recruited and whether the results reasonably apply to a community, hospital or nursing-facility patient (p600–601).

Table 40-4 includes mortality, disability-free survival, incident diseases, cognitive or physical impairment, syndromes and outcomes related to care and recovery. These measures need not all move together. Preventing a new diagnostic code while increasing falls or medication burden may not improve healthspan. A change in an investigator-selected composite may be hard for a patient to interpret unless the individual outcomes are presented. Standardized outcomes aid comparison across studies, but the practical meaning of a gain in walking ability can differ between an independently living person and a resident already requiring help with transfers (p600–601).

### Distinguish a predictor from a validated treatment surrogate

Table 40-5 starts with a biomarker's association with chronological age and long-term mortality. It next asks whether it captures several aging-related outcomes, can change over time, and whether change predicts a change in outcome risk. The most demanding criterion is evidence that it mediates the effect of an intervention already shown to improve clinical outcomes. A marker may predict who is at high risk without being suitable for deciding whether a treatment works. A fall in an inflammatory marker after treatment could demonstrate a biological effect while leaving the rate of disability or disease unchanged (p601–602).

A panel can combine signals from distinct systems and may be more informative than a single marker. In the Cardiovascular Health Study example, a composite of **five circulating biomarkers** had a mortality hazard ratio of **1.30 per point** with a **95% confidence interval of 1.25–1.34**, and attenuated the age–mortality association by **25%**. These are prognostic relationships within a particular model. They do not mean that reducing the score by one point with a drug will lower mortality by the same proportion. To evaluate that claim would require evidence about changes in the score during an intervention and the resulting clinical outcomes (p602).

Telomere measurements have substantial variability, limiting their individual clinical application. DNA methylation clocks are calibrated against different targets, from chronological age to risk-related phenotypes. Two clocks can therefore respond differently to the same intervention without either being a direct measure of years gained. In the CALERIE post-hoc analysis, a composite physiological estimate advanced less over **2 years** in participants assigned to restriction. Distinguish a modeled change from directly observing longer survival without disability. Review how a score was derived, whether it was validated outside its development sample, and which as-yet-unmeasured clinical effect is being inferred (p602).

It helps to place three study results side by side. A measurement of a molecular target before and after dosing can establish target engagement. A baseline biomarker associated with future disease can help select a higher-risk population for a subsequent trial. A randomized trial that demonstrates longer disability-free survival answers a clinical question, provided harms are also measured. None of these designs substitutes for the others. Combining them can strengthen interpretation if the target, marker and outcome are linked in the same population and time frame; combining unrelated associations across different populations does not demonstrate mediation (p599–602).

Chronological age itself is a good reminder of the distinction. It predicts many adverse events, but it cannot change in response to treatment. A clinical trial needs an outcome or measure that can respond and that has a defined direction of benefit. For a patient, remaining able to shop or dress can be meaningful even when the precise biological mechanism is uncertain. For validating a biomarker, that uncertainty still matters and should be described separately (p600–602).

### Counseling about a marketed intervention

Begin with what the patient wants: less fatigue, preservation of independence, fewer illnesses or longer life. Then identify what the product has actually been shown to do in humans resembling that patient. Being sold without a prescription, being repurposed from another disease, or having an effect in an animal experiment does not settle dose, interactions, purity, duration or net benefit for an older adult. A qualifying clinical trial provides defined eligibility, monitoring and comparison; a commercial clinic offering an age estimate or a treatment package does not by itself supply that evidence. Review current medicines, costs and whether the proposed product would displace established care (p602–603).

The major barriers are practical as well as biological. Clinical studies need an accepted outcome, enough events or an appropriate stressor, a way to measure biological effects, and investigators familiar with typical older patients. A treatment acting on an adaptive response could be harmful, or a seemingly favorable mechanism might matter only in a narrow disease state. For the reader, the most productive interpretation is specific: which mechanism was affected, in whom, for how long, with which clinical result and with what safety information? Those details determine what can be said to a patient today and which question remains for a future trial (p599–603).

## Exam traps

- Mouse survival, human vaccine response and human survival are separate endpoints (p597–600).
- A marker correlated with chronological age may represent adaptation, injury or disease rather than a cause of aging (p601–603).
- An open-label disease pilot offers limited evidence when deciding whether an intervention benefits the general older population (p599).
