# Chapter 20 Study: The Role of Social Workers

## Summary

**What social workers contribute**
- Social workers assess social problems, support coping, mediate conflict and obtain resources for patients and families; geriatric expertise is not universal among social workers (p277).
- Direct work includes counseling, therapy, education and groups. Support groups can help caregivers understand illness and cope with its effects (p277-278).
- Linkage work connects patients with services and addresses lack of information, access barriers, stigma and fears about accepting help (p278).
- Case management assesses needs, coordinates delivery and reassesses whether services remain useful as circumstances change (p278-279).

**Match help to the setting**
- Home care addresses adaptation, caregiving, safety, finances, medication misuse, abuse and end-of-life issues; services vary across agencies (p279).
- Community agencies combine direct support, mental health care, education, service development and policy roles (p280).
- Social work in nursing homes and assisted living involves psychosocial assessment, adjustment support, resident advocacy and work with families and staff; assisted living does not require a staff social worker, and roles vary more than in skilled nursing facilities (p280).
- In acute care, social workers screen for risk, counsel patients and families, and help plan discharge (p280-281).
- Hospice/palliative social work addresses loss, family communication, psychosocial and spiritual needs, transitions and bereavement (p281).

**Vulnerability and assessment**
- Explore culture and within-group differences rather than assuming shared needs. Poverty, immigration/language barriers, discrimination and sexual or gender identity can affect access and trust (p281-283).
- In the United States, more than **50%** of people living with HIV are **50 or older**; late testing may leave newly diagnosed older adults facing more immediate challenges (p283).
- Abuse may require repeated contacts to detect. Reporting duties and procedures vary locally; assess risk and work with protective services when indicated (p283-284).
- Cognitive assessment includes function, living conditions, family dynamics, resources and legal/advance-planning needs; provide stage-appropriate dementia support (p284).
- Mental illness and substance misuse can impair independence. Do not automatically attribute symptoms to age, dementia or depression; consider isolation, pain and losses (p285).

### From identifying a need to getting help that lasts

A referral succeeds only if the service can be used. A patient may understand that rehabilitation would help yet lack transportation or fear that accepting a home visit will cost them their independence. The broker identifies an appropriate resource and helps the patient reach it. The case manager links several services, monitors what happens and revises the plan when needs or eligibility change (p278–279).

At hospital discharge the relevant question is what the patient and family can manage at home. A list of available services does not establish caregiver availability or ability to use them. In hospice the same communication skills serve a different purpose: helping people express divergent wishes, navigate transitions and prepare for bereavement (p280–281).

Look for neglect in the setting as well as on examination. Pressure wounds, poor nutrition and unsanitary living conditions warrant further assessment. Fear of discrimination can also make a person reluctant to disclose relationships or seek services, particularly on entering a residential facility (p282–284).

### Identify the social problem that is changing the clinical outcome

An older adult repeatedly misses appointments after discharge. The immediate chart label may be “did not attend,” but that observation gives no explanation. The person may lack transport, be unable to leave the apartment, misunderstand whether the visit is needed, or fear that a professional who sees their living conditions will recommend placement. A social worker can explore what the appointment means to the patient, what practical resources exist and which obstacle is actually preventing access. Each explanation leads to a different intervention, and adding another appointment alone addresses none of them (p277–279).

Relatives, neighbors and other informal helpers are part of the assessment. Ask who the patient identifies as supportive, what those people already do and whether that support can continue. The person named as next of kin may live far away or have competing care obligations. A neighbor who shops regularly may be able to notice a change but be unable to provide personal care. The social worker can build on what the network already does, mediate competing expectations and connect the patient to formal services where gaps remain (p277–279).

Working frequently with older patients is different from training in gerontological social work. The chapter reports that **76%** of social workers in health care settings work with older patients, while emphasizing that not all have specialist geriatric training. The relevant expertise includes understanding how disability, cognition, family roles, benefits and institutional transitions affect each other. When making a referral, describe the question: help with adjustment after bereavement, concern about financial exploitation, or coordination of several home services calls for different skills (p277–278).

### Direct service: counseling, education and group work

Direct service helps the person or family address a problem through interaction with the social worker. After a diagnosis that threatens independence, counseling may focus on grief, changing identity or conflict about accepting help. Therapy, when appropriate to the clinician's training and the patient's needs, can address a mental health problem. Education can make unfamiliar medical processes understandable enough for the patient to participate. None of these activities requires that the person's difficulty be explained exclusively by a psychiatric diagnosis. A reasonable emotional response to loss can coexist with practical barriers requiring separate action (p277–278).

Table 20-1 distinguishes a group worker, educator, advocate and case manager, among other roles. A group can be educational, therapeutic, social or focused on support. Consider a person caring for a spouse with dementia who has interpreted behavioral changes as deliberate hostility. Information about the illness addresses one need; learning how other caregivers respond addresses a second; contact with people in a similar situation addresses isolation. A group alone will not provide respite or nighttime assistance, so assess whether the caregiver also needs a service or a change in the care arrangement (p277–279, p284).

An educator gives usable information: what a service does, how a transition will occur and what choices the patient can make. An advocate helps preserve the patient's perspective when an organization, family or funding rule points toward a plan the patient does not want. For example, a recommendation for residential care may reflect an assumption that no home support exists. The advocate can establish whether a feasible home option has been explored. Advocacy does not require promising a preferred outcome when the necessary service is unavailable or cannot make the situation safe (p278–279).

Mediation is valuable when family members disagree about the meaning of a recommendation. One relative may equate seeking help with abandonment, another with protection, while the patient sees it as a threat to privacy. Elicit what each person is trying to preserve and identify the concrete tasks that have become difficult. A conversation about who can help with meals and transportation may be more productive than a general argument about whether the patient is “independent.” Return to the patient's priorities and preserve a way to reassess the arrangement if needs change (p277–279).

### Linkage, case management and barriers to use

Brokerage connects an identified need to a resource. To make a useful referral, the worker needs to know the service's actual eligibility criteria, cost, geographical reach and practical availability. The patient may need help making the first contact or understanding what information the agency requests. Lack of information is only one barrier. If the transport service stops before the patient's return journey or the service user cannot communicate in its working language, a successful telephone referral has not established access (p278–279).

Case management is necessary when several services depend on one another. A patient may need a rehabilitation appointment, a means of reaching it, someone to assist with transfers and a mental health contact after a difficult loss. The case manager assesses needs, arranges and coordinates the services, follows whether they were actually delivered and revises the plan. Check what happened from the patient's perspective: a home visit that arrived when the person was at therapy may count as an attempted service for the agency but delivered no help (p278–279).

Assess stigma and previous experience before interpreting refusal. Some people worry that accepting help will expose a mental illness, disclose family conflict or lead inevitably to loss of their home. Others have experienced discrimination by institutions and have good reasons for caution. Explain the proposed worker's role and the specific issue that can be addressed, then ask what support the person would find acceptable. A patient may decline one agency but accept a different arrangement that meets the same need and preserves important relationships or privacy (p278–279, p281–283).

### How the work changes by setting

A home visit reveals the environment in which the plan must work. The social worker may find that stairs, a lack of food, inability to afford medication or an exhausted caregiver is more important to the current problem than the medical team's initial explanation. Assess adaptation to illness, safety, finances, relationships, substance use and end-of-life concerns as appropriate. Table 20-2 links these activities to setting. A home health agency may offer a narrower set of visits than a community organization, so identify which needs can be addressed during the episode and who can remain involved afterward (p279–280).

Community health and mental health agencies may combine counseling and case management with outreach, group programs, education, planning or policy work. A patient isolated by mobility problems may never appear at a clinic to request help, so community-based contact can change who obtains care. At the program level, repeated difficulty obtaining transportation for many patients can signal a gap in local services rather than many unrelated individual failures. A social worker can address the immediate referral and contribute to developing a more accessible service for others (p279–280).

Long-term residential care introduces a different source of distress: an institution has become the person's home. Ask which routines, relationships and choices matter and what changed at admission. The social worker can help a resident adapt, advocate when preferences are overlooked, and support families who feel grief or guilt about placement. Staff may interpret withdrawal as a fixed characteristic unless someone learns that the person stopped participating after losing contact with an important companion. Work with the facility and the family to bring that information into the care plan (p280).

During hospital discharge planning, the social worker assesses caregiving needs, available support, financial barriers and the home environment. Determine the previous living situation, what has changed functionally, whether a caregiver is willing and able to help, and which equipment or services can actually be obtained. If relatives disagree about destination, assess whether the disagreement concerns safety, finances, values or an unrealistic assumption about the help one person can provide. Placement, home support and rehabilitation are distinct options. A safe move requires communication with the agency or team receiving the patient (p280–281).

Hospice and palliative settings bring anticipatory loss, family communication, spiritual concerns, practical planning and bereavement to the foreground. Table 20-3 includes helping patients and families understand options, express preferences and access needed support. Someone may wish to remain at home while a partner fears being unable to manage symptoms overnight. Both positions should be heard. Explore what assistance is available and whether it addresses the specific concern. A preference for dying at home is meaningful without being sufficient proof that such a plan is safe or sustainable (p281).

### Culture, poverty and relationships that services can overlook

Culturally responsive assessment starts by asking, not assigning a preference from the patient's perceived group membership. Language, migration history, education, family structure, religion and experiences of discrimination may differ even between patients who identify with the same community. Ask how the person prefers decisions to be discussed and whom they wish to include. Avoid making a relative responsible for complex communication simply because that person is present. Consider whether the offered service will be understandable, trusted and acceptable to this individual (p281–282).

Poverty can impose choices invisible in the clinical record. A person may understand the instructions but be unable to afford transportation, food, housing or medication at the same time. Financial strain can also affect a family member's ability to reduce paid work to provide care. Ask about concrete tradeoffs without assuming that failure to follow a plan reflects low motivation. Determine which benefit, service or advocacy would change the limiting resource, and reassess whether the patient's circumstances improved after the referral (p281–282).

Older immigrants and refugees may face language barriers, unfamiliar services, interrupted support networks or differing expectations of formal care. Migration experience can include loss or trauma that a routine social history misses. At the same time, cultural knowledge, family ties and community organizations can be sources of resilience. Identify the language the person uses most comfortably, who already provides trustworthy support and whether the patient knows how to reach an appropriate service. Immigration category alone does not establish the person's beliefs, eligibility or preference for family involvement (p282).

Sexual orientation and gender identity can affect disclosure, trust and access, particularly on entering a residential setting. The person who provides daily care may be a partner or chosen family member whom the service has not recognized. Ask privately which relationships matter and whom the patient wants included, and avoid disclosing personal information more widely than the patient intends. A reluctance to discuss an important relationship can reflect prior discrimination. Respectful inquiry can reveal a strong source of support that a narrowly defined family history would otherwise miss (p282–283).

The chapter notes that **more than half of Americans living with HIV are aged 50 or older**. Some have lived with infection and its social consequences for many years, while others are newly diagnosed after delayed testing. These circumstances can involve different losses, stigma, treatment needs and support networks. Ask about the meaning of the diagnosis, the availability of help and whether confidentiality concerns are interfering with care. Do not presume that an older patient has no sexual health concerns or that everyone around the patient already knows the diagnosis (p283).

One way to detect overlapping barriers is to ask who the patient would actually call if the plan failed this evening. The answer may reveal that the identified caregiver does not speak the service's language, cannot enter the patient's building, or does not know the diagnosis. Clarify with the patient which information may be shared, what support each person has agreed to provide and how a gap can be filled. This is especially important at transitions, when several organizations may assume someone else has already established these facts (p278–283).

### Recognizing possible harm without deciding too early who caused it

Table 20-4 distinguishes physical abuse, psychological abuse, sexual abuse, financial exploitation and neglect, including self-neglect. A person with pressure wounds, poor hygiene, inadequate intake or unsafe housing requires assessment of needs and safety, but one finding alone rarely identifies its cause. Disability, limited resources, inadequate caregiving, self-neglect or deliberate mistreatment may produce overlapping signs. Document the observation and clarify what help was required, who agreed to provide it and what actually occurred. A discrepancy between accounts warrants further investigation without automatically establishing intent (p283–284).

Financial exploitation may have fewer obvious physical signs. Ask about sudden difficulty paying for necessities, control of money and whether transactions reflect the person's wishes. A relative who helps with banking can be a valued support, a source of risk, or both in different respects. Interview the patient in circumstances that permit an account free of intimidation. If the person is afraid that disclosure will lead to loss of the only available caregiver, the safety plan must address the dependence as well as the suspected exploitation (p283–284).

The chapter emphasizes that detection can take repeated contacts. A brief encounter in the presence of a possible perpetrator may not elicit a disclosure. Use the multidisciplinary team and appropriate protective services, recognizing that reporting duties and procedures vary by jurisdiction. Immediate safety, the person's ability to participate, the reliability of available support and the risk of retaliation all matter to the response. Follow up rather than treating the act of making a referral as proof that the person is now protected (p283–284).

### Cognitive impairment, mental illness and substance use

When cognition declines, assess function in its usual context. Who manages meals, appointments and money? Which errors are new, and what support is already compensating for them? Education can help family members interpret disease-related behavior, while practical planning addresses safety, caregiver stress and future decision making. A person may need help with complex arrangements yet still express priorities about daily care, relationships and place of residence. Tailor support to the current stage and revisit the plan as abilities and caregiver resources change (p284).

Table 20-5 brings together conditions that can complicate assessment, including dementia, depression, anxiety, substance use and longstanding severe mental illness or developmental disability. The chapter gives a general depression prevalence of about **5%**, but rates up to **44%** in some homebound and socially isolated populations. These are estimates for different groups, not two competing answers about a patient's diagnosis. Depression may present with cognitive and affective concerns and anxiety as restlessness or trouble concentrating. Ask about onset, distress and functional change before attributing the presentation to age or dementia (p284–285).

Alcohol, prescribed psychoactive agents and other substances deserve sensitive, specific questioning. Widowhood, retirement, isolation, pain and disability can change both substance use and its consequences. Falls or confusion may be the first visible problem. A person may be using prescribed medicines for a genuine symptom while experiencing harm from a combination, particularly when alcohol or other sedatives are involved. Communicate the concrete exposure and harm to the medical team and assess the social or emotional issue the person is trying to manage. This permits a safer plan without dismissing the original symptom (p284–285).

Across these situations, good follow-up tests whether the intervention changed the problem the patient identified. Does the person now reach appointments, feel safe discussing a relationship, have help preparing meals, or know whom to contact during a caregiving crisis? An agency may record a completed assessment while the patient remains unable to use a service. The social worker's role includes noticing that difference, revising the plan with the patient and bringing the result back to the clinical team (p277–281, p284–285).

## Exam traps

- A named service may be inaccessible because of transportation, eligibility, stigma or fear of losing control (p278–279).
- A warning sign of neglect prompts assessment. It does not by itself identify who caused it (p283–284).
- People can share an ethnic background and differ substantially in language, resources, migration history and preferences (p281–282).

## Recall drill

**1. What can a social worker add when clinicians and relatives disagree about discharge?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Explore the patient's goals and actual supports, facilitate communication and mediate disagreement while advocating for the patient's needs. (p277–278, p280–281)</details>

**2. How does direct counseling differ from brokerage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Counseling helps the person or family cope with a problem. Brokerage connects the person to the service or resource that can meet a need. (p277–278)</details>

**3. What does a group add for someone caring for a relative with dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Education about the illness, contact with peers facing similar issues and support in coping with its effects. (p277–278)</details>

**4. A patient needs rehabilitation, psychotherapy and transportation. Which role coordinates the whole plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Case management: assess needs and eligibility, arrange and coordinate services, monitor delivery and reassess as conditions change. (p278–279)</details>

**5. Why follow up after making a referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cost, lack of information, stigma or access barriers may prevent use. Confirm what happened and adjust the arrangement if it failed. (p278–279)</details>

**6. Which concerns become more apparent during a home visit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Home conditions, practical safety, caregiver demands, finances, medication use and how the patient adapts to illness in daily life. (p279)</details>

**7. How can a community social worker reach people beyond those receiving individual counseling?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Through community education, consultation, group programs, service development and work on local policy. (pp279–280)</details>

**8. What should be established before relying on a family discharge plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>What care is needed, who is available and able to provide it, and what additional services can actually be arranged. (p280–281)</details>

**9. What can social work contribute after a person enters long-term care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assess adjustment and psychosocial needs, advocate for the resident and work with staff and family as care needs or relationships change. (p280)</details>

**10. Which hospice tasks extend beyond locating services?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Facilitating family communication, addressing emotional and spiritual concerns, supporting transitions and helping relatives through loss and bereavement. (p281)</details>

**11. Why ask an immigrant about language and personal history instead of inferring needs from nationality?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Experiences vary with language proficiency, reasons for migration, resources and time spent in the country. Those details change what help is accessible. (p281–282)</details>

**12. Why might someone entering residential care conceal a long-standing relationship?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Prior discrimination or fear of unequal treatment can make disclosure feel unsafe. Ask whom the person wants involved and protect their privacy. (p282–283)</details>

**13. Which warning signs might prompt assessment of neglect?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Poor nutrition or hygiene, pressure wounds, inadequate access to medical care or unsafe living conditions. Explore circumstances, safety and available support. (p283–284)</details>

**14. What does a dementia assessment need beyond a cognitive test?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Information about function at home, the physical and social environment, family relationships, available resources and legal or advance-planning needs. (p284)</details>

**15. Why explore alcohol or other substance use in a newly isolated older adult?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Loss, pain or isolation may contribute to misuse. Changes in mood or function can otherwise be misattributed to aging or cognitive illness. (p285)</details>
