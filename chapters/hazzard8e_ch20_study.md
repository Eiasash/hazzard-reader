# Chapter 20 Study: The Role of Social Workers

## Summary

### What social workers contribute
- Social workers assess social problems, support coping, mediate conflict and obtain resources for patients and families; geriatric expertise is not universal among social workers (p277).
- Direct work includes counseling, therapy, education and groups. Support groups can help caregivers understand illness and cope with its effects (p277-278).
- Linkage work connects patients with services and addresses lack of information, access barriers, stigma and fears about accepting help (p278).
- Case management assesses needs, coordinates delivery and reassesses whether services remain useful as circumstances change (p278-279).

### Match help to the setting
- Home care addresses adaptation, caregiving, safety, finances, medication misuse, abuse and end-of-life issues; services vary across agencies (p279).
- Community agencies combine direct support, mental health care, education, service development and policy roles (p280).
- Nursing homes and assisted living require psychosocial assessment, adjustment support, resident advocacy and work with families and staff (p280).
- Hospital discharge planning starts early; assess practical capacity and connect the patient with appropriate community or institutional support (p280-281).
- Hospice/palliative social work addresses loss, family communication, psychosocial and spiritual needs, transitions and bereavement (p281).

### Vulnerability and assessment
- Explore culture and within-group differences rather than assuming shared needs. Poverty, immigration/language barriers, discrimination and sexual or gender identity can affect access and trust (p281-283).
- In the United States, more than **50%** of people living with HIV are **50 or older**; late testing may leave newly diagnosed older adults facing more immediate challenges (p283).
- Abuse may require repeated contacts to detect. Reporting duties and procedures vary locally; assess risk and work with protective services when indicated (p283-284).
- Cognitive assessment includes function, living conditions, family dynamics, resources and legal/advance-planning needs; provide stage-appropriate dementia support (p284).
- Mental illness and substance misuse can impair independence. Do not automatically attribute symptoms to age, dementia or depression; consider isolation, pain and losses (p285).

### From identifying a need to getting help that lasts

A referral succeeds only if the service can be used. A patient may understand that rehabilitation would help yet lack transportation or fear that accepting a home visit will cost them their independence. The broker identifies an appropriate resource and helps the patient reach it. The case manager links several services, monitors what happens and revises the plan when needs or eligibility change (p278–279).

At hospital discharge the relevant question is what the patient and family can manage at home. A list of available services does not establish caregiver availability or ability to use them. In hospice the same communication skills serve a different purpose: helping people express divergent wishes, navigate transitions and prepare for bereavement (p280–281).

Look for neglect in the setting as well as on examination. Pressure wounds, poor nutrition and unsanitary living conditions warrant further assessment. Financial exploitation may have few physical signs. Fear of discrimination can also make a person reluctant to disclose relationships or seek services, particularly on entering a residential facility (p282–284).

## Exam traps

- A named service may be inaccessible because of transportation, eligibility, stigma or fear of losing control (p278–279).
- A warning sign of neglect prompts assessment. It does not by itself identify who caused it (p283–284).
- People can share an ethnic background and differ substantially in language, resources, migration history and preferences (p281–282).

## Recall drill

**1. What can a social worker add when clinicians and relatives disagree about discharge?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Explore the patient's goals and actual supports, facilitate communication and mediate disagreement while advocating for the patient's needs. (p277–278, p280–281)</details>

**2. How does direct counseling differ from brokerage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Counseling helps the person or family cope with a problem. Brokerage connects the person to the service or resource that can meet a need. (p277–278)</details>

**3. What does a group add for someone caring for a relative with dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Education about the illness, contact with peers facing similar issues and support in coping with its effects. (p277–278)</details>

**4. A patient needs rehabilitation, psychotherapy and transportation. Which role coordinates the whole plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Case management: assess needs and eligibility, arrange and coordinate services, monitor delivery and reassess as conditions change. (p278–279)</details>

**5. Why follow up after making a referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cost, lack of information, stigma or access barriers may prevent use. Confirm what happened and adjust the arrangement if it failed. (p278–279)</details>

**6. Which concerns become more apparent during a home visit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Home conditions, practical safety, caregiver demands, finances, medication use and how the patient adapts to illness in daily life. (p279)</details>

**7. How can a community social worker reach people beyond those receiving individual counseling?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Through community education, consultation, group programs, service development and work on local policy. (p280)</details>

**8. What should be established before relying on a family discharge plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>What care is needed, who is available and able to provide it, and what additional services can actually be arranged. (p280–281)</details>

**9. What can social work contribute after a person enters long-term care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assess adjustment and psychosocial needs, advocate for the resident and work with staff and family as care needs or relationships change. (p280)</details>

**10. Which hospice tasks extend beyond locating services?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Facilitating family communication, addressing emotional and spiritual concerns, supporting transitions and helping relatives through loss and bereavement. (p281)</details>

**11. Why ask an immigrant about language and personal history instead of inferring needs from nationality?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Experiences vary with language proficiency, reasons for migration, resources and time spent in the country. Those details change what help is accessible. (p281–282)</details>

**12. Why might someone entering residential care conceal a long-standing relationship?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Prior discrimination or fear of unequal treatment can make disclosure feel unsafe. Ask whom the person wants involved and protect their privacy. (p282–283)</details>

**13. Which warning signs might prompt assessment of neglect?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Poor nutrition or hygiene, pressure wounds, inadequate access to medical care or unsafe living conditions. Explore circumstances, safety and available support. (p283–284)</details>

**14. What does a dementia assessment need beyond a cognitive test?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Information about function at home, the physical and social environment, family relationships, available resources and legal or advance-planning needs. (p284)</details>

**15. Why explore alcohol or other substance use in a newly isolated older adult?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Loss, pain or isolation may contribute to misuse. Changes in mood or function can otherwise be misattributed to aging or cognitive illness. (p285)</details>
