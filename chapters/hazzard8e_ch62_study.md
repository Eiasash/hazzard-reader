# Cerebrovascular Disease

Start with the distribution and timing of the deficit. Localize the lesion, distinguish ischemia from hemorrhage, and use the mechanism to select treatment.

## Ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack

### Transient symptoms and early risk

An abrupt focal deficit demands urgent assessment even if it resolves. Under the tissue-based definition, a matching acute diffusion-weighted MRI lesion means infarction. Recurrent risk is concentrated early (<a href="?chapter=62#p953">pp953</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p957">957</a>).

The ABCD² thresholds in Hazzard are **age >60 years** (1 point) and **presenting BP >140/90** (1 point). Unilateral weakness scores 2, isolated speech disturbance 1, duration 10–59 minutes 1 or ≥60 minutes 2, and diabetes 1 (<a href="?chapter=62#p957">p957</a>). Applying the original validated criteria to the four presentations in [June 2022 Q91](?chapter=bank&q=mcq-c659381a9b8442881efcb5a1) gives scores of **3, 7, 4 and 5**, respectively.

> **Current practice: ABCD² thresholds and urgency of referral.** The original validated score gives a point for **age ≥60** and a point for **systolic BP ≥140 or diastolic BP ≥90**. NICE advises against using ABCD² to determine urgency of referral — [Johnston et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(07)60150-0), [NICE NG128, 1.1.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng128/chapter/Recommendations).

### Arterial, lacunar and cardioembolic mechanisms

**Artery-to-artery embolism** means thrombus forms on diseased arterial endothelium and travels downstream. An ulcerated internal carotid plaque can embolize at **50–70%** stenosis, although this is less common than with severe stenosis. Hypoperfusion requires inadequate collateral flow and may produce border-zone injury. A lacune is a small deep noncortical infarct in a perforator territory. Lipohyalinosis or atheroma near the perforator origin is usual. Embolism can occur. Pure motor or pure sensory syndromes without aphasia, neglect or a cortical field cut support the small-vessel mechanism (<a href="?chapter=62#p957">pp957</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p961">961</a>).

Cardioembolic deficits can be maximal at onset and affect more than one vascular territory. Atrial fibrillation is a major cause in older adults. A patent foramen ovale is common enough to be incidental. A venous embolus can cross it, and thrombus can also arise at the opening. A cryptogenic stroke warrants assessment of the cervical and intracranial vessels and heart rhythm before assigning a mechanism. Selected patients benefit from longer rhythm monitoring for intermittent AF (<a href="?chapter=62#p961">pp961</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p965">965</a>).

Determine ischemic mechanism: large-artery, lacunar, cardioembolic, cryptogenic or other. Etiology guides treatment and prevention (<a href="?chapter=62#p954">p954</a>). Distribution on brain imaging, stenosis or occlusion on head and neck vascular imaging, and evidence of a cardiac source on ECG, rhythm monitoring or selected echocardiography guide both treatment and prevention (<a href="?chapter=62#p954">p954</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p964">pp964</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p966">966</a>).

## Localizing a stroke

### Anterior and middle cerebral territories

<span data-study-phrase="aca">Start with the distribution of the deficit.</span> ACA ischemia affects the medial frontal lobe. Contralateral leg weakness usually exceeds arm weakness, and bilateral medial frontal injury can impair both legs, bladder control and initiation. A patient who seems apathetic as well as weak may have **abulia**, a marked reduction in self-initiated behavior. A patent anterior communicating artery may protect against proximal occlusion. An anatomical variant in which one A1 supplies both distal territories makes bilateral injury possible after a single occlusion (<a href="?chapter=62#p958">p958</a>).

An MCA stem occlusion may combine dense contralateral weakness and sensory loss with a gaze preference toward the injured hemisphere. In a smaller cortical branch infarct, the face and arm may be much weaker than the leg. Superior division injury on the language-dominant side can leave comprehension relatively intact while speech becomes effortful. Inferior division injury may produce fluent speech with poor comprehension and little weakness. In a right-handed patient, a large right MCA stroke more often disrupts attention to the left than language. A homonymous visual field defect can occur after an MCA stroke on either side. Neglect is failure to attend, even when visual or tactile input is detectable (<a href="?chapter=62#p955">pp955</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p958">958</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#book-figure-62-1">Figures 62-1</a> and <a href="?chapter=62#book-figure-62-2">62-2</a>).

### Language and learned movement

<span data-study-phrase="speech">At the bedside, ask for a spontaneous sentence, give a simple command, and ask the patient to repeat a phrase.</span> Halting speech with reasonably preserved comprehension and poor repetition fits Broca aphasia. Fluent speech with preserved comprehension but strikingly poor repetition points toward conduction aphasia. Wernicke aphasia impairs comprehension despite fluent output. In global aphasia, both speech production and comprehension are severely affected. A single invented word carries less localizing weight than the whole language examination (Stroke Rehabilitation Clinician Handbook 2020, ch. 1, <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-aphasia">pp11</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#ch62-aphasia">14</a>).

A dominant-hemisphere stroke can cause apraxia. Ask the person to pantomime using a familiar tool, allowing for weakness, comprehension and sensation. Someone who knows what is being asked may still be unable to organize the learned gesture. Anosognosia means unawareness of a deficit. Astereognosis means inability to identify a familiar object by touch despite preserved elementary sensation. Neither is synonymous with apraxia (May 2024 atlas, <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-praxis">Figure 5</a>, handbook <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-praxis">pp9</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#ch62-praxis">11</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-praxis">15</a>).

### Crossed signs and visual fields

A deficit on one side of the face or in one cranial nerve combined with weakness or sensory loss in the opposite limbs suggests a brainstem lesion. Ipsilateral failure of abduction and contralateral weakness localize to the lower pons. An ipsilateral third-nerve palsy with contralateral weakness points higher, to the midbrain. Vertigo, dysphagia, hoarseness, ipsilateral facial and contralateral body sensory changes suggest lateral medullary ischemia. Occipital PCA infarction instead produces a contralateral homonymous field defect (<a href="?chapter=62#p959">pp959</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p960">960</a>, handbook <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-crossed">p17</a>).

## Evaluation and reperfusion

### Brain and vessel imaging

Obtain urgent brain and vascular imaging. Noncontrast CT rapidly excludes hemorrhage; a negative early CT does not exclude infarction (<a href="?chapter=62#p953">p953</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p954">954</a>). MRI diffusion imaging is more sensitive for acute infarction; CTA/MRA identifies major arterial disease and large-vessel occlusion (<a href="?chapter=62#p953">p953</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p965">p965</a>). CT can identify a large established infarct. Perfusion imaging can distinguish irreversibly injured core from salvageable penumbra in selected late or unknown-onset presentations. Establish the last-known-well time, remaining disability, baseline function, anticoagulant exposure and bleeding risk. Advanced age alone does not exclude an otherwise eligible patient from reperfusion (<a href="?chapter=62#p953">pp953</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p955">955</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p964">964</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p969">969</a>).

In an otherwise unexplained recurrent arterial stroke, a persistent low platelet count can suggest an acquired prothrombotic disorder. Consider antiphospholipid antibody testing in selected cryptogenic strokes; antiphospholipid syndrome can cause thrombocytopenia. Palpitations instead suggest rhythm monitoring, while fever and systemic signs may prompt an endocarditis evaluation. Other less common mechanisms include arterial dissection, giant cell arteritis, other vasculitides, cerebral venous thrombosis, cancer-associated thrombosis and hereditary arteriopathies. The accompanying symptoms and the pattern on vessel imaging guide investigation (<a href="?chapter=62#p962">pp962</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p965">965</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p971">971</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p974">974</a>, <a href="?chapter=96#book-table-96-3">Table 96-3</a> <a href="?chapter=96#p1513">p1513</a>).

### Intravenous thrombolysis

Alteplase given earlier offers a greater opportunity to preserve function. For eligible patients the printed IV regimen is **0.9 mg/kg**, with **10% as an immediate bolus** and the remainder infused **over one hour** (<a href="?chapter=62#p966">p966</a>).

> **Current practice: alteplase maximum dose and bolus duration.** The US Activase label specifies **90 mg maximum** and gives the 10% bolus **over one minute** — [Activase prescribing information](https://www.gene.com/download/pdf/activase_prescribing.pdf).

The standard IV alteplase window extends to **4.5 hours** in selected patients, including those presenting at **3–4.5 hours**. For some wake-up or extended-window strokes, tissue imaging helps identify salvageable brain (<a href="?chapter=62#p967">p967</a>). After IV thrombolysis, keep BP below **180/105 mm Hg for at least 24 hours** and obtain follow-up CT or MRI. Antithrombotics are generally withheld until hemorrhage is excluded. Worsening headache, reduced alertness or a new deficit warrants immediate assessment for bleeding rather than waiting for the routine scan (<a href="?chapter=62#p966">pp966</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p970">970</a>).

### Thrombectomy and swelling

Vessel location, disabling deficit, infarct volume and salvageable tissue guide thrombectomy selection. DAWN studied carefully selected anterior circulation occlusions **6–24 hours** after the patient was last known well. Favorable imaging does not justify delay. Older age alone does not negate reperfusion benefit (<a href="?chapter=62#p968">pp968</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p969">969</a>).

In large hemispheric or cerebellar infarcts, edema can threaten life. Declining consciousness, brainstem compression or hydrocephalus warrants urgent reassessment and consideration of decompression in selected patients (<a href="?chapter=62#p974">pp974</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p976">976</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p979">979</a>).

## Preventing another stroke

### Antithrombotic treatment

Aspirin **50–325 mg/day** is commonly used after noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or TIA when appropriate. Consider bleeding history and the post-thrombolysis restriction (<a href="?chapter=62#p970">pp970</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p971">971</a>). Short dual antiplatelet treatment can reduce early recurrence after selected minor ischemic strokes or high-risk TIAs. Prolonged treatment raises bleeding risk; after lacunar stroke long-term aspirin plus clopidogrel increased bleeding and death without superior prevention (<a href="?chapter=62#p973">p973</a>).

AF usually calls for anticoagulation when safe after infarction. Empiric anticoagulation for embolic stroke of undetermined source did not outperform aspirin. NAVIGATE ESUS found excess major bleeding with rivaroxaban (<a href="?chapter=62#p974">p974</a>).

### Vascular risk and stenosis

Assess severe symptomatic carotid stenosis promptly for revascularization. Intracranial stenosis generally starts with intensive medical treatment. Control blood pressure, treat diabetes, support exercise and smoking cessation, and use statin therapy where indicated. After recent stroke or TIA with atherosclerotic disease, Treat Stroke to Target supports LDL below **70 mg/dL** (<a href="?chapter=62#p970">pp970</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p975">975</a>).

## Intracranial hemorrhage and complications

### Deep and lobar hemorrhage

Deep hemorrhage most commonly reflects hypertensive disease of small penetrating arteries in the putamen, thalamus, pons or cerebellum. Recurrent spontaneous lobar hemorrhage in an older adult instead suggests amyloid deposition in cortical and leptomeningeal vessels. Susceptibility-weighted MRI can show further small lobar bleeds. A hemorrhagic metastasis or malformation remains possible when the history and imaging suggest it. Sudden onset alone cannot distinguish ischemia from bleeding. Obtain urgent brain imaging (<a href="?chapter=62#p975">pp975</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p977">977</a>).

Assess location, mass effect and anticoagulant exposure promptly. A stable repeat scan does not reverse warfarin. For warfarin-associated intracerebral hemorrhage the printed treatment is **IV vitamin K plus fresh frozen plasma/cryoprecipitate or PCC** (<a href="?chapter=62#p977">p977</a>).

> **Current practice: Warfarin reversal in intracerebral hemorrhage.** For spontaneous ICH on a vitamin K antagonist with INR ≥2.0, give four-factor PCC in preference to fresh frozen plasma, then IV vitamin K promptly to sustain reversal. PCC may also be reasonable at INR 1.3–1.9. Plasma is an alternative if PCC is unavailable. Cryoprecipitate alone does not replace the vitamin K–dependent factors. [AHA/ASA spontaneous ICH guideline, section 5.2.1, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000407).

### Seizures after stroke

An acute seizure can complicate a cortical infarct or a hemorrhage. Within 24 hours of onset, **8.4%** of patients with hemorrhagic stroke and **2.9%** of those with cerebral infarction had a seizure. Pooling infarction with transient ischemic attack lowered the latter figure to **2.4%**. An embolic infarct is one ischemic subtype; its seizure risk need not equal the pooled ischemic rate. Degenerative disease, including Alzheimer disease, is another cause in later life. A seizure soon after stroke and unprovoked epilepsy months later have different time courses. Treat a documented seizure. Routine primary antiseizure prophylaxis after spontaneous ICH is generally unnecessary (<a href="?chapter=62#p976">p976</a>, Harrison 21e <a href="?chapter=62#ch62-seizure">p3310</a>, [Szaflarski et al., Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5316476/)).

### Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage

A ruptured aneurysm may cause a sudden severe headache, vomiting, neck stiffness or reduced consciousness. Noncontrast head CT is the initial study. If CT is negative and suspicion for SAH remains high, Hazzard says **lumbar puncture must be performed** (<a href="?chapter=62#p977">pp977</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p978">978</a>).

> **Current practice: lumbar puncture after a negative CT.** After a negative CT performed within six hours and reported by a radiologist, NICE advises against routine LP. After a later negative CT, consider LP — [NICE NG228, recommendations 1.1.11–1.1.14](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng228/chapter/Recommendations).

After aneurysmal SAH, **rerupture is most frequent in the first 72 hours**. Vasospasm often develops on days **4–14** and may cause a new focal deficit. Delayed hydrocephalus can appear days to weeks later and may require **ventriculoperitoneal shunting** (<a href="?chapter=62#p978">p978</a>).

> **Current practice: drainage for delayed hydrocephalus.** For persistent or progressive symptoms with chronic hydrocephalus, NICE advises considering CSF drainage or diversion. If the likely benefit of permanent diversion is uncertain, consider a trial of temporary drainage — [NICE NG228, recommendations 1.3.3–1.3.5](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng228/chapter/Recommendations).

## Recovery and goals of care

Clarify the patient's pre-stroke function, goals and acceptable outcomes when discussing decompression, ventilatory support, feeding and rehabilitation (<a href="?chapter=62#p974">pp974</a>, <a href="?chapter=62#p976">976</a>–<a href="?chapter=62#p979">979</a>).

Plan recovery around the tasks that matter to the patient, with physical, occupational and speech-language assessment as indicated. Swallowing problems, aspiration, immobility, pressure injury, spasticity, shoulder problems and depression interfere with progress. For DVT prevention, the printed regimen uses subcutaneous heparin or pneumatic compression followed by **ambulation as soon as possible** (<a href="?chapter=62#p970">p970</a>).

> **Current practice: early mobilization and fluoxetine.** High-dose mobilization within the first 24 hours after acute ischemic stroke can worsen outcomes. Routine fluoxetine for motor recovery has not improved functional outcome and increases fracture risk — [AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline, 2026, Rehabilitation](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000513).

## Key numbers

- Stroke incidence and mortality rise with age. In the United States, about **66%** of patients hospitalized with stroke are **65 or older**, and **17%** of strokes occur at age **85 or older** (<a href="?chapter=62#p953">p953</a>).

- In clinical trials after SAH, oral nimodipine **60 mg every 4 hours** reduced delayed ischemia and mortality (<a href="?chapter=62#p978">p978</a>).
