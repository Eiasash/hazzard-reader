## Localizing weakness, neglect and language impairment

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-0ed5d2ae800de06734a63b64 mcq-b46cd8ffb128f21dac24b95e mcq-76c3fe4d0034388d48825975 mcq-1c216c76c7ca78a420618afe mcq-04094d640ab7256b38fe0c81 mcq-ce5e6c2fec8b48bc7e0444b6 mcq-f8e2b002ef51e8ed9620074a mcq-2bf17228f99344b4887b156b" data-exam-ids="mcq-87d97d9a7a60602c31a10797" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Start with the distribution of the deficit. ACA ischemia affects the medial frontal lobe. Contralateral leg weakness usually exceeds arm weakness, and bilateral medial frontal injury can impair both legs, bladder control and initiation. A patient who seems apathetic as well as weak may have **abulia**, a marked reduction in self-initiated behavior. A patent anterior communicating artery may protect against proximal occlusion. An anatomical variant in which one A1 supplies both distal territories makes bilateral injury possible after a single occlusion (p958).

An MCA stem occlusion may combine dense contralateral weakness and sensory loss with a gaze preference toward the injured hemisphere. In a smaller cortical branch infarct, the face and arm may be much weaker than the leg. Superior division injury on the language-dominant side can leave comprehension relatively intact while speech becomes effortful. Inferior division injury may produce fluent speech with poor comprehension and little weakness. In a right-handed patient, a large right MCA stroke more often disrupts attention to the left than language. A homonymous visual field defect can occur after an MCA stroke on either side. Neglect is failure to attend, even when visual or tactile input is detectable (pp955–958, Figures 62-1 and 62-2).

At the bedside, ask for a spontaneous sentence, give a simple command, and ask the patient to repeat a phrase. Halting speech with reasonably preserved comprehension and poor repetition fits Broca aphasia. Fluent speech with preserved comprehension but strikingly poor repetition points toward conduction aphasia. Wernicke aphasia impairs comprehension despite fluent output. In global aphasia, both speech production and comprehension are severely affected. A single invented word carries less localizing weight than the whole language examination (Stroke Rehabilitation Clinician Handbook 2020, ch. 1, pp11–14).

The May 2024 CT question tests dominant-hemisphere apraxia. Ask the person to pantomime using a familiar tool, allowing for weakness, comprehension and sensation. Someone who knows what is being asked may still be unable to organize the learned gesture. Anosognosia means unawareness of a deficit. Astereognosis means inability to identify a familiar object by touch despite preserved elementary sensation. Neither is synonymous with apraxia (May 2024 atlas, Figure 5, handbook pp9–11, 15).

A deficit on one side of the face or in one cranial nerve combined with weakness or sensory loss in the opposite limbs suggests a brainstem lesion. Ipsilateral failure of abduction and contralateral weakness localize to the lower pons. An ipsilateral third-nerve palsy with contralateral weakness points higher, to the midbrain. Vertigo, dysphagia, hoarseness, ipsilateral facial and contralateral body sensory changes suggest lateral medullary ischemia. Occipital PCA infarction instead produces a contralateral homonymous field defect (pp959–960, handbook p17).

## Choosing an ischemic mechanism and estimating early risk

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-fd265aa00a76f0d393b73d2a mcq-c659381a9b8442881efcb5a1" data-exam-ids="mcq-08ad58cb588514eb5cb52bcb" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

The same abrupt deficit can follow local small-vessel occlusion, a thrombus shed from an upstream plaque, or a cardiac embolus. CT or MRI identifies the distribution of injury. Head and neck vascular imaging identifies stenosis or occlusion, while ECG, rhythm monitoring and selected echocardiography assess a cardiac source. A transient deficit with a corresponding acute DWI lesion constitutes an infarct under the tissue-based definition. Resolution of symptoms calls for urgent evaluation because recurrent risk is concentrated early (pp953–957, 964–966).

**Artery-to-artery embolism** means thrombus forms on diseased arterial endothelium and travels downstream. An ulcerated internal carotid plaque can embolize at 50–70% stenosis, although this is less common than with severe stenosis. Hypoperfusion requires inadequate collateral flow and may produce border-zone injury. A lacune is a small deep noncortical infarct in a perforator territory. Lipohyalinosis or atheroma near the perforator origin is usual. Embolism can occur. Pure motor or pure sensory syndromes without aphasia, neglect or a cortical field cut support the small-vessel mechanism (pp957–961).

Cardioembolic deficits can be maximal at onset and affect more than one vascular territory. Atrial fibrillation is a major cause in older adults. A patent foramen ovale is common enough to be incidental. A venous embolus can cross it, and thrombus can also arise at the opening. A cryptogenic stroke warrants assessment of the cervical and intracranial vessels and heart rhythm before assigning a mechanism. Selected patients benefit from longer rhythm monitoring for intermittent AF (pp961–965).

Hazzard’s ABCD² thresholds are **age >60 years** (1 point) and **presenting BP >140/90** (1 point). Unilateral weakness scores 2, isolated speech disturbance 1, duration 10–59 minutes 1 or ≥60 minutes 2, and diabetes 1 (p957). In June 2022 Q91 the four scenarios score **3, 7, 4 and 5** under the original Johnston scoring system. The second and fourth options received official credit.

> Current practice (not the book): the original validated score gives a point for **age ≥60** and a point for **systolic BP ≥140 or diastolic BP ≥90**. NICE advises against using ABCD² to determine urgency of referral — [Johnston et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(07)60150-0), [NICE NG128, 1.1.6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng128/chapter/Recommendations).

In an otherwise unexplained recurrent arterial stroke, a persistent low platelet count can suggest an acquired prothrombotic disorder. Hazzard includes antiphospholipid antibody testing in selected cryptogenic strokes and lists antiphospholipid syndrome among causes of thrombocytopenia. Palpitations instead suggest rhythm monitoring, while fever and systemic signs may prompt an endocarditis evaluation. Other less common mechanisms include arterial dissection, giant cell arteritis, other vasculitides, cerebral venous thrombosis, cancer-associated thrombosis and hereditary arteriopathies. The accompanying symptoms and the pattern on vessel imaging guide investigation (pp962–965, 971–974, Table 96-3 p1513).

## Hemorrhage, seizures and deterioration during recovery

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-e6b1e9f6cadbead54a532673 mcq-e093e5a4b6fbeef11a52e246" data-exam-ids="mcq-452a5972839367e2dbab3541" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Deep hemorrhage most commonly reflects hypertensive disease of small penetrating arteries in the putamen, thalamus, pons or cerebellum. Recurrent spontaneous lobar hemorrhage in an older adult instead suggests amyloid deposition in cortical and leptomeningeal vessels. Susceptibility-weighted MRI can show further small lobar bleeds. A hemorrhagic metastasis or malformation remains possible when the history and imaging suggest it. Sudden onset alone cannot distinguish ischemia from bleeding. Obtain urgent brain imaging (pp975–977).

An acute seizure can complicate a cortical infarct or a hemorrhage. Within 24 hours of onset, 8.4% of patients with hemorrhagic stroke and 2.9% of those with cerebral infarction had a seizure. Pooling infarction with transient ischemic attack lowered the latter figure to 2.4%. Harrison's comparison uses embolic stroke as a subgroup. Degenerative disease, including Alzheimer disease, is another cause in later life. A seizure soon after stroke and unprovoked epilepsy months later have different time courses. Treat a documented seizure. Routine primary antiseizure prophylaxis after spontaneous ICH is generally unnecessary (p976, Harrison 21e p3310, [Szaflarski et al., Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5316476/)).

A ruptured aneurysm may cause a sudden severe headache, vomiting, neck stiffness or reduced consciousness. Noncontrast head CT is the initial study. If CT is negative and suspicion for SAH remains high, Hazzard says **lumbar puncture must be performed** (pp977–978).

> Current practice (not the book): after a negative CT performed within six hours and reported by a radiologist, NICE advises against routine LP. After a later negative CT, consider LP — [NICE NG228, recommendations 1.1.11–1.1.14](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng228/chapter/Recommendations).

After aneurysmal SAH, **rerupture is most frequent in the first 72 hours**. Vasospasm often develops on days 4–14 and may cause a new focal deficit. Delayed hydrocephalus can appear days to weeks later and may require **ventriculoperitoneal shunting** (p978).

> Current practice (not the book): for persistent or progressive symptoms with chronic hydrocephalus, NICE advises considering CSF drainage or diversion. If the likely benefit of permanent diversion is uncertain, consider a trial of temporary drainage — [NICE NG228, recommendations 1.3.3–1.3.5](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng228/chapter/Recommendations).

## Reperfusion, prevention and recovery

Rapid noncontrast CT excludes hemorrhage and can identify a large established infarct. CTA identifies a potentially treatable large-vessel occlusion. DWI is sensitive to early infarction, and perfusion imaging can help distinguish irreversibly injured core from salvageable penumbra in selected late or unknown-onset presentations. History must establish when the patient was last known well, what disability remains, baseline function, anticoagulant exposure and bleeding risks. In an otherwise eligible patient, advanced age alone is insufficient reason to withhold reperfusion (pp953–955, 964–969).

Earlier IV thrombolysis offers more opportunity to preserve function. Hazzard describes alteplase within the standard 4.5-hour window and separate imaging-selected extended-window trials. For thrombectomy, vessel location, disabling deficit, infarct volume and salvageable tissue determine eligibility. DAWN evaluated carefully selected anterior circulation occlusions 6–24 hours from last known well. The deadline is a limit for eligibility, never a reason to wait. Worsening headache, reduced alertness or a new deficit after a lytic requires immediate evaluation for intracranial bleeding, even before the routine follow-up scan (pp966–970).

For secondary prevention, match treatment to the mechanism. Antiplatelets are generally appropriate for noncardioembolic disease. Short-term dual therapy can reduce early recurrence after selected minor ischemic strokes or high-risk TIAs, but bleeding risk rises with prolonged treatment. A patient with AF usually needs anticoagulation when safe after infarction. Empiric anticoagulation for embolic stroke of undetermined source failed to outperform aspirin. NAVIGATE ESUS found excess major bleeding with rivaroxaban. Severe symptomatic carotid stenosis warrants prompt assessment for revascularization. Intracranial stenosis generally starts with intensive medical management. Blood pressure control, statin therapy where indicated, smoking cessation, exercise and diabetes care address recurrence across mechanisms (pp970–975).

In large hemispheric or cerebellar infarcts, edema can threaten life. Declining consciousness, brainstem compression or hydrocephalus warrants urgent reassessment and consideration of neurosurgical decompression in selected patients. In ICH, assess hematoma location, mass effect and anticoagulant exposure promptly. A stable repeat scan does not neutralize warfarin. For warfarin-associated ICH, Hazzard lists **IV vitamin K plus fresh frozen plasma/cryoprecipitate or PCC** (p977).

> Current practice (not the book): in VKA-associated spontaneous ICH with INR ≥2.0, four-factor PCC is preferred to FFP for rapid reversal, with IV vitamin K given directly after factor replacement — [AHA/ASA ICH guideline, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000407).

Clarify the patient's pre-stroke function, goals and acceptable outcomes when discussing decompression, ventilatory support, feeding and rehabilitation (pp974, 976–979).

Plan recovery around the tasks that matter to the patient, with physical, occupational and speech-language assessment as indicated. Swallowing problems, aspiration, immobility, pressure injury, spasticity, shoulder problems and depression interfere with progress. For DVT prevention, Hazzard describes subcutaneous heparin or pneumatic compression followed by **ambulation as soon as possible** (p970).

> Current practice (not the book): high-dose mobilization within the first 24 hours after acute ischemic stroke can worsen outcomes. Routine fluoxetine for motor recovery has not improved functional outcome and increases fracture risk — [AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline, 2026, Rehabilitation](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000513).

## Summary

**Mechanism & diagnosis**
- Determine ischemic mechanism: large-artery, lacunar, cardioembolic, cryptogenic or other. Etiology guides treatment and prevention (p954).
- Obtain urgent brain and vascular imaging. Noncontrast CT rapidly excludes hemorrhage; a negative early CT does not exclude infarction (p953–954).
- MRI diffusion imaging is more sensitive for acute infarction; CTA/MRA identifies major arterial disease and large-vessel occlusion (p953, p965).

**Acute ischemic treatment**
- Hazzard’s IV alteplase regimen is **0.9 mg/kg**, with **10% as an immediate bolus** and the remainder infused **over one hour** (p966).
  > Current practice (not the book): the US Activase label specifies **90 mg maximum** and gives the 10% bolus **over one minute** — [Activase prescribing information](https://www.gene.com/download/pdf/activase_prescribing.pdf).
- Earlier treatment is better. The chapter supports the selected **3–4.5-hour** window and imaging-based selection for some wake-up/extended-window presentations (p967).
- Thrombectomy benefits selected disabling large-vessel occlusions; imaging-selected DAWN patients were **6–24 hours** from last-known-well. Favorable tissue imaging is not a reason to delay treatment (p968–969).
- After IV thrombolysis, maintain BP below **180/105 mm Hg** for at least **24 hours** and obtain follow-up CT/MRI; antithrombotics are generally withheld until hemorrhage is excluded (p969).

**Secondary prevention**
- Aspirin **50–325 mg/day** is commonly used after ischemic stroke/TIA when appropriate; consider bleeding history and the post-thrombolysis restriction (p970–971).
- Match antithrombotic treatment to etiology; routine anticoagulation for embolic stroke of undetermined source did not improve outcomes and increased bleeding in the trials summarized (p974).
- Long-term aspirin/clopidogrel combination after lacunar stroke increased bleeding and death without superior stroke prevention (p973).
- Control vascular risks; the Treat Stroke to Target trial supports LDL below **70 mg/dL** after recent stroke/TIA with atherosclerotic disease (p975).

**Hemorrhage & exam traps**
- Deep hemorrhage commonly reflects hypertension; lobar microbleeds suggest cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Imaging, not symptoms alone, distinguishes hemorrhage from infarction (p975–976).
- Suspected subarachnoid hemorrhage starts with CT; persistent suspicion after negative CT requires lumbar puncture (p978).
- Oral nimodipine **60 mg every 4 hours** after SAH reduces delayed ischemia and mortality in the cited trials (p978).
- Age alone does not negate reperfusion benefit; disabling deficits and eligibility matter. Incorporate rehabilitation, symptom control and palliative goals throughout care (p968–969, p978).
