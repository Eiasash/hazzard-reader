## AF and stroke prevention

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-93e0cbe108de9a8412f0af06 mcq-52e0e246a50af6e23d65cdc9" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Identify the valve lesion first.** In AF with moderate or severe mitral stenosis or any mechanical valve, use a vitamin K antagonist. For rheumatic mitral stenosis the usual INR target is 2–3. A mechanical valve's target also depends on its position and type. A measured GFR of 29 does not turn rivaroxaban into an appropriate choice for the 69-year-old with rheumatic stenosis. Severe **native aortic stenosis** does not carry the same restriction: apixaban can be suitable for the 85-year-old with diabetes. In dialysis, avoid dabigatran. Read recommendations for advanced kidney disease together with the product information for the treatment setting (p. 1203, Table 77-8).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0cd20cd280f46d0acf25449c" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**A patient in sinus rhythm at discharge can still need stroke prevention.** Infection may reveal an underlying tendency to AF. In the two exam cases, age 75 supplies two CHA₂DS₂-VASc points and prior MI supplies one. A mechanical fall six months earlier calls for attention to tripping hazards. It does not in itself offset the benefit of anticoagulation. For stable coronary disease years after the event, oral anticoagulation alone generally limits bleeding compared with continuing aspirin, assuming no history of stent thrombosis or another current antiplatelet indication. At age 75, 70 kg and normal kidney function, the man takes the usual apixaban AF dose of **5 mg twice daily**. Arrange follow-up for recurrent AF and reassess stroke and bleeding risks (pp. 1202–1205, Tables 77-7 to 77-9). [ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline, §§6.8.2 and 10.10](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ef2bb73ec06eb89fbd6291e8" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Read the correct half of Table 77-7.** Part A estimates stroke risk. Heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, vascular disease, age 65–74 and female sex each contribute one point. Age 75 or older contributes two in place of the younger age band. Prior stroke, TIA or systemic embolism contributes two. The 73-year-old woman with diabetes and hypertension therefore has four points and a **4% annual estimate** in this historical table. Actual risk at a given score varies among populations. The 2024 ESC guideline uses CHA₂DS₂-VA without sex: it recommends anticoagulation at two or more points and consideration at one. Keep the scoring system specified by the question (pp. 1202–1203). [ESC 2024 AF guideline, §6.1](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae176).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-9cf3a3d46c26e13809d224e1" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**HAS-BLED flags harms that can be reduced.** Part B includes hypertension, renal or liver dysfunction, prior stroke, prior bleeding, unstable INR, older age, and medicines or alcohol that increase bleeding. Uncontrolled hypertension is common to the stroke-prevention discussion and the bleeding assessment. Diverticulosis without bleeding and head trauma without a documented major bleed do not earn independent points. Review blood pressure, NSAIDs, unnecessary antiplatelets, alcohol, hemoglobin and renal function. A score of three or higher calls for closer follow-up, and does not by itself justify withholding anticoagulation (p. 1202, Table 77-7B).

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-102e4ab82b9fd8d644e4dbbb" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Choosing a direct anticoagulant.** In people aged 75 and older, the trials summarized on p. 1202 found less intracranial bleeding with DOACs than with warfarin, and favorable major-bleeding results for apixaban and edoxaban. The June 2026 question specifically tests Beers: for long-term AF or VTE treatment, prefer a safer alternative to rivaroxaban, particularly apixaban. Once-daily dosing can justify an exception if essential for adherence. Renal impairment calls for assessing individual drug suitability. An available antidote alone is a poor reason to prefer dabigatran. [AGS Beers Criteria 2023, Table 2, p. 2058](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.18372).

## AF symptoms, ventricular rate and sinus rhythm

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f2071d46927ab12d9f43632b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Start by identifying the treatment goal.** Beta blockers, diltiazem and verapamil slow AV nodal conduction. Dihydropyridines such as amlodipine are antihypertensives without useful AF rate control. A pulse in the chapter's range of 80–110/min may be acceptable in the absence of significant symptoms or heart failure. Titrate against symptoms, blood pressure and conduction. Rapid escalation can cause hypotension or AV block. Avoid diltiazem and verapamil in substantially reduced systolic function. Digoxin's narrow therapeutic window becomes especially important with fluctuating renal function. AV-node ablation with pacing can control a refractory ventricular rate while leaving AF and its embolic risk in place (p. 1201).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-cdc9bd78706bb7538460a9ec mcq-2f108a771e027a49d0986f2e" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**For rhythm control, read across the row for the underlying heart disease.** Flecainide and propafenone are options in structurally normal hearts but are unsuitable with ischemic myocardial disease or HFrEF. Table 77-6 lists amiodarone and dofetilide for AF with reduced EF. Amiodarone is the choice offered after a recent admission with NYHA IV heart failure. Dofetilide is the choice offered in the separate case with ischemic cardiomyopathy and EF 35%. Dofetilide initiation requires renal dosing and inpatient QT monitoring; amiodarone warrants surveillance for extracardiac toxicity. Avoid dronedarone after recent decompensation or with advanced symptomatic heart failure (p. 1201). [ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline, §§8.3.1–8.3.2](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193).

**Which evidence applies to this patient?** Older drug-strategy trials supported rate control in mildly symptomatic patients. The AFFIRM analysis of patients over 70 associated antiarrhythmic therapy with higher all-cause mortality. Early rhythm control in recently diagnosed AF and rhythm control in selected patients with heart failure can improve outcomes. Persistent symptoms, treatment tolerance, stage of AF and patient preferences matter. Electrical cardioversion restores sinus rhythm acutely; catheter ablation is more effective than drugs at suppressing recurrence but does not remove the need to assess stroke risk. CABANA did not show superiority for its primary composite endpoint in the intention-to-treat comparison (pp. 1200–1202). [ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline, §8.1](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193).

## A pause, a block or a shockable rhythm

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-80487577b314a49f9eb9ccb2" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Look for atrial activity during the pause.** The June 2023 Basic strip runs across three lines. A faster irregular rhythm gives way to a long quiet interval in the middle line, then slower beats return. Regular P waves do not continue through the pause. This supports sinus node dysfunction in a patient with recurrent presyncope. In advanced AV block, atrial impulses continue but fail to produce ventricular complexes. Check for a reversible driver and establish a relationship between symptoms and bradycardia. Permanent pacing relieves symptoms attributable to sinus node dysfunction. The 4.2-second pause illustrated in Figure 77-1B belongs to the textbook example, not a measured duration from this examination strip (pp. 1195–1197, Table 77-3).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-eea29e0984ea5088b834d159" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Primary prevention asks who remains at risk after recovery.** In ischemic heart failure, an EF of 35% and NYHA class III symptoms six months after MI fit the usual criteria despite guideline-directed therapy, provided meaningful survival beyond a year is expected. Two weeks after MI and one month after bypass surgery are too early for routine primary-prevention implantation. Allow at least 40 days after MI and 90 days after revascularization when applicable. **Secondary prevention asks what caused the arrest.** A survivor of VF without a reversible trigger can qualify even with EF 45%. VF confined to an acute reversible ischemic event requires a different assessment. The June 2023 question leaves the timing of VF unspecified. In very old adults, discuss function, cognition, competing illness and the patient's priorities before implanting a device (pp. 1206–1207). [AHA/ACC/HRS ICD decision aid](https://www.acc.org/-/media/Non-Clinical/Files-PDFs-Excel-MS-Word-etc/Guidelines/2018/VASCD-Guideline-Tool.pdf).

## Other chapter decisions

**Which mechanism caused the collapse?** A standing pressure fall, a meal-related episode, a reflex prodrome and an abrupt pause each suggest a different mechanism. Older patients may have more than one. Review vasodilators, diuretics, volume loss and autonomic disease. A postprandial episode typically occurs 30–90 minutes after eating. Correct contributors and teach slow position changes before escalating medication. In carotid sinus syndrome, the physiologic response must reproduce symptoms. A positive physiologic response alone is hypersensitivity (pp. 1193–1195).

**Pacing and resynchronization.** Sinus node disease and AV block have different electrocardiographic signatures. For new unstable symptomatic AV block after TAVR, stabilize the patient. Persistent symptomatic or hemodynamically significant block calls for permanent pacing before discharge. Some conduction disturbances resolve during observation. [ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline, §8.1.2.4](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000628). New LBBB or PR prolongation may require surveillance after discharge. Frequent right ventricular pacing can worsen synchrony, ventricular function and mitral regurgitation. An existing indication for pacing with substantial anticipated RV pacing and impaired EF is a different CRT pathway from the patient with symptomatic heart failure, LBBB and a broad QRS (pp. 1197–1200, Tables 77-3 to 77-5).

**An embolic stroke without documented AF.** Trials of empiric anticoagulation for embolic stroke of undetermined source did not reduce recurrent stroke compared with aspirin. Prolonged monitoring may reveal occult AF. In a patient with a pacemaker, inspect the stored electrogram to confirm that a recorded atrial high-rate episode is genuine. Duration and baseline stroke risk matter when discussing anticoagulation for device-detected episodes. Left atrial appendage occlusion is a separate option in selected patients unsuitable for long-term anticoagulation. It requires a plan for postimplant antithrombotic treatment and imaging (pp. 1202–1203).

**Flutter, SVT and ventricular ectopy.** Typical flutter uses the cavotricuspid isthmus. Ablation can interrupt that circuit, while stroke prevention still depends on embolic risk. AV nodal reentry is the commonest SVT mechanism described in older patients. For a stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia, vagal maneuvers and adenosine can interrupt AV node-dependent reentry. Instability calls for synchronized cardioversion. Pre-excited AF is an important exception to AV nodal blockade. Incidental nonsustained VT prompts a search for structural disease, ischemia and impaired EF before deciding on prognosis or a device (p. 1206, Table 77-10).

## Summary

**Syncope in older adults**
- Sharp rise in syncope incidence after **age 70** (p1193); worse outcomes (higher hospitalization/death) than in younger patients.
- Orthostatic hypotension (OH): prevalence **30%** (p1193) in those >75y, up to **50%** (p1193) in frail nursing-home residents. Defined as SBP drop **≥ 20 mmHg** (p1193) or DBP drop **≥ 10 mmHg** (p1193) on standing. Four subtypes (p1194): initial (within 15s), classic (within 3 min), delayed (>3 min), neurogenic (autonomic-failure-driven).
- Vasovagal syncope (VVS) has three response types (p1194): cardioinhibitory = pause **≥ 3 s** (p1194) or HR **< 40 bpm** (p1194) for more than 10s; vasodepressor = SBP falls **≥ 50 mmHg** without symptoms or **30–50 mmHg** with symptoms, HR fall **≤ 10%** (Table 77-2, p1194); mixed. Dual-chamber pacing reasonable for age **> 40y** (p1194) with recurrent VVS and spontaneous pauses.
- Carotid sinus syndrome: CSH prevalence up to **30%** (p1195) among older adults with unexplained falls; CSS = **≥ 3-second** (p1195) pause or SBP drop **≥ 50 mmHg** (p1195) on carotid sinus massage, plus symptoms.

**Bradyarrhythmia**
- Two mechanisms: sinus node dysfunction (SND) and atrioventricular block (AVB) (p1195).
- SND (historically "sick sinus syndrome"): sinus bradycardia, sinus arrest, or SA exit block (p1195); tachy-brady syndrome common in older SND patients.
- **More than 80%** (p1193) of permanent pacemakers (PPM) are placed in patients **65 years** or older (p1193); SND is the leading indication for PPM in this age group.
- AVB has three degrees (p1198) — first degree (PR **> 200 ms** (p1198)), second degree (Mobitz I/Wenckebach vs Mobitz II), third degree/complete.
- PPM indications (Tables 77-3/77-4, p1197/p1199): symptomatic SND or symptomatic Mobitz II/high-grade/3rd-degree AVB, if not attributable to reversible causes or caused by medication required for other conditions; device selection — SND: single-chamber atrial if no AV conduction abnormality, dual-chamber if present; AVB: single-chamber ventricular if infrequent pacing expected.
  > Current practice (not the book): in acquired Mobitz II, high-grade or third-degree AV block not caused by reversible or physiologic causes, permanent pacing is recommended regardless of symptoms — ACC/AHA/HRS bradycardia guideline, 2018.
- CRT highest-likelihood-to-respond criteria (Table 77-5, p1199, image): **LVEF ≤ 35%** (p1199, image), sinus rhythm, LBBB, **QRS ≥ 150 ms** (p1199, image), NYHA II/III/ambulatory IV. A *separate* AVB-driven pathway (in running text, not the table) favors CRT over conventional RV pacing when **LVEF < 50%** (p1199) and ventricular pacing is expected **> 40%** (p1199) of the time — a different LVEF cutoff from the HF/dyssynchrony CRT indication above.
- Mode Selection Trial: RV pacing **≥ 40%** (p1199) of the time → **2.6-fold** (p1199) increase in HF hospitalizations.
- TAVR: new LBBB in **19–55%** (p1200), high-degree AVB in **10%** (p1200) after TAVR; up to **30%** (p1200) of new-LBBB patients have their first high-degree AVB episode after discharge.

**Atrial fibrillation (AF)**
- Rate control target HR **80–110 bpm** (p1201); β-blockers/non-dihydropyridine CCBs first-line, but **CCBs are related to higher mortality when LVEF <40%**; digoxin less effective but can be considered (caution: narrow therapeutic index).
- AAD choice for AF rhythm control depends on comorbidity (Table 77-6, p1201): no heart disease → class Ic or III; CAD → class Ic contraindicated, class III OK; reduced EF/HF → amiodarone and dofetilide can be used (dronedarone & class Ic contraindicated).
- AFFIRM trial: AAD therapy associated with higher all-cause mortality in the subgroup **older than 70** (p1201).
- CABANA trial (**n = 2204** (p1201)) patients: catheter ablation not superior to AAD for the primary composite endpoint at 5y, but reduced AF recurrence and the combined death/CV-hospitalization secondary endpoint.
- **Stroke risk is 5× higher** (p1202) with AF. CHA2DS2-VASc: anticoagulate if **≥ 2** (p1203) points in men or **≥ 3** (p1203) in women (Table 77-8); maximum score **9** (p1202). HAS-BLED: **0–2** (p1202) score = low risk, **3–9** (p1202) score = high risk.
- DOACs preferred over warfarin as first-line stroke prevention **for nonvalvular AF** — valvular AF (moderate–severe MS or mechanical valve) needs warfarin regardless of score; no dabigatran/rivaroxaban/edoxaban in ESRD/dialysis (Table 77-8, p1203); apixaban and edoxaban show lower major-bleeding risk in patients **75 years** or older (p1202), while full-dose dabigatran and rivaroxaban carry increased GI bleeding risk (p1202 recommends a PPI with these agents). In clinical practice, individualize gastroprotection according to overall gastrointestinal bleeding risk. [ESC 2024 AF guideline, §6.7.1](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae176).
- **WATCHMAN:** the original trials used warfarin to INR **2–3** with aspirin for approximately **45 days**. Reassessment by TEE looked for device thrombus and significant peri-device leak before transition to antiplatelet therapy. Current postimplant regimens depend on device, bleeding risk, imaging and labeling (p1203).

**DOACs** (Table 77-9, p1204–1205 — table reproduced as a page image; percentages below verified directly against that image, not text-searchable)
- **Dabigatran.** RE-LY studied 150 mg and 110 mg twice daily: 150 mg reduced stroke/systemic embolism by 34% with similar major bleeding to warfarin, while 110 mg had similar efficacy and about 20% less major bleeding. The **110 mg twice-daily AF regimen is jurisdiction-specific** (for example, European labeling in selected older patients or with verapamil) and is not the US adult AF dose. Dose by local labeling and renal function. Specific reversal agent: idarucizumab (Table 77-9, p1204).
- **Rivaroxaban.** In ROCKET-AF, 20 mg daily (15 mg for CrCl 30–49 mL/min) was noninferior to warfarin for stroke prevention, with a similar major-bleeding rate. US adult AF labeling uses **20 mg with the evening meal when CrCl >50 mL/min**, or **15 mg with the evening meal when CrCl ≤50 mL/min** within labeled renal-use conditions. US commercial sales of andexanet alfa ended in December 2025. Management of life-threatening bleeding follows local reversal protocols (Table 77-9, p1204).
- **Apixaban for AF:** 5 mg twice daily, reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily when **at least two** criteria apply: age **≥80 years**, weight **≤60 kg**, serum creatinine **≥1.5 mg/dL**. ARISTOTLE found 21% less stroke/systemic embolism and 31% less major bleeding versus warfarin (Table 77-9, p1205, Eliquis prescribing information).
  > Current practice (not the book): the Eliquis label's weight criterion is body weight **≤60 kg** (not <60) — Eliquis prescribing information §2.1 (BMS).
- **Edoxaban.** In the US, adult nonvalvular AF dosing is **60 mg once daily for CrCl >50 to 95 mL/min**, or **30 mg once daily for CrCl 15–50 mL/min**. Avoid use for NVAF when CrCl exceeds 95 mL/min. Weight and P-gp criteria in Table 77-9 pertain to trial protocols and some other indications or jurisdictions. ENGAGE AF studied higher- and lower-dose strategies, both noninferior for stroke/systemic embolism in its primary analysis, with less major bleeding. The low-dose arm had more ischemic stroke than warfarin (p1205, Savaysa prescribing information §2.1).

**Other tachyarrhythmias**
- Atrial flutter: cavotricuspid-isthmus ablation success **> 90–95%** (p1206), complications **< 1–2%** (p1206).
- SVT: AVNRT is the most common type in older adults. β-blockers and CCBs are first-line therapy (p1206); acute options in Table 77-10 include vagal maneuvers and adenosine, with synchronized cardioversion if hemodynamically unstable or refractory to adenosine (Table 77-10, p1206); ablation success **85%** (p1206) to better than **95%** (p1206), major complications **< 2–3%** (p1206).
- **Primary-prevention ICD:** on GDMT, ischemic cardiomyopathy with LVEF **≤35%** and NYHA II–III, or LVEF **≤30%** and NYHA I, after the applicable waiting periods (at least **40 days after MI** and reassessment after revascularization, generally at least **90 days**). Nonischemic cardiomyopathy may qualify with LVEF ≤35% and NYHA II–III despite GDMT. Expected meaningful survival should exceed one year (p1206, AHA/ACC/HFSA).
- ICD secondary prevention: cardiac arrest from VT/VF not from a reversible cause; spontaneous sustained VT with structural heart disease; or unexplained syncope with inducible hemodynamically significant VT/VF at EPS when drug therapy is ineffective, not tolerated, or not preferred (p1206).

**End-of-life device management**
- PPM deactivation can be decided by the patient or legal surrogate via shared decision-making; after that, a written request to deactivate the PPM plus a DNR order is required (p1200). For ICDs, the patient or legal surrogate should be informed of the right to deactivate when the end-of-life decision is appropriate (p1207).
- PPM-dependent patients may die immediately on deactivation; non-dependent patients need monitoring for symptoms (p1200).

The discussion on pp. 1200 and 1207 reflects the US context. For Israeli patients, see the Israeli guidance above on future ICD shocks and ongoing pacing.

**Exam traps / book notes**
- The CHA2DS2-VASc "adjusted stroke risk" table (Table 77-7A, p1202, image) is non-monotonic as printed, and dips **twice**: score **6** → **9.8** %/year, score **7** → **9.6** %/year (a small dip), then score **8** → **6.7** %/year (a bigger dip, equal to score 5) — reproduced exactly as in the book; don't assume risk rises smoothly with each added point, and don't assume the 7→8 step is the only place it falls.
- Two different LVEF thresholds trigger CRT in this chapter: **≤ 35%** (p1199, image, Table 77-5) for the classic HF/dyssynchrony indication vs **< 50%** (p1199) for the AVB-driven "expected to need >40% ventricular pacing" pathway (running text) — easy to conflate on a question stem.
- Not all DOACs reduce bleeding vs warfarin: apixaban and edoxaban show less major bleeding at their studied doses; dabigatran only at **110 mg** (150 mg shows similar bleeding to warfarin, not less); rivaroxaban (20 mg, the dose studied) shows only a similar major-bleeding rate to warfarin (p1204, image).

## Recall

**1. In a nursing-home resident, orthostatic hypotension is defined by what blood-pressure drop, and how prevalent is it in frail older nursing-home residents?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

SBP drop ≥ 20 mmHg or DBP drop ≥ 10 mmHg on standing; up to 50% of frail nursing-home residents (p1193).

</details>

**2. Name the four subtypes of orthostatic hypotension.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Initial (immediate, within 15 s), classic (within 3 min), delayed (>3 min), and neurogenic (autonomic-nervous-system dysfunction) (p1194).

</details>

**3. What defines the cardioinhibitory type of vasovagal response, and at what age is dual-chamber pacing considered reasonable for recurrent vasovagal syncope with spontaneous pauses?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A cardioinhibitory vasovagal response may produce a pause **≥3 seconds** or a heart rate **<40/min for more than 10 seconds**. Dual-chamber pacing may be reasonable in selected patients **over 40** with recurrent vasovagal syncope and documented prolonged spontaneous pauses (p1194).

</details>

**4. What carotid sinus massage finding defines carotid sinus hypersensitivity, and what additional feature makes it carotid sinus syndrome?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A pause ≥ 3 seconds or a systolic BP drop ≥ 50 mmHg during carotid sinus massage; carotid sinus syndrome requires that this be accompanied by symptoms of syncope or presyncope (p1195).

</details>

**5. What proportion of permanent pacemakers are placed in patients 65 or older, and what is the leading indication in this age group?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

More than 80%; sinoatrial (sinus node) dysfunction is the leading indication (p1193).

</details>

**6. What are the highest-likelihood-to-respond criteria for cardiac resynchronization therapy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

LVEF ≤ 35%, sinus rhythm, LBBB, QRS ≥ 150 ms, NYHA class II, III, or ambulatory IV (p1199).

</details>

**8. After TAVR, what percentage of patients develop new LBBB, and what percentage develop high-degree AV block?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

New LBBB in 19% to 55%; high-degree AVB in 10% (p1200).

</details>

**10. How many patients were randomized in the CABANA trial, and was catheter ablation superior to antiarrhythmic drugs for the primary composite endpoint?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

2,204 patients; catheter ablation was NOT superior to AAD for the primary endpoint (all-cause mortality, disabling stroke, serious bleeding, or cardiac arrest) at 5 years, though it did reduce AF recurrence and the combined death/cardiovascular-hospitalization secondary endpoint (p1201).

</details>

**11. What CHA2DS2-VASc score threshold triggers anticoagulation in men versus women, and what is the maximum possible score?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

≥ 2 in men or ≥ 3 in women (p1203, Table 77-8); maximum score is 9 (p1202, Table 77-7A).

</details>

**12. On the HAS-BLED score, what ranges represent low versus high bleeding risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0–2 = low risk; 3–9 = high risk (p1202).

</details>

**13. Which two DOACs show a lower major-bleeding risk than warfarin in patients 75 years or older, and which two carry an increased risk of GI bleeding at full dose?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Apixaban and edoxaban show lower major bleeding; full-dose dabigatran and rivaroxaban are associated with increased GI bleeding risk (p1202). Hazzard recommends a PPI with these agents. The 2024 ESC guideline individualizes gastroprotection according to gastrointestinal bleeding risk.

</details>

**14. What was the initial warfarin regimen in the original WATCHMAN studies, and what should follow-up imaging assess before changing antithrombotic therapy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The historical regimen used warfarin **INR 2–3** with aspirin for about **45 days**. Before switching to the planned antiplatelet regimen, assess device-related thrombus and peri-device leak. Current regimens and imaging follow-up depend on device labeling and the patient's bleeding/thrombotic risks (p1203).

</details>

**15. What are the primary-prevention ICD criteria for ischemic cardiomyopathy, including the required time from MI or revascularization?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

After MI, primary-prevention ICD criteria include **LVEF ≤35% with NYHA II–III**, or **LVEF ≤30% with NYHA I**, despite guideline-directed therapy and with expected meaningful survival beyond one year. Assess at least **40 days after MI** and reassess after revascularization, generally at least **90 days** afterward (p1206, AHA/ACC/HFSA).

</details>
