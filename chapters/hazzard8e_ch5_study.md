# Chapter 5 Study: Sex Differences in Health and Longevity

> Excluded from the IMA required reading list (P005-2026); not cited in the 2020-2026 past papers.

## Summary

**Survival differences and their interpretation**
- Women live longer than men across countries and historical periods with reliable data, but the size of the gap varies (p83-86).
- Life expectancy summarizes mortality experience; it is not a guarantee of how long a particular living person will survive (p83-84).
- Sex differences arise across the life course. Behavioral, environmental and cultural influences modify a probable biological contribution (p84-86).
- Smoking patterns contributed to historical widening and narrowing of the gap; national trends cannot be reduced to sex hormones alone (p86-87).

**Does longer survival mean slower aging?**
- By the demographic definition based on the rate of age-related mortality increase, men and women age at surprisingly similar rates despite different mortality levels (p87).
- Women appear more resistant to many fatal diseases or acquire them later; this supports a different interpretation when aging is measured by disease onset (p87-89).
- Death certificates can misattribute causes, especially chronic diseases; changes in definitions and recognition complicate comparisons over time (p89-90).
- Alzheimer disease and other dementias are exceptions to the broad female survival advantage; both longevity and greater susceptibility contribute (p90).
- Menopausal hormone decline is more abrupt than testosterone decline in men. Hormone replacement does not establish generalized rejuvenation or improved longevity (p90).
- Female hormone therapy has mixed disease effects without enhanced longevity; testosterone replacement has limited benefits for age-related conditions in normal older men (p90).

**The health-survival paradox**
- Women survive longer yet commonly report more physical illness, functional limitations, symptoms and health-service use; survival and health are different outcomes (p92-93).
- Some of this difference may reflect symptom reporting, but the pattern extends beyond self-report; interpret each measure and setting carefully (p92-93).
- Income, education, race, behavior and access to care interact with gender. US patterns are not automatically universal (p90-92).
- Mechanisms behind the paradox remain incompletely explained. The chapter frames these as research questions, not grounds for assuming an individual's health from sex alone (p93).

### Reading levels, slopes and causes of death

In Sweden in **1773**, life expectancy at birth fell to **18 years**. About half of deaths occurred before age five, while most adult deaths occurred much later. A historical life expectancy at birth can therefore say more about infant and childhood mortality than about what an adult who survived childhood could expect (p83).

On a logarithmic mortality plot, the height of a curve at a given age describes the level of mortality. Its slope describes how rapidly mortality rises with age. From age 50 the mortality rate doubled in approximately **8 years** in the US periods compared, even though survival improved greatly between them. The distinction matters when deciding whether women “age more slowly” (p84–85, p87).

In the US example, women experienced their first myocardial infarction about **8 years later** on average than men. Later onset can coexist with worse survival after the event, partly because women are older at presentation. A death rate adjusted for age, the fraction of all deaths assigned to heart disease and survival after an infarct each answer a different question (p88–90).

### Start with the life table and its starting age

The historical female survival advantage is striking because it recurs in countries with very different environments, cultures and patterns of disease. That observation is about groups. It does not mean that all women live longer than all men, and it does not specify how much of the gap is biological. In interpreting the chapter, name the population, calendar period and starting age. Compare a woman and a man who have both survived to an older age with the appropriate conditional survival estimates rather than importing the life expectancy of babies born in a different period (p83–85).

A period life expectancy applies a specified period's age-specific death rates to a hypothetical life course. It can be calculated before anyone born in that period has reached old age. A birth-cohort result instead reflects the mortality actually experienced by a generation as it passes through changing environments and medical treatment. If future risks fall, an individual born during a given period may experience quite different mortality from the period calculation. Life expectancy at a later age is conditional on having reached that age; distinguish expected remaining years from expected age at death when reporting it (p83–84).

Figure 5-1 illustrates why infant mortality must be considered. In the Swedish crisis of **1773**, estimated life expectancy at birth was **18 years**, and about half of deaths occurred before **age 5**. Many people who survived infancy and childhood nevertheless reached middle and later life. It would be incorrect to imagine an adult population in which death at eighteen was typical. When comparing men and women who had already reached 50, the chapter reports a difference of about **one year** in that crisis year. The surviving groups have passed through the same broad historical period but are selected in ways absent from the at-birth calculation (p83–84).

Large changes in life expectancy at birth can reflect changes at several ages. Reducing early deaths has a particularly large effect because each prevented death in infancy represents many potential years. Once early mortality becomes uncommon, improvements during adult and older ages contribute more prominently. Looking only at the difference between two national life expectancies can conceal improvement in both groups. A gap can narrow because the disadvantaged group gains more, because the advantaged group stalls, or through a combination. Inspect the two underlying trajectories before deciding that one group's health is deteriorating (p84–87).

### Reading level, ratio and slope on age-specific mortality curves

On an age-specific mortality curve, the height at a given age is the death rate among people who have survived to that age. A vertical difference between curves means different risk at that point. On a logarithmic axis their slopes show how rapidly mortality multiplies with increasing age. Two groups can have parallel slopes but different mortality levels throughout adulthood. With lower risk at every given age, one group can still have more survivors at later ages. The demographic rate of aging inferred from the slope and the average length of life inferred from the full mortality pattern therefore need not give the same answer (p84–85, p87).

Figure 5-2 compares US patterns in **1900 and 2000**. At both dates, mortality rates from about age 50 doubled approximately every **8 years**, yet the later period had much lower rates over a broad range of ages. The figure's at-birth estimates rose from **46 to 74 years for men** and **49 to 79 years for women**. This is an example in which improved survival can arise largely through lowering the level of mortality, without a corresponding change in the proportional rate at which it increases across older ages. Reductions before midlife also contributed substantially to the gain (p84–85).

Panel B expresses an age-specific male-to-female mortality ratio. A value of one means equal rates. A peak in young adulthood can reflect excess male deaths from injuries, homicide and suicide, not unusually rapid biological aging during those years. The location of the largest gap is not identical in every country. In the chapter's Japanese comparison the young-adult male excess persists, yet the largest differences occur later, around ages **60–80**. State whether the figure gives a rate, an absolute rate difference or a ratio; a high relative ratio at an age with low absolute mortality can contribute less to total deaths than a smaller ratio at an older age (p85).

The female advantage in early infancy, before many adult behavioral exposures are relevant, supports a biological contribution. It does not eliminate the role of environment or identify one particular hormone as the cause. Conversely, similar mortality at some ages during periods of high infectious or maternal mortality does not establish biological equivalence across all risks. Different causes can offset one another in an all-cause rate. This is the reason to examine the age pattern, historical setting and cause-specific data together rather than use one observed gap as a direct estimate of innate longevity (p83–87).

### Calendar trends, smoking and historical context

Figure 5-3 demonstrates how the choice of scale changes interpretation. An absolute difference of **3 years** is proportionately larger against male life expectancy of **40 years** than against **70 years**. A graph of the gap in years and one expressing the gap as a percentage can therefore emphasize different historical intervals without disagreeing about the underlying observations. Similarly, in an era in which infant deaths fluctuate widely, the gap at birth can vary more than the gap conditional on reaching midlife. Later, when deaths in early life become much less common, the two measures can move in parallel (p85–86).

The Swedish trajectory widened strongly after the middle of the twentieth century and then narrowed. Cross-national comparisons show that the size of the gap is not fixed. A large female advantage can arise chiefly because male mortality is high, not because women in that country have exceptionally long lives. Ask which sex is changing relative to its own historical level and to people of the same sex elsewhere. National comparisons also depend on the time interval: an observation about Russia, Japan, Sweden or the United States in this chapter is a historical estimate, not a current international ranking (p85–87).

Smoking provides a plausible behavioral contribution with a long delay between exposure and death. Men and women did not begin, intensify or stop smoking at the same time in all countries. Figure 5-4 compares shifts in smoking prevalence with later mortality from lung cancer and chronic respiratory disease. A falling smoking prevalence today need not coincide with a simultaneous decline in mortality, because the deaths occur after accumulated exposure. In Japan, comparatively low smoking prevalence among women contrasted with patterns in countries where women's uptake approached that of men. This comparison helps explain variation; it cannot account for the infant survival advantage or every cause of death (p86–87).

The US example also illustrates why a narrowing gap can accompany gains in both sexes. Between **1985 and 2017** the chapter reports an increase in life expectancy of **3.2 years for women and 5.2 years for men**. A faster gain by men narrows the difference without showing that women's life expectancy fell. The chapter relates the slower improvement among US women relative to some other high-income populations partly to the timing and intensity of smoking. Predictions about later convergence were predictions at publication, not observations that the full expected change had already occurred (p87, p91).

### Cause-specific mortality: use the right comparison

Table 5-1 presents US data for **1960 and 2017**. It gives the fraction of all deaths assigned to particular causes and age-adjusted rates separately. The fraction is compositional: if another major cause becomes less common, a disease can account for a larger share of deaths even if its own risk has not risen. Age adjustment addresses a different difficulty by comparing rates at a standard age distribution. When men and women have different age distributions, a proportionate mortality comparison can conceal a substantial difference in age-specific risk or age at onset (p88–89).

In the 2017 heart-disease row, **24% of male deaths and 22% of female deaths** were attributed to heart disease, apparently quite similar. The age-adjusted rates were **209 versus 130 per 100,000** for men and women respectively. Later onset can help produce this pattern even if women do not survive longer once an acute event has occurred. Timing matters to healthspan as well: an infarct late in life and one decades earlier can contribute equally to a count but very differently to years lived free of cardiovascular disease. To compare survival after the event itself, take into account age and clinical status at presentation (p88–89).

The standards used to age-adjust the **1960** and **2017** US rates differ. The chapter explicitly warns against interpreting their numerical difference as a standardized time trend. The male-to-female rates within a particular year's standard are informative, and the author compares the ratios across years to describe changes in sex differences. For stroke in 2017, similar age-adjusted rates coexist with different shares of total deaths. The reason is not an arithmetic inconsistency: the measures answer different questions about deaths and denominators (p88–89).

Death certificates add another limitation. A clinician choosing one underlying cause may attribute death differently when several chronic diseases and an acute complication coexist. Autopsy is uncommon, and trends can reflect changing diagnostic or reporting practices. Diabetes may contribute substantially to vascular disease without appearing as the recorded cause of death. Recognition of dementia and certification practices have also changed over the decades. A large change in a cause-of-death category may therefore reflect altered labeling alongside real changes in incidence or survival. Interpret historical counts together with data on disease onset and clinical outcomes when available (p89–90).

### Does the Alzheimer's disease pattern represent an exception?

Women represent a larger share of people with Alzheimer disease, and the chapter treats dementia as an important exception to their advantage for many fatal diseases. Begin by accounting for longer survival into ages where dementia is common. Then ask whether women and men of the same age have the same probability of disease, whether the length of time lived with the disease differs, and whether dementia is recorded as a cause of death. These questions correspond to incidence, prevalence, disease duration and mortality respectively. Figure 5-5 addresses age and prevalence; it is not a randomized test of a cause (p89–90).

A clinic serving the very old will naturally see many women, even in the absence of any sex difference in incidence at a given age. Conversely, the observation that older age is common in women cannot by itself explain every same-age difference reported in the chapter. Differences in cardiovascular survival, ascertainment, biological susceptibility and disease duration can coexist. The clinically relevant inference is to pay attention to dementia in both sexes and to interpret a group estimate carefully, rather than use sex to explain a particular patient's symptoms or to rank an individual's prognosis (p89–90).

### Hormonal observations and health inequalities

Menopause leads to a relatively abrupt fall in ovarian hormone production; testosterone changes more gradually with male aging. A temporal association between a hormone's decline and later disease does not demonstrate that maintaining younger concentrations improves longevity. The chapter distinguishes effects on particular symptoms or diseases from an effect on overall survival. Replacement trials in women have shown a mixture of benefits and harms depending on the outcome and context. The discussion of men likewise does not establish broad anti-aging benefit from testosterone in otherwise normal older men. A therapeutic indication for one patient is different from a claim about the origin of the population survival gap (p90).

Historical comparisons of men who underwent loss of gonadal function are provocative but difficult to interpret. The chapter discusses court records and an institutional comparison; neither assigned exposure randomly. Selection into a household or institution, social roles, nutrition, hygiene and access to care may differ between the groups and from modern circumstances. An age difference at death does not isolate lifelong hormone exposure from these factors. Such observations generate hypotheses about tradeoffs between reproduction, physiology and survival. They cannot establish an intervention to prolong life or be directly compared with treatment for symptomatic hormone deficiency (p90).

US county analyses reveal variation hidden by national averages. Counties differ in income, education, racial composition, urbanicity, access to care and behavioral exposures. In some areas improvement slowed or reversed while the national figure rose; the trends also differed between men and women. A county-level association does not determine which factor caused the change for the individuals who died there. Compare within-sex trends when the question concerns geographic inequality, and compare men and women within the same context when the question concerns the local survival gap. It is possible for inequality within one sex to exceed the average difference between sexes (p90–92).

Education and race or ethnicity modify how reported US trends should be interpreted. The chapter describes groups of women with less education who did not share the same improvement as more advantaged groups in certain analyses. The same pattern was not seen consistently in all racial or ethnic populations. The membership of an education category can also change over time as more people complete schooling. A statement about one US subgroup and time period should not be generalized to all women, all people with less schooling or other health systems. Ask which comparison was made and what relevant exposures or care opportunities differed (p91–92).

### Living longer with more limitations

The health-survival paradox concerns distinct endpoints. Women commonly outlive men yet report more chronic physical problems, symptoms and functional difficulty. Years lived with disability incorporate prevalence and severity; they do not count only the final dependent years. A group can have both a lower rate of fatal disease and a higher burden of painful or disabling conditions. A woman who avoids an early fatal infarct can still spend years with osteoarthritis or back pain. Both contribute to health, but only one event necessarily appears in a cause-of-death table (p92–93).

Figure 5-6 compares reports of functional tasks such as walking, bending or climbing steps across the United States, Jamaica, Malaysia and Bangladesh. Similar broad patterns across different social settings weaken the idea that one country's reporting conventions are sufficient explanation. They do not erase potential differences in the meaning of a survey response, in the built environment, or in what a task requires. Objective measures, health service use and reports of disease can provide complementary information. Even where average difficulty is greater in one group, the examination of a patient should establish which activity is affected and the cause in that person (p92–93).

One explanation proposes that women recognize symptoms and seek care more readily. The evidence is mixed, and this mechanism alone would have to account for observations across very different populations. Another invokes selective survival: if less healthy men die earlier, the remaining men may appear more robust. That hypothesis predicts an increasing divergence in measured health with age. Some cross-national studies cited in the chapter did not find the predicted widening. A plausible process can still contribute without accounting for all the evidence. Distinguish a hypothesis's prediction from an observed correlation when reading a comparison of older survivors (p92–93).

Greater burden of bone and joint disease, chronic pain and autoimmune or inflammatory conditions may contribute to functional limitation. The chapter discusses whether more vigorous immune responses could offer some protection against infection while also contributing to inflammatory morbidity. Chronic pain can disrupt sleep, limit activity and affect well-being even when it contributes little directly to mortality. These are potential tradeoffs, not a single established mechanism. To judge whether one sex “ages more slowly,” specify whether the outcome is mortality-rate slope, age at first fatal disease, time lived with disability or ability to carry out an activity. The answer need not agree across all four (p87–93).

## Exam traps

- Similar slopes on a log mortality plot can coexist with different absolute risks at the same age (p84–85, p87).
- A higher fraction of deaths attributed to a disease does not necessarily mean its age-adjusted death rate increased (p88–90).
- Disability, symptom burden, age at disease onset and survival are distinct outcomes in the health-survival paradox (p89, p92–93).
