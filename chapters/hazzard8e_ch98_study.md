# Chapter 98 Study: Thyroid Diseases

## Summary

### Physiology & normal aging changes
- T4 is the major secreted hormone, at **11×** the secretion rate of T3; only **0.04%** of T4 and **0.4%** of T3 circulate free (biologically active) — the rest is protein-bound (TBG, prealbumin, albumin) (p1545).
- Free T4 is preferred over total T4 for assessment, since binding-protein levels vary (p1545).
- TSH gradually **rises** with normal aging in euthyroid people without thyroid disease — this is NOT occult thyroid failure (free T4 stays normal) (p1546).
- With age: T4 production falls **80→60 μg/day**, T3 production falls **30→20 μg/day**, and T4 half-life rises from **7 to 9 days** by age 80+. TBG concentration does **not** change with age (p1546).
- Reference range shifts with age: upper limit of normal TSH is **3.6 mIU/L** at age 20-29 vs **7.5 mIU/L** at 80+ — the reference range may differ between older and younger people (p1554).

### Drugs, nonthyroidal illness
- Amiodarone is **37%** organic iodide by weight (p1547); can cause thyrotoxicosis from a destructive thyroiditis (treat with corticosteroids) or from increased hormone production in underlying nodular disease/Graves (treat with methimazole) — often hard to distinguish, so both may be given initially — or hypothyroidism in Hashimoto glands. In most cases amiodarone should not be stopped (p1547-1548).
- High-dose corticosteroids and dopamine can each suppress TSH without true thyroid dysfunction — relevant in hospitalized patients (p1548).
- Nonthyroidal illness ("euthyroid sick") syndrome: low T3 first, then low free T4, then mild low TSH in the sickest patients; the full abnormal constellation carries **80%** mortality (p1548). Do not treat with thyroid hormone.

### Hyperthyroidism
- Overt hyperthyroidism = low TSH + elevated free T4 or T3; prevalence **1-2%** in older people (p1549).
- In older overtly hyperthyroid patients, only **3 of 19** signs exceed 50% prevalence: tachycardia (**71%**), fatigue (**56%**), weight loss (**50%**) *[sic — the book's own Table 98-3, p1549, gives weight loss 50%: three signs reach ≥50%, only two exceed it]* — classic hyperadrenergic signs (tremor, sweating, heat intolerance, nervousness) are far less common than in the young. "**Apathetic hyperthyroidism**" — goiter and ophthalmopathy often absent (p1549).
- Methimazole is preferred over PTU (longer half-life, fewer adverse effects); PTU carries a fulminant hepatic failure risk. Skin reactions occur in **4-6%**, agranulocytosis in **0.1-0.5%** (p1550).
- With antithyroid drugs, even in good candidates (Graves with mild-moderate hyperthyroidism and a small gland), recurrence exceeds **60%** (p1550).
- Radioactive iodine: stop antithyroid drugs 3-5 days before; observe contact precautions for **5 days** after treatment (p1550).
- Subclinical hyperthyroidism = low TSH, normal free T4/T3; prevalence **1-6%**, persistence **>50%** on repeat testing. Treatment is now recommended (increased AF, heart failure, CV mortality, fracture, dementia risk) (p1551).

### Hypothyroidism
- Overt hypothyroidism = high TSH + low free T4; prevalence **1-2%**. T3 is not useful for assessing hypothyroidism (p1551).
- Only **2 of 24** signs exceed 50% prevalence in older hypothyroid patients: fatigue (**68%**) and weakness (**53%**) (p1551).
- Full replacement dose in older adults is **lower** than the young-adult **1.6 μg/kg/day**; "start low, go slow" in patients ≥65 with cardiac disease, using 25-50 μg tablets (p1552).
- TSH testing usually occurs **6 weeks** after initiation (6 half-lives); older patients may need **7-8 weeks** to reach steady state. Target TSH is upper-normal, up to **4-6 mU/L** in those >80 (p1552).
- IV levothyroxine dose = **75%** of the oral dose (p1552).
- Missing **1 dose/week** on a 112 μg daily regimen drops the effective average dose to 96 μg — check adherence before assuming disease progression when TSH varies (p1552).
- Up to **40%** of older adults on thyroid hormone replacement have a low TSH (iatrogenic overreplacement) — risk mirrors endogenous hyperthyroidism (AF, bone loss) (p1552).
- Subclinical hypothyroidism = high TSH, normal free T4; prevalence **4%** general population, **~10%** at 65+. Retest in **1-3 months** if TSH 4.5-14.9 mIU/mL, or **1-2 weeks** if ≥15.0 (p1553). *[sic — the book's own Figure 98-4, p1554, gives thyrotropin in mU/L]*
- TSH **≥10 mIU/L** predicts CV mortality, CHD, and heart failure; TSH **≥7 mIU/L** predicts CV mortality alone (p1553). Per Figure 98-4, treat if TSH is persistently **≥10.0 mU/mL** (age ≥65); consider treatment at **7.0-9.9 mU/mL** (p1553). *[sic — the book's own Figure 98-4, p1554, gives thyrotropin in mU/L]*
- **Myxedema coma**: give IV hydrocortisone **before/with** thyroid hormone — treating with thyroid hormone alone can precipitate adrenal crisis. Initial levothyroxine **200-400 μg**, then **75-100 μg/day**; avoid added T3 (cardiac risk) (p1553).

### Thyroid nodules & cancer
- **50%** of thyroid glands have a nodule at autopsy; malignancy is present in **7-9%** of thyroid nodules, higher in men than women. Evaluation does not differ by age; nonpalpable nodules carry the same risk as palpable nodules of the same size, and multiple nodules the same risk as a solitary nodule (p1555).
- FNA indicated: nodules **≥1 cm** solid + hypoechoic; **≥1.5 cm** isoechoic/hyperechoic solid; abnormal cervical lymph nodes. FNA **or observation**: mixed cystic-solid or spongiform nodules **≥2 cm**. FNA not required: purely cystic nodules or nodules **<1 cm** (Table 98-8, p1555).
- Benign-nodule follow-up: repeat ultrasound in **12-18 months**, then surveillance every **2-5 years**. A nodule with two benign FNAs needs no further aspiration (p1556).
- Thyroid cancer incidence peaks at age **65-69** (**29.6/100,000**); 5-year survival is **98%** (age 50-64), **97%** (65-74), **88%** (75+) (p1556).
- Lobectomy (instead of total thyroidectomy) is an option for tumors **1-4 cm** without extrathyroidal extension or node involvement (p1556).
- **Anaplastic** thyroid carcinoma: mean age at diagnosis **65**; median survival only **5 months** — worst-prognosis thyroid cancer (p1556).
- Medullary thyroid carcinoma: RAI has no effect (C-cells lack NIS); all patients need *RET* germline testing (p1556).

### Book contradiction (exam trap)
- The book's own **"Hypothyroidism"** section heading (p1551) is immediately followed by the sentence "Overt **hyper**thyroidism is defined as a high TSH with low free T4 concentrations" — a genuine misprint in the source text (verified against the page image, not a transcription error here). Read it as **hypothyroidism**; do not let a quick skim flip the diagnosis in your head.

## Drill

**1. By roughly what factor does T4 secretion exceed T3 secretion from the thyroid, and what fraction of each circulates free?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

T4 secretion is about 11× that of T3. Only 0.04% of T4 and 0.4% of T3 circulate free (biologically active); the rest is protein-bound (p1545).

</details>

**2. Does TSH rising with normal aging indicate occult thyroid failure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

No — in euthyroid older people without thyroid disease, TSH gradually rises while free T4 stays normal; this is not occult failure (p1546).

</details>

**3. How does the reference range for "normal" TSH differ between a 25-year-old and an 85-year-old?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Upper limit of normal is about 3.6 mIU/L at age 20-29 versus 7.5 mIU/L at age 80+, when the 95% confidence interval is applied to a population free of thyroid disease (p1554).

</details>

**4. What are the two distinct mechanisms by which amiodarone can cause thyrotoxicosis, and how are they treated differently?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Increased thyroid hormone production in an underlying nodular or Graves gland is treated with methimazole; destructive thyroiditis from a direct toxic effect is treated acutely with corticosteroids. They can be hard to tell apart, so patients may initially get both. In most cases amiodarone should not be stopped (p1547-1548).

</details>

**5. What is the mortality rate associated with the full constellation of thyroid-test abnormalities in nonthyroidal illness syndrome, and should it be treated with thyroid hormone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Mortality is about 80% with the full pattern; thyroid hormone replacement (T4 or T3) gives no benefit (p1548).

</details>

**6. In older patients with overt hyperthyroidism, which three signs exceed 50% prevalence, and how does this differ from younger patients?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Tachycardia (71%), fatigue (56%), and weight loss (50%) *[sic — the book's own Table 98-3, p1549, gives weight loss 50%: three signs reach ≥50%, only two exceed it]*. Classic hyperadrenergic signs (tremor, sweating, nervousness, heat intolerance) are far less common than in young patients — "apathetic hyperthyroidism" (p1549).

</details>

**7. Why is methimazole generally preferred over propylthiouracil (PTU) for Graves disease, and what are their shared adverse-effect rates?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Methimazole has a longer half-life and fewer adverse effects; PTU carries a risk of fulminant hepatic failure. Both cause skin reactions in 4-6% and agranulocytosis in 0.1-0.5% of patients (p1550).

</details>

**8. In older patients with overt hypothyroidism, which two signs exceed 50% prevalence?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fatigue (68%) and weakness (53%) — the only two of 24 signs studied that exceeded 50% (p1551).

</details>

**9. What levothyroxine dosing approach is recommended for a 70-year-old with cardiac disease starting treatment for hypothyroidism, and when should TSH be rechecked?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

"Start low, go slow" using the smallest tablets (25-50 μg). TSH testing usually occurs at 6 weeks after initiation (6 half-lives), though older patients may need 7-8 weeks to reach steady state; target TSH toward the upper-normal range, up to 4-6 mU/L if over age 80 (p1552).

</details>

**10. A patient on 112 μg levothyroxine daily has a rising TSH. Before assuming disease progression, what simple explanation should be checked?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Adherence: missing just one dose per week drops the effective average daily dose to 96 μg, which can be enough to raise TSH (p1552).

</details>

**11. What proportion of older adults on thyroid hormone replacement have a low TSH from overreplacement, and what are the associated risks?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 40%. Risks mirror endogenous hyperthyroidism, most notably atrial fibrillation and bone loss (p1552).

</details>

**12. At what TSH threshold is treatment of subclinical hypothyroidism recommended in a patient aged 65 or older, and at what threshold should it be considered?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Treat if TSH is persistently ≥10.0 mU/mL; consider treatment if TSH is 7.0-9.9 mU/mL (p1553-1554). *[sic — the book's own Figure 98-4, p1554, gives thyrotropin in mU/L]*

</details>

**13. In managing myxedema coma, what is the critical sequencing error to avoid regarding hydrocortisone and thyroid hormone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hydrocortisone must be given before or with thyroid hormone replacement — treating with thyroid hormone alone can precipitate adrenal crisis in a patient with impaired adrenal reserve (p1553).

</details>

**14. What thyroid nodule characteristics indicate FNA is required versus not required (Table 98-8)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

FNA indicated: nodules ≥1 cm that are solid and hypoechoic, ≥1.5 cm isoechoic/hyperechoic solid, or abnormal cervical lymph nodes. FNA or observation: mixed cystic-solid or spongiform nodules ≥2 cm. FNA not required: purely cystic nodules or nodules <1 cm (p1555).

</details>

**15. What is the median survival for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, and why does radioactive iodine not work for medullary thyroid carcinoma?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Median survival for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is about 5 months. Radioactive iodine has no effect on medullary thyroid carcinoma because the C-cells do not contain the sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) (p1556).

</details>
