# Chapter 98 Study: Thyroid Diseases

## What the exams asked

- Recognize myxedema crisis and give adrenal cover before or with thyroid replacement. [2020 Q71](?chapter=98#asked-chapter) and [June 2025 Basic Q112](?chapter=98#exam-p1553-mcq-b2542a05496f3c5c65789a5f).
- Distinguish transient low T3 after pneumonia from intrinsic thyroid or pituitary failure. [2020 Q74](?chapter=98#asked-chapter) and [June 2026 Q61](?chapter=98#exam-p1548-mcq-804b6ad2cccaa569c0b6acaf).
- Recognize expected early thyroid-test changes after amiodarone. [December 2021 Q27](?chapter=98#asked-chapter).
- Treat amiodarone-associated thyrotoxicosis according to its mechanism and distinguish symptom control from reducing hormone excess. [May 2024 Q60](?chapter=98#exam-p1547-mcq-02bd4d5ac321fb03ca1d5e62) and [June 2026 Q51](?chapter=98#exam-p1547-mcq-614440aa6ac525d6d42ed6f2).
- Choose the first thyroid function test when hypothyroid symptoms appear on amiodarone. [June 2025 Basic Q104](?chapter=98#exam-p1547-mcq-9bddccff96cb66a3829e208c).
- Identify the drug class that suppresses pituitary TSH. [June 2025 Basic Q118](?chapter=98#exam-p1547-mcq-cb89f90016ad0e9c97dcff99).
- Recognize a physiologic rise in TSH with preserved free T4 in healthy older adults. [September 2024 Q51](?chapter=98#exam-p1546-mcq-af57ecd77b2b3894df010e23) and [June 2026 Basic Q119](?chapter=98#exam-p1546-mcq-7400870fc10d03a49c2e9070).
- Compare cognitive, cardiac and gastrointestinal presentations of hyperthyroidism in older and younger patients. [June 2023 Q61](?chapter=98#exam-p1549-mcq-7f4a3ed0b5c9a81e1780006a).
- Start treatment for overt hypothyroidism with heart disease and choose a safe TSH target after age 80. [June 2025 Q6](?chapter=98#exam-p1552-mcq-a9564119e3f03b2dcc342926).
- Distinguish impaired levothyroxine absorption from increased hepatic clearance. [May 2024 Q24](?chapter=98#exam-p1553-mcq-73208d3bcb5312abed0b44aa).
- Select a suspicious thyroid nodule for aspiration and distinguish sonographic risk from palpability or multiplicity. [December 2021 Q100](?chapter=98#asked-chapter) and [June 2025 Q55](?chapter=98#exam-p1555-mcq-6593d3a8fd894eded1a95488).

## A cold, drowsy patient with severe hypothyroidism

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b2542a05496f3c5c65789a5f" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation and reduced responsiveness mark decompensation. The patient may still respond to pain. Look for longstanding untreated hypothyroidism, withdrawal of replacement after thyroidectomy, infection, cold exposure or sedating medication. Hyponatremia, hypoglycemia and elevated muscle creatine kinase can accompany the syndrome. In the 2025 case, the mental status and temperature make the endocrine emergency more informative than the isolated sodium value (pp1552–1553).

Draw cortisol immediately and give IV hydrocortisone before or with thyroid hormone. If adrenal reserve is impaired, giving thyroid hormone first can precipitate adrenal crisis. An adequate pretreatment cortisol allows hydrocortisone to be stopped. The book gives an IV levothyroxine loading dose of 200–400 micrograms followed by 75–100 micrograms daily. Use the lower end of the loading range in older patients or those with coronary disease or an arrhythmia. [American Thyroid Association, recommendation 21a](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028) Address ventilation, metabolic disturbances and the precipitant concurrently. Rapid T3 exposure can stress an older heart, and slower drug metabolism increases sensitivity to sedatives (p1553).

## Interpreting thyroid tests during and after critical illness

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-804b6ad2cccaa569c0b6acaf" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Severe illness reduces conversion of T4 to T3. T3 falls first and reverse T3 can rise. With increasing severity, free T4 can fall and TSH can then decline. Dopamine or high-dose glucocorticoids may suppress TSH further. In the 2020 intensive-care vignette all three measurements were low. In the 2026 rehabilitation vignette T3 was low, TSH only slightly low and free T4 still normal. Those are compatible points on the same spectrum (p1548).

Low TSH with low free T4 can resemble central hypothyroidism. Other pituitary or hypothalamic findings would prompt further assessment. In a patient improving after pneumonia, thyroid hormone has shown no benefit for this illness-related pattern. Recheck thyroid function after recovery. A temporary TSH rebound can also occur during recovery. Neither reverse T3 measurement nor thyroid ultrasound usually answers the question in these vignettes (p1548).

## Amiodarone, hormone production and pituitary TSH

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-02bd4d5ac321fb03ca1d5e62 mcq-614440aa6ac525d6d42ed6f2" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>First distinguish an expected drug effect from overt thyrotoxicosis. Soon after amiodarone is started, a transient TSH rise with higher free T4 and lower T3 can occur in a previously normal gland. This was the December 2021 pattern. By contrast, a suppressed TSH with high free hormone and symptoms calls for treatment. Damaged follicles leak preformed hormone in destructive thyroiditis. Corticosteroids treat that process. Autonomous nodules or Graves tissue can instead increase synthesis after an iodine load, for which methimazole is appropriate (pp1547–1548).

An apparently normal prior history favors destructive thyroiditis but does not prove it. Because amiodarone provides abundant iodine, uptake scanning cannot reliably separate the mechanisms during exposure. The book describes initial combined corticosteroids and methimazole when the mechanism is uncertain. A beta blocker may control tachycardia or tremor without treating the excess hormone itself (p1550). Amiodarone accumulates in tissues and effects can persist for months. Thyroid dysfunction can usually be managed while the antiarrhythmic is continued (pp1547–1548).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-9bddccff96cb66a3829e208c" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Cold intolerance, fatigue, constipation and weight gain after starting amiodarone raise a different question. The iodine-containing drug may be associated with hypothyroidism, especially with underlying Hashimoto disease. Start with TSH, then interpret an abnormal result with free T4. TBG is a transport protein and ultrasound describes the gland's structure. Neither measures whether the patient has sufficient circulating hormone (Table 98-1, pp1547–1548, 1552).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-cb89f90016ad0e9c97dcff99" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>In Table 98-1, dopamine, glucocorticoids, octreotide and bexarotene decrease TSH secretion. Lithium appears under changes in thyroid hormone secretion, sertraline under increased hepatic thyroid hormone metabolism, and tamoxifen under increased TBG. These entries act at different points between pituitary signaling and peripheral hormone availability. June 2025 Basic Q118 accepted lithium and prednisone, although only glucocorticoids appear in the reduced-TSH category (pp1547–1548).

## Age, hormone levels and symptoms

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-af57ecd77b2b3894df010e23 mcq-7400870fc10d03a49c2e9070" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>TSH can rise gradually while free T4 remains stable in people without thyroid disease or thyroid autoimmunity. Reduced clearance balances reduced production. TBG concentration stays stable, while thyroid autoantibodies become more common in older populations. In a reference population without thyroid disease, the upper TSH limit was 7.5 mIU/L at age 80 and over, compared with 3.6 at ages 20–29. A TSH of 6.1 with normal free T4 in a well 82-year-old fits that age-related shift. The same reasoning would not explain a low free T4 with a TSH of 40 (pp1546, 1554).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7f4a3ed0b5c9a81e1780006a" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>In the study reproduced in Table 98-3, 16% of hyperthyroid patients aged at least 70 had confusion, compared with none aged 50 or younger. Tachycardia was still common at 71% versus 96%. Sweating was less common at 24% versus 95%, as was diarrhea at 18% versus 43%. Those are frequencies in the study groups, not diagnostic cutoffs. An older patient may present with apathy, loss of appetite, atrial fibrillation or heart failure even without tremor or obvious goiter (p1549).

## Overt primary hypothyroidism and a rising TSH on treatment

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-a9564119e3f03b2dcc342926" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>High TSH with low free T4 means overt primary hypothyroidism. In the postoperative 82-year-old, TSH of 40 with low free T4 warrants treatment despite the recent fracture and nonspecific fatigue. In people aged 65 or older with cardiac disease, begin with a small levothyroxine dose and increase cautiously. The chapter gives 25–50 micrograms as examples. For stable primary hypothyroidism with known coronary artery disease, the ATA advises starting oral levothyroxine at 12.5–25 micrograms daily and titrating to symptoms and TSH ([ATA, section 4b](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2014.0028)). TSH is usually reassessed after six weeks and some older people need seven to eight weeks for equilibration. Beyond age 80 a TSH target of 4–6 mU/L may be appropriate. Excess replacement increases the risks of atrial fibrillation and bone loss (p1552).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-73208d3bcb5312abed0b44aa" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>When previously stable TSH rises, ask about missed doses and how tablets are taken before increasing the prescription. Missing one 112-microgram tablet every week lowers the average daily exposure to 96 micrograms. Calcium carbonate and oral iron bind levothyroxine in the gut. A proton pump inhibitor such as esomeprazole can also decrease absorption. Carbamazepine can increase dose requirements by accelerating metabolism. The May 2024 sitting accepted all four choices, but Table 98-7 assigns carbamazepine to a different mechanism from the three absorption examples (pp1552–1553).

A high TSH with normal free T4 requires a different decision. Confirm a first TSH of 4.5–14.9 mU/L in one to three months and a first TSH of at least 15 mU/L in one to two weeks. In adults aged at least 65, Figure 98-4 recommends treatment for persistent TSH of at least 10 mU/L, consideration at 7.0–9.9 mU/L and observation for lower persistent elevations. At the milder end, a randomized trial did not demonstrate improvement in fatigue or thyroid symptoms in older adults. Cardiovascular associations behind the thresholds do not prove that treatment prevents those events (pp1553–1554, Figure 98-4).

## Thyroid nodules and the decision to aspirate

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-6593d3a8fd894eded1a95488" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Use dedicated thyroid ultrasound to assess lesion size, margins, shape, echogenicity, calcification pattern and cervical lymph nodes. Microcalcifications within a nodule increase concern, as do irregular margins and a taller-than-wide shape. The June 2025 question says “calcifications” broadly. The book specifies microcalcifications. A palpable and an incidental nonpalpable nodule of equal size have similar risk. Multiple nodules carry similar patient-level risk to a solitary one, so assess each lesion on its own features (p1555).

The December 2021 vignette gives normal thyroid function and a solid 2-cm nodule with microcalcifications. It warrants ultrasound-guided FNA. A purely cystic lesion generally does not. If TSH is low, a radionuclide scan establishes whether the nodule is functioning. A hot nodule rarely contains malignancy and usually needs no cytologic evaluation. For indeterminate aspirates, molecular testing can refine risk. Even a cytologically benign goiter may need surgery if it compresses the trachea or esophagus (pp1555–1556, Table 98-8).

## Additional decisions across the chapter

When TSH is low, measure free T4 and consider T3. Autonomous nodules can cause isolated T3 elevation. TSH-receptor antibodies and uptake patterns can help distinguish Graves disease or autonomous tissue from thyroiditis when the patient has no confounding iodine exposure. A patient with delirium, extreme tachycardia or heart failure from thyrotoxicosis needs urgent hospital management. Antithyroid drugs block synthesis and do little for hormone already leaking from damaged follicles. Radioiodine can treat a persistent autonomous nodule or Graves disease. In older patients thyroidectomy is reserved for selected situations because of operative risk (pp1549–1551).

The chapter distinguishes asymptomatic screening from evaluating a clinical suspicion. Symptoms can be sparse and TSH is readily measured, but common subclinical findings have uncertain treatment benefits. The cited USPSTF assessment finds insufficient evidence for routine screening. This does not prevent testing in a patient with compatible illness. When central hypothyroidism is a concern, assess both TSH and free T4 (pp1548, 1552–1554).

After a first benign aspirate, page 1556 gives an initial ultrasound interval of 12–18 months. ATA recommendation 23 uses the ultrasound pattern. A highly suspicious nodule needs repeat ultrasound and US-guided aspiration within 12 months. For low or intermediate suspicion, repeat ultrasound at 12–24 months. For very low suspicion, any repeat scan should be at least 24 months later. New suspicious features or significant growth may warrant reassessment. After two benign US-guided aspirates, cancer surveillance of that nodule can stop. A large nodule may still need review if it causes pressure symptoms ([ATA, recommendation 23](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2015.0020)). Papillary carcinoma is the common follicular-cell cancer and can be more aggressive in older people. Extent of surgery, TSH suppression and postoperative radioiodine depend on risk. Medullary cancer arises from C cells, which lack the sodium-iodide symporter, so radioactive iodine has no therapeutic effect. A rapidly growing neck mass can be anaplastic carcinoma or thyroid lymphoma. Lymphoma often arises with Hashimoto disease and requires a large-needle or surgical biopsy because treatment differs (pp1555–1556).

## Summary

### Physiology & normal aging changes
- T4 is the major secreted hormone, at **11×** the secretion rate of T3; only **0.04%** of T4 and **0.4%** of T3 circulate free (biologically active) — the rest is protein-bound (TBG, prealbumin, albumin) (p1545).
- Free T4 is preferred over total T4 for assessment, since binding-protein levels vary (p1545).
- TSH gradually **rises** with normal aging in euthyroid people without thyroid disease — this is NOT occult thyroid failure (free T4 stays normal) (p1546).
- With age: T4 production falls **80→60 μg/day**, T3 production falls **30→20 μg/day**, and T4 half-life rises from **7 to 9 days** by age 80+. TBG concentration does **not** change with age (p1546).
- Reference range shifts with age: upper limit of normal TSH is **3.6 mIU/L** at age 20-29 vs **7.5 mIU/L** at 80+ — the reference range may differ between older and younger people (p1554).

### Drugs, nonthyroidal illness
- Amiodarone is **37%** organic iodide by weight (p1547); can cause thyrotoxicosis from a destructive thyroiditis (treat with corticosteroids) or from increased hormone production in underlying nodular disease/Graves (treat with methimazole) — often hard to distinguish, so both may be given initially — or hypothyroidism in Hashimoto glands. In most cases amiodarone should not be stopped (p1547-1548).
- High-dose corticosteroids and dopamine can each suppress TSH without true thyroid dysfunction — relevant in hospitalized patients (p1548).
- Nonthyroidal illness ("euthyroid sick") syndrome: low T3 first, then low free T4, then mild low TSH in the sickest patients; the full abnormal constellation carries **80%** mortality (p1548). Do not treat with thyroid hormone.

### Hyperthyroidism
- Overt hyperthyroidism = low TSH + elevated free T4 or T3; prevalence **1-2%** in older people (p1549).
- In older overtly hyperthyroid patients, only **3 of 19** signs reach 50% prevalence: tachycardia (**71%**), fatigue (**56%**), weight loss (**50%**) (two exceed 50%; Table 98-3, p1549) — classic hyperadrenergic signs (tremor, sweating, heat intolerance, nervousness) are far less common than in the young. "**Apathetic hyperthyroidism**" — goiter and ophthalmopathy often absent (p1549).
- Methimazole is preferred over PTU (longer half-life, fewer adverse effects); PTU carries a fulminant hepatic failure risk. Skin reactions occur in **4-6%**, agranulocytosis in **0.1-0.5%** (p1550).
- With antithyroid drugs, even in good candidates (Graves with mild-moderate hyperthyroidism and a small gland), recurrence exceeds **60%** (p1550).
- Radioactive iodine: stop antithyroid drugs 3-5 days before; observe contact precautions for **5 days** after treatment (p1550).
- Subclinical hyperthyroidism = low TSH, normal free T4/T3; prevalence **1-6%**, persistence **>50%** on repeat testing. Confirm persistence. For adults aged 65 and older with endogenous subclinical hyperthyroidism, the ATA recommends treatment when TSH stays below 0.1 mU/L, and consideration of treatment when it remains between 0.1 mU/L and the lower reference limit. Consider symptoms, cardiac disease and osteoporosis (p1551). [ATA recommendations 83 and 85](https://doi.org/10.1089/thy.2016.0229).

### Hypothyroidism
- Overt hypothyroidism = high TSH + low free T4; prevalence **1-2%**. T3 is not useful for assessing hypothyroidism (p1551).
- Only **2 of 24** signs exceed 50% prevalence in older hypothyroid patients: fatigue (**68%**) and weakness (**53%**) (p1551).
- Full replacement dose in older adults is **lower** than the young-adult **1.6 μg/kg/day**. With known coronary artery disease, start oral levothyroxine at **12.5–25 μg/day** and increase cautiously according to symptoms and TSH (p1552, ATA section 4b).
- TSH testing usually occurs **6 weeks** after initiation (6 half-lives); older patients may need **7-8 weeks** to reach steady state. Target TSH is upper-normal, up to **4-6 mU/L** in those >80 (p1552).
- IV levothyroxine dose = **75%** of the oral dose (p1552).
- Missing **1 dose/week** on a 112 μg daily regimen drops the effective average dose to 96 μg — check adherence before assuming disease progression when TSH varies (p1552).
- Up to **40%** of older adults on thyroid hormone replacement have a low TSH (iatrogenic overreplacement) — risk mirrors endogenous hyperthyroidism (AF, bone loss) (p1552).
- Subclinical hypothyroidism = high TSH, normal free T4; prevalence **4%** general population, **~10%** at 65+. Retest in **1-3 months** if TSH 4.5-14.9 mIU/L, or **1-2 weeks** if ≥15.0 (p1553). *(Figure 98-4, p1554, confirms mU/L; the p1553 prose misprints the unit.)*
- TSH **≥10 mIU/L** predicts CV mortality, CHD, and heart failure; TSH **≥7 mIU/L** predicts CV mortality alone (p1553). Per Figure 98-4, treat if TSH is persistently **≥10.0 mU/L** (age ≥65); consider treatment at **7.0-9.9 mU/L** (p1553). *(Figure 98-4, p1554, uses mU/L; the prose on p1553 misprints mU/mL.)*
- **Myxedema coma**: give IV hydrocortisone **before/with** thyroid hormone — treating with thyroid hormone alone can precipitate adrenal crisis. Initial IV levothyroxine **200–400 μg**, using the lower end for older patients or those with cardiac disease, then the chapter gives **75–100 μg/day**. Rapid T3 exposure may increase cardiac risk (p1553; ATA recommendation 21a).

### Thyroid nodules & cancer
- **50%** of thyroid glands have a nodule at autopsy; malignancy is present in **7-9%** of thyroid nodules, higher in men than women. Evaluation does not differ by age; nonpalpable nodules carry the same risk as palpable nodules of the same size, and multiple nodules the same risk as a solitary nodule (p1555).
- FNA indicated: nodules **≥1 cm** solid + hypoechoic; **≥1.5 cm** isoechoic/hyperechoic solid; abnormal cervical lymph nodes. FNA **or observation**: mixed cystic-solid or spongiform nodules **≥2 cm**. FNA not required: purely cystic nodules or nodules **<1 cm**; low TSH prompts radionuclide scanning, and functioning hot nodules do not require cytologic evaluation (Table 98-8, p1555).
- After a benign FNA, match surveillance to ultrasound risk. **High suspicion** calls for repeat ultrasound and US-guided FNA within 12 months. **Low or intermediate suspicion** calls for ultrasound at 12–24 months. With **very low suspicion**, wait at least 24 months if rescanning. Two benign US-guided FNAs end routine surveillance for malignancy in that nodule (p1556, ATA recommendation 23).
- Thyroid cancer incidence peaks at age **65-69** (**29.6/100,000**); 5-year survival is **98%** (age 50-64), **97%** (65-74), **88%** (75+) (p1556).
- Lobectomy (instead of total thyroidectomy) is an option for tumors **1-4 cm** without extrathyroidal extension or node involvement (p1556).
- **Anaplastic** thyroid carcinoma: mean age at diagnosis **65**; median survival only **5 months** — worst-prognosis thyroid cancer (p1556).
- Medullary thyroid carcinoma: RAI has no effect (C-cells lack NIS); all patients need *RET* germline testing (p1556).

### Book contradiction (exam trap)
- The book's own **"Hypothyroidism"** section heading (p1551) is immediately followed by the sentence "Overt **hyper**thyroidism is defined as a high TSH with low free T4 concentrations" — a genuine misprint in the source text (verified against the page image, not a transcription error here). Read it as **hypothyroidism**; do not let a quick skim flip the diagnosis in your head.

## Recall

**1. By roughly what factor does T4 secretion exceed T3 secretion from the thyroid, and what fraction of each circulates free?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

T4 secretion is about 11× that of T3. Only 0.04% of T4 and 0.4% of T3 circulate free (biologically active); the rest is protein-bound (p1545).

</details>

**3. How does the reference range for "normal" TSH differ between a 25-year-old and an 85-year-old?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Upper limit of normal is about 3.6 mIU/L at age 20-29 versus 7.5 mIU/L at age 80+, when the 95% confidence interval is applied to a population free of thyroid disease (p1554).

</details>

**4. What are the two distinct mechanisms by which amiodarone can cause thyrotoxicosis, and how are they treated differently?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Increased thyroid hormone production in an underlying nodular or Graves gland is treated with methimazole; destructive thyroiditis from a direct toxic effect is treated acutely with corticosteroids. They can be hard to tell apart, so patients may initially get both. In most cases amiodarone should not be stopped (p1547-1548).

</details>

**5. What is the mortality rate associated with the full constellation of thyroid-test abnormalities in nonthyroidal illness syndrome, and should it be treated with thyroid hormone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Mortality is about 80% with the full pattern; thyroid hormone replacement (T4 or T3) gives no benefit (p1548).

</details>

**6. In older patients with overt hyperthyroidism, which three signs reach at least 50% prevalence, and how does this differ from younger patients?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Tachycardia (71%), fatigue (56%), and weight loss (50%) (Table 98-3, p1549). Classic hyperadrenergic signs (tremor, sweating, nervousness, heat intolerance) are far less common than in young patients — "apathetic hyperthyroidism" (p1549).

</details>

**7. Why is methimazole generally preferred over propylthiouracil (PTU) for Graves disease, and what are their shared adverse-effect rates?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Methimazole has a longer half-life and fewer adverse effects; PTU carries a risk of fulminant hepatic failure. Both cause skin reactions in 4-6% and agranulocytosis in 0.1-0.5% of patients (p1550).

</details>

**8. In older patients with overt hypothyroidism, which two signs exceed 50% prevalence?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fatigue (68%) and weakness (53%) — the only two of 24 signs studied that exceeded 50% (p1551).

</details>

**9. What levothyroxine dosing approach is recommended for a 70-year-old with cardiac disease starting treatment for hypothyroidism, and when should TSH be rechecked?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

For stable primary hypothyroidism with known coronary artery disease, start oral levothyroxine at 12.5–25 μg daily and titrate gradually according to cardiac symptoms and TSH (ATA section 4b). TSH testing usually occurs at 6 weeks after initiation (6 half-lives), though older patients may need 7-8 weeks to reach steady state; target TSH toward the upper-normal range, up to 4-6 mU/L if over age 80 (p1552).

</details>

**11. What proportion of older adults on thyroid hormone replacement have a low TSH from overreplacement, and what are the associated risks?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 40%. Risks mirror endogenous hyperthyroidism, most notably atrial fibrillation and bone loss (p1552).

</details>

**12. At what TSH threshold is treatment of subclinical hypothyroidism recommended in a patient aged 65 or older, and at what threshold should it be considered?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Treat if TSH is persistently ≥10.0 mU/L; consider treatment if TSH is 7.0-9.9 mU/L (p1553-1554). *(Figure 98-4, p1554, uses mU/L; the prose on p1553 misprints mU/mL.)*

</details>

**14. What thyroid nodule characteristics indicate FNA is required versus not required (Table 98-8)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

FNA indicated: nodules ≥1 cm that are solid and hypoechoic, ≥1.5 cm isoechoic/hyperechoic solid, or abnormal cervical lymph nodes. FNA or observation: mixed cystic-solid or spongiform nodules ≥2 cm. FNA not required: purely cystic nodules or nodules <1 cm; low TSH prompts radionuclide scanning, and functioning hot nodules do not require cytologic evaluation (p1555).

</details>

**15. What is the median survival for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, and why does radioactive iodine not work for medullary thyroid carcinoma?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Median survival for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma is about 5 months. Radioactive iodine has no effect on medullary thyroid carcinoma because the C-cells do not contain the sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) (p1556).

</details>

>   - Small intestinal diseases: celiac disease, *Helicobacter pylori*-associated gastritis, atrophic gastritis, Crohn disease, short bowel
> - *Increased levothyroxine metabolism*: phenobarbital, carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampin
> - *Increased thyroid-binding globulin*: estrogens, hepatitis
> - *Decreased thyroid-binding globulin*: androgen use, progressive liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, severe systemic illness
