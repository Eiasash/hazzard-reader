# Chapter 44 Study: Sleep Disorders

## What the exams asked

- Normal aging lengthens the time to fall asleep. [June 2023 Q43](?chapter=44#exam-p643-mcq-c6818d990da1a7ac4b1db4da)
- A hypnogram shows more wakefulness after sleep onset and less deep sleep with age. [June 2026 Q50](?chapter=44#exam-p644-mcq-722507b469ee330def4732a6)
- Shouting or punching during dreams can precede Parkinson disease. [September 2024 Q34](?chapter=44#exam-p654-mcq-eebcff8ec2efad1443c4bb06)
- Parkinson disease, loss of smell and nightmares during probable REM sleep raise suspicion for REM sleep behavior disorder. [September 2024 Q56](?chapter=44#exam-p655-mcq-4c92bbd3cca86626e195a256)
- A fall out of bed during a dream makes injury prevention and selection of an RBD treatment urgent. [June 2025 Q35](?chapter=44#exam-p655-mcq-0e047bf953f0e6f426a35fb0)
- Restless legs syndrome has several drug options, with important limits on when to use levodopa and opioids. [May 2024 Q94](?chapter=44#exam-p653-mcq-b279e83e228404a30ed965fe)
- Day–night reversal in a nursing-home resident with advanced dementia calls for daytime light and activity. [June 2026 Q78](?chapter=44#exam-p663-mcq-7049069651d5b4f5ebce9c85)
- Repeated falls during escalating brotizolam use call for tapering and behavioral insomnia treatment. [2020 Q22](?chapter=44#asked-chapter)
- Z-drugs shorten sleep latency only slightly and can cause delirium and falls. The exam cites the 2019 AGS Beers guide. [2020 Q56](?chapter=44#asked-chapter)
- Nocturia with morning headache and difficult-to-control hypertension warrants sleep testing even without reported snoring. [December 2021 Q54](?chapter=44#asked-chapter)
- A beta-blocker can contribute to new insomnia. [June 2022 Q14](?chapter=44#asked-chapter)

## Sleep architecture and normal aging

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-c6818d990da1a7ac4b1db4da mcq-722507b469ee330def4732a6" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>
**Sleep latency** is the time from trying to sleep until sleep begins. **Wake after sleep onset** adds up the time awake after sleep first begins. Both can increase with age. The second change is visible in Figure 44-1 as more time awake during the sleep period. Sleep efficiency is time asleep divided by time in bed. Remaining in bed for the same duration while spending more of it awake therefore lowers efficiency. Hazzard 8e, pp. 643–644, Figure 44-1.

Sleep cycles alternate between NREM and REM roughly every 90 minutes. N3 is the deep, slow-wave stage of NREM. Its contribution falls with age, particularly in men. REM is a different state in which vivid dreams are common and skeletal muscles are normally quiet. The book describes inconsistent evidence for an age-related change in REM duration. New daytime sleepiness, repeated nocturia or frequent painful awakenings warrant an assessment for contributory illness and medications. Hazzard 8e, pp. 643–644.

## Dream enactment, Parkinson disease and injury

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-eebcff8ec2efad1443c4bb06" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>
A bed partner may describe shouting, punching or kicking in sleep. In REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), REM atonia fails and the sleeper can act out a dream. Ask about both injuries and near misses. Overnight video polysomnography with muscle recordings can document abnormal activity during REM and help distinguish obstructive respiratory arousals, nocturnal seizures and NREM arousal disorders. Isolated RBD can precede parkinsonism, cognitive symptoms or autonomic dysfunction for years. The chapter describes high conversion rates in longitudinal clinical cohorts. An individual time to conversion cannot be inferred from those cohorts. Hazzard 8e, pp. 653–655, Figure 44-8.

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-4c92bbd3cca86626e195a256 mcq-0e047bf953f0e6f426a35fb0" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>
Parkinson disease, hyposmia and constipation make the clinical context especially suggestive. Nightmares alone are insufficient to diagnose RBD. Ask what the patient physically did during the dream and obtain a bed-partner history. A hip fracture after falling out of bed gives a concrete treatment goal. Lower the bed, remove hazards close to the sleeper, pad sharp furniture and protect the bed partner. If medication is needed, the book favors melatonin in many older adults because clonazepam can worsen sedation and balance. Bupropion is an exception among the antidepressants commonly implicated as precipitants. Hazzard 8e, pp. 654–655.

Both treatment questions offer melatonin under the brand Circadin. The AASM conditionally supports **immediate-release melatonin** for RBD secondary to a medical condition. Evidence for prolonged-release melatonin is insufficient for a recommendation. A small Parkinson disease trial found no reduction in dream-enactment frequency with 4 mg prolonged-release melatonin compared with placebo. [AASM, recommendation 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071384/) · [Gilat et al., Parkinson disease trial](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.27886)

## Restless legs, iron and periodic movements

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b279e83e228404a30ed965fe" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>
Restless legs syndrome (RLS) produces an urge to move while awake. Sitting or lying still brings it on, movement relieves it, and late afternoon or evening is typically worst. Ask about iron deficiency, kidney disease and exacerbating medications. In contrast, periodic limb movements occur during sleep. They can accompany RLS and several other sleep disorders. Frequent movements on a recording warrant a PLMD diagnosis only when they explain clinically significant sleep or daytime impairment and other causes have been excluded. Hazzard 8e, pp. 651–652.

Table 44-2 includes pregabalin, carbidopa/levodopa and tramadol. Magnesium is absent. Inclusion in the table is a different question from choosing an initial prescription. Pregabalin and other alpha-2-delta ligands reduce sensory symptoms and may also help concurrent neuropathic pain. Renal impairment increases concern about accumulation and sedation. Levodopa is reserved for occasional symptoms because augmentation can make symptoms start earlier, become more intense or spread. Opioids are reserved for refractory RLS. The AASM gives pregabalin a strong recommendation and suggests against standard use of levodopa. Hazzard 8e, pp. 652–653, Table 44-2. [AASM RLS guideline, recommendations 3 and 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/)

## Daytime light in residential care

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7049069651d5b4f5ebce9c85" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>
In moderate-to-severe dementia, impaired circadian signaling can combine with long daytime periods in bed, limited light or activity and nighttime disruption. Check the resident’s actual 24-hour routine with staff. Promote daytime light, activity within the resident’s abilities, regular waking and mealtimes, and a dark, quiet night. Advanced cognitive impairment may limit participation in formal CBT-I. Caregivers can deliver behavioral interventions. CPAP treats confirmed sleep-disordered breathing and sedatives can add confusion and falls. Hazzard 8e, pp. 662–663, Table 44-9.

## Insomnia, medication review and nocturia

**Escalating hypnotic use.** A hypnotic can shorten the wait for sleep yet worsen next-day balance. In a patient taking brotizolam nightly for years, review falls and dependence, agree on a gradual taper and offer CBT-I during withdrawal. CBT-I addresses the association between bed and wakefulness, inadequate sleep pressure and anxious beliefs about sleep. Simply switching hypnotics leaves these factors untreated. The 2019 AGS Beers criteria describe limited improvement in latency and duration with Z-drugs and clinically important risks of delirium, falls and fractures. Hazzard 8e, pp. 659–662. [Benzodiazepine deprescribing guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) · [AGS Beers criteria, 2019, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.15767)

**Nocturia with morning headache.** An older patient may wake after an apneic event and describe only the trip to the bathroom. Ask about unrefreshing sleep, witnessed pauses and daytime symptoms, and examine for predisposing factors. Difficult-to-control hypertension and obesity increase suspicion. A report of no snoring and a mildly enlarged prostate do not explain away the sleep-related pattern. Sleep testing answers the respiratory question, especially after bladder-directed therapy fails. Hazzard 8e, pp. 646–649.

**New insomnia on long-term medication.** Ask when each drug is taken and when the symptoms began. Table 44-3 includes beta-blockers in its alerting column. Of bisoprolol, enalapril, valsartan and doxazosin, the beta-blocker is the drug specifically highlighted in the exam. Sedating daytime medications can also impair nighttime sleep indirectly by promoting naps. Hazzard 8e, p. 658, Table 44-3.

## Other parasomnias and circadian timing

NREM parasomnias can involve behaviors during incomplete awakening. Ask about timing, responsiveness, dream recall and drugs, and consider polysomnography if a seizure or RBD is plausible. In a circadian phase disorder, sleep can be consolidated when the person follows their preferred schedule. A diary with actigraphy can distinguish clock timing from insomnia. Morning bright light generally advances the clock, and evening light generally delays it. For advanced sleep phase with undesirably early waking, light later in the day can move sleep later. Hazzard 8e, pp. 655–657.

## Summary

### Sleep & aging
- Sleep cycles roughly every **90 minutes**; N3 (slow-wave) sleep declines from early adulthood, more so in men; sleep efficiency falls with age (p643).
- Poor sleep tracks with comorbidity burden more than chronological age; excessive daytime sleepiness/napping is linked to amyloid-β accumulation — possible early marker of neurodegeneration (p644).

### Sleep-disordered breathing (OSA/CSA)
- The book diagnoses sleep-disordered breathing at **AHI >15/hour**, or **>5/hour** with significant symptoms or related comorbidities. **AHI >30/hour** generally indicates severe SDB (p645).

  > Current guidance (separate from the book): Adult obstructive sleep apnea can be diagnosed with **≥15 obstructive respiratory events/hour**, or **≥5/hour** with relevant symptoms or comorbidities. Severe OSA begins at **AHI ≥30/hour** (ICSD-3-TR, VA/DoD 2025 guideline, Appendix F).
- Age-dependent SDB risk factors (Table 44-1, p646): ↑BMI/central obesity, ↓muscle tension, altered airway anatomy, ↑airway collapsibility, ↓thyroid function, ↓lung volume.
- Moderate-to-severe SDB estimated in **6%** of women, **13%** of men in a 2007–2010 cohort of middle-aged adults; OSA underdiagnosed, especially in women (p646).
- CSA is **two to three times** more prevalent at age 65–90 vs 39–64; an estimated upwards of **50%** of stable heart-failure patients have some form of SDB (p646).
- Neck circumference **> 17 inches** (men) / **> 16 inches** (women) predicts SDB, less so in elderly; Friedman palate positions **III and IV** = high OSA risk (p647).
- Peripheral arterial tone–based home devices are not validated for central apneas and should not be used with alpha-blockers or atrial fibrillation; HSAT only for high pretest probability without significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity; a negative HSAT does not rule out OSA (p648–649).
- CPAP pressure usually **5 to 20 cm H2O**; observational studies found it well tolerated above age 80 and in mild-moderate dementia (p649).
- Only **50% to 70%** of patients remain CPAP-adherent at 1 year (p649).
- SERVE-HF found increased mortality with its ASV device in symptomatic chronic heart failure, **LVEF ≤45%** and predominantly central sleep apnea. Any consideration of ASV in HFrEF requires an experienced center, shared decision-making and close follow-up (p649; [SERVE-HF](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26323938/), AASM 2025 central sleep apnea guideline).
- Hypoglossal nerve stimulation trials limited to highly selected CPAP-intolerant patients with BMI **less than 32 kg/m2**; cohort of **62** patients aged 65–80 showed benefit (p650).

### RLS / periodic limb movement disorder
- RLS affects up to **8%** of older adults; **1.5 to 2 times** more common in women; **two to five times** more prevalent in chronic renal failure (p651).
- PLMS last **0.5–10 seconds**, occur **5–90 seconds** apart and comprise **at least four** consecutive movements. Adult PLMD requires **more than 15 movements/hour** with clinically significant sleep disturbance or daytime dysfunction unexplained by another disorder (p651; AASM 2025 RLS/PLMD guideline).
- PLMS coexist with RLS (**80%–90%**), RBD (**70%**), narcolepsy (**45%–65%**) — PLMD is a diagnosis of exclusion (p651).
- PLMS seen on PSG in up to **45%** of older adults, often not clinically significant (p652).
- Check ferritin and transferrin saturation in clinically significant RLS. AASM guidance considers iron at **ferritin ≤75 µg/L or transferrin saturation <20%**, with IV iron when ferritin is 75–100 µg/L if iron is indicated. The moderate–severe RLS trial permitting ferritin **75–300 µg/L** required **transferrin saturation <20%** in that range (p652; [trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5655783/), AASM 2025 RLS/PLMD guideline).
- Dopamine-agonist augmentation risk **2% to 15%** per year, worse with higher dose/duration (p652).
- **RLS drug selection.** The AASM favors gabapentin, gabapentin enacarbil and pregabalin when appropriate. FDA-labeled gabapentin enacarbil is **600 mg daily** for RLS with normal renal function; 1,200 mg adds adverse effects without additional benefit. Dopamine agonists can cause augmentation and behavioral disinhibition. If pramipexole is chosen, the labeled RLS titration is **0.125–0.5 mg once daily**. The labeled ropinirole range begins at **0.25 mg**, up to **4 mg daily**. Table 44-2 lists carbidopa/levodopa **25/100–25/200 mg** for infrequent use because of augmentation. Table 44-2 lists off-label gabapentin **100–1,800 mg** and pregabalin **300 mg** as studied doses. Adjust for renal function and monitor sedation. The table lists clonazepam **0.25–2 mg** and temazepam **15–30 mg**, with limited evidence and cognitive/fall risks. Its off-label opioid examples are codeine **30–60 mg**, tramadol **50–100 mg**, oxycodone **5–15 mg**, hydrocodone **5–15 mg** and methadone **5–10 mg**. These are historical table ranges. Specialist assessment of respiratory, dependence and medication risks is essential in refractory RLS (p653; FDA prescribing information for Horizant and pramipexole, AASM 2025 RLS/PLMD guideline).
  > Current practice (not the book): FDA-labelled RLS dosing of pramipexole is 0.125 mg once daily 2–3 h before bedtime, titrated if needed to 0.25 then 0.5 mg; no evidence that 0.75 mg adds benefit — US prescribing information (pramipexole tablets, DailyMed), 2026; Current practice (not the book): AASM conditionally suggests against standard pramipexole, transdermal rotigotine or ropinirole use in adults with RLS because of augmentation with longer-term treatment; evidence certainty is moderate for pramipexole/ropinirole and low for rotigotine; patients prioritizing short-term symptom relief over long-term adverse effects may still choose these agents - AASM 2025, recommendations 12-14, p144

### REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)
- Prevalence ~**1%** over age 40, ~**2%** over age 60; male-predominant, typically presents mid-60s (p654).
- Diagnosis requires REM sleep without atonia plus dream-enactment behavior (p654).
- In selected longitudinal cohorts, up to **90%** of people presenting with isolated RBD developed a clinically evident synucleinopathy (Parkinson, Lewy body dementia, MSA) over **10- to 15-year** follow-up (p654).
- Melatonin is the primary first choice in most circumstances; avoiding benzodiazepines is generally preferable in older patients. In a single-center retrospective study, only melatonin was associated with reduced injuries/falls and had a better side-effect profile than clonazepam (p655).

### Circadian rhythm disorders
- Advanced sleep-wake phase disorder affects ~**1%** of a middle-aged population; more common with aging (p657).
- Irregular sleep-wake rhythm common in Alzheimer, Parkinson, and Huntington disease; sundowning may be a phenotype of a circadian rhythm disturbance (p657).
- Tasimelteon is an MT1/MT2 agonist approved for **Non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder in adults** and for **nighttime sleep disturbances in Smith–Magenis syndrome**. The formulation for the latter depends on age (p657; [FDA Hetlioz prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/205677Orig1s013lbl.pdf)).

### Insomnia
- Chronic insomnia causes sleep difficulty despite adequate opportunity, with daytime consequences, **at least 3 nights/week for at least 3 months**. Short-term insomnia lasts **less than 3 months** (p657; ICSD-3-TR, VA/DoD 2025 guideline, Appendix F).
- Prevalence up to **10%** in young adults, rising to **30% to 48%** after age 65; more common in women; up to **40%** of caregivers use sleep aids (p658).
- In one study, **a third** of older patients had persistent severe insomnia at 4-year follow-up; **more than 80%** of women over 85 report sleeping difficulties (p658).
- CBT-I is first-line treatment for all adults with insomnia (p659).
- Stimulus control: get out of bed if not asleep within **20 minutes** (p662).
- Sleep restriction: time-in-bed = average sleep time **+ 15 minutes**, advance 15 min once efficiency **80%–85%**, goal ~**8 hours** (p662).
- Drugs: Benzodiazepines ↓ sleep latency ~**10 minutes**, ↑ total sleep time **30 to 60 minutes**; avoid long-acting flurazepam/quazepam; all Beers-listed (p659). Temazepam start **7.5 mg**, usual **7.5–30 mg**, half-life **8.8 h** — caution: psychomotor impairment, increased fall risk; eszopiclone start **1 mg**, usual **1–2 mg**, half-life **6 h** — caution: avoid high-fat meals, unpleasant taste/headache (Table 44-5, p660). Zolpidem/zaleplon — rapid onset, take immediately before bed; Beers-listed (p659). Ramelteon (MT1/MT2 agonist) — few side effects; lack of amnestic properties makes it somewhat less effective for subjective improvement in sleep (p660). Suvorexant and lemborexant are dual orexin receptor antagonists for insomnia. The suvorexant trial in mild–moderate probable Alzheimer dementia randomized **285**, of whom **277 completed**. Placebo-adjusted total sleep time improved by **28 minutes at week 4** (p660; [Herring et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31944580/), FDA Belsomra prescribing information). Doxepin low-dose **3–6 mg** for insomnia vs **100–300 mg** as an antidepressant (p661). Avoid diphenhydramine as primary therapy — long half-life, next-day sedation, rapid tolerance, dry mouth, urinary retention, delirium, decreased cognition (p661).

### Institutionalized / dementia
- Nocturnal wandering and confusion are a leading cause of institutionalization; CPAP can be used successfully in mild-moderate dementia, and treating SDB can improve cognitive function (p662–663).

## Recall

**1. What AHI value alone is diagnostic of SDB, and what value defines severe disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

AHI greater than 15/hour, or greater than 5/hour with significant symptoms or related comorbidities. An AHI greater than 30/hour generally indicates severe SDB (p645).

</details>

**3. What neck circumference cutoffs raise suspicion for SDB in men and women?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Greater than 17 inches in men, greater than 16 inches in women (predictive value is weaker in older patients) (p647).

</details>

**5. Which heart-failure population had excess mortality with the ASV device in SERVE-HF, and how does this inform referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

LVEF **≤45%** in symptomatic chronic heart failure with predominantly central sleep apnea. SERVE-HF found higher mortality with its ASV device in this population. The AASM limits consideration of ASV for central apnea in HFrEF to experienced centers with shared decision-making and close follow-up (p649). [AASM 2025, recommendation 4](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.11858)

</details>

**8. What is "augmentation" in RLS pharmacotherapy, and how often does it occur per year on long-acting dopamine agonists?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Worsening of RLS — earlier onset, increased intensity, and/or spread to arms/trunk; estimated at 2% to 15% per year, more likely with higher dose and longer duration (p652).

</details>

**10. What long-term risk of a clinically evident synucleinopathy has been described in selected cohorts presenting with isolated RBD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 90% over 10 to 15 years in selected clinical cohorts. This estimate applies to isolated RBD and is not an individual prediction (p654, Figure 44-8).

</details>

**11. What is the preferred first-choice medication for RBD in older adults, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Melatonin — in a single-center retrospective study, associated with fewer injuries/falls and a better side-effect profile than clonazepam; avoiding benzodiazepines is generally preferable in older patients (p655).

</details>

**15. Which two benzodiazepines should be avoided in older adults with insomnia because of next-day effects?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Flurazepam and quazepam (long-acting agents) (p659).

</details>
