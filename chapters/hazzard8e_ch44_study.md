# Sleep Disorders

Identify whether the main complaint is breathing, an urge to move, dream enactment or insomnia before selecting treatment.

## Sleep and aging

**Sleep latency** is the time from trying to sleep until sleep begins. **Wake after sleep onset** adds up the time awake after sleep first begins. Both can increase with age. The second change is visible in <a href="?chapter=44#book-figure-44-1">Figure 44-1</a> as more time awake during the sleep period. Sleep efficiency is time asleep divided by time in bed. Remaining in bed for the same duration while spending more of it awake therefore lowers efficiency. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p643">pp. 643</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p644">644</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#book-figure-44-1">Figure 44-1</a>.

Sleep cycles alternate between NREM and REM roughly every 90 minutes. N3 is the deep, slow-wave stage of NREM. Its contribution falls with age, particularly in men. REM is a different state in which vivid dreams are common and skeletal muscles are normally quiet. The book describes inconsistent evidence for an age-related change in REM duration. New daytime sleepiness, repeated nocturia or frequent painful awakenings warrant an assessment for contributory illness and medications. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p643">pp. 643</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p644">644</a>.

Sleep cycles roughly every **90 minutes**; N3 (slow-wave) sleep declines from early adulthood, more so in men; sleep efficiency falls with age (<a href="?chapter=44#p643">p643</a>).

Poor sleep tracks with comorbidity burden more than chronological age; excessive daytime sleepiness/napping is linked to amyloid-β accumulation — possible early marker of neurodegeneration (<a href="?chapter=44#p644">p644</a>).

## Sleep-disordered breathing

The book diagnoses sleep-disordered breathing at **AHI >15/hour**, or **>5/hour** with significant symptoms or related comorbidities. **AHI >30/hour** generally indicates severe SDB (<a href="?chapter=44#p645">p645</a>).

> **Current practice: Apnea diagnostic thresholds.** Adult obstructive sleep apnea can be diagnosed with **≥15 obstructive respiratory events/hour**, or **≥5/hour** with relevant symptoms or comorbidities. Severe OSA begins at **AHI ≥30/hour** (ICSD-3-TR, VA/DoD 2025 guideline, Appendix F).

Age-dependent SDB risk factors (<a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-1">Table 44-1</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#p646">p646</a>): ↑BMI/central obesity, ↓muscle tension, altered airway anatomy, ↑airway collapsibility, ↓thyroid function, ↓lung volume.

Moderate-to-severe SDB estimated in **6%** of women, **13%** of men in a 2007–2010 cohort of middle-aged adults; OSA underdiagnosed, especially in women (<a href="?chapter=44#p646">p646</a>).

CSA is **two to three times** more prevalent at age 65–90 vs 39–64; an estimated upwards of **50%** of stable heart-failure patients have some form of SDB (<a href="?chapter=44#p646">p646</a>).

Neck circumference **> 17 inches** (men) / **> 16 inches** (women) predicts SDB, less so in elderly; Friedman palate positions **III and IV** = high OSA risk (<a href="?chapter=44#p647">p647</a>).

Peripheral arterial tone–based home devices are not validated for central apneas and should not be used with alpha-blockers or atrial fibrillation; HSAT only for high pretest probability without significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity; a negative HSAT does not rule out OSA (<a href="?chapter=44#p648">p648</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p649">649</a>).

CPAP pressure usually **5 to 20 cm H2O**; observational studies found it well tolerated above age 80 and in mild-moderate dementia (<a href="?chapter=44#p649">p649</a>).

Only **50% to 70%** of patients remain CPAP-adherent at 1 year (<a href="?chapter=44#p649">p649</a>).

Hazzard reports greater mortality with ASV for central sleep apnea in heart failure with **EF <45%** and generally advises against ASV in this group (<a href="?chapter=44#p649">p649</a>).

> **Current practice: Adaptive servo-ventilation in heart failure.** SERVE-HF found increased mortality with its ASV device in symptomatic chronic heart failure, **LVEF ≤45%** and predominantly central sleep apnea. Any consideration of ASV in HFrEF requires an experienced center, shared decision-making and close follow-up ([SERVE-HF](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26323938/), AASM 2025 central sleep apnea guideline).

Hypoglossal nerve stimulation trials limited to highly selected CPAP-intolerant patients with BMI **less than 32 kg/m2**; cohort of **62** patients aged 65–80 showed benefit (<a href="?chapter=44#p650">p650</a>).

## Restless legs and periodic limb movement

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) produces an urge to move while awake. Sitting or lying still brings it on, movement relieves it, and late afternoon or evening is typically worst. Ask about iron deficiency, kidney disease and exacerbating medications. In contrast, periodic limb movements occur during sleep. They can accompany RLS and several other sleep disorders. Frequent movements on a recording warrant a PLMD diagnosis only when they explain clinically significant sleep or daytime impairment and other causes have been excluded. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p651">pp. 651</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p652">652</a>.

RLS affects up to **8%** of older adults; **1.5 to 2 times** more common in women; **two to five times** more prevalent in chronic renal failure (<a href="?chapter=44#p651">p651</a>).

PLMS last **0.5–10 seconds**, occur **5–90 seconds** apart and comprise **at least four** consecutive movements. Hazzard requires **at least 15 movements per hour** in addition to the clinical criteria for PLMD (<a href="?chapter=44#p651">pp651</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p652">652</a>).

> **Current practice: Periodic limb movement disorder.** The AASM adult PLMD definition uses **>15/hour** with clinically significant sleep disturbance or daytime dysfunction unexplained by another disorder. [AASM RLS guideline, recommendations 3 and 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/).

PLMS coexist with RLS (**80%–90%**), RBD (**70%**), narcolepsy (**45%–65%**) — PLMD is a diagnosis of exclusion (<a href="?chapter=44#p651">p651</a>).

Hazzard recommends iron for RLS when ferritin is **<75 μg/L**. It describes evidence for IV iron in moderate-to-severe RLS with ferritin **<300 μg/L**, although IV iron is usually reserved for malabsorption (<a href="?chapter=44#p652">p652</a>).

> **Current practice: Iron thresholds in restless legs.** Check ferritin and transferrin saturation in clinically significant RLS. AASM guidance considers iron at **ferritin ≤75 µg/L or transferrin saturation <20%**, with IV iron when ferritin is 75–100 µg/L if iron is indicated. The moderate–severe RLS trial permitting ferritin **75–300 µg/L** required **transferrin saturation <20%** in that range ([trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5655783/), AASM 2025 RLS/PLMD guideline).

<a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-2">Table 44-2</a> includes pregabalin, carbidopa/levodopa and tramadol. Magnesium is absent. Hazzard lists dopamine agonists as the established treatment of choice and also discusses alpha-2-delta ligands for first-line treatment (<a href="?chapter=44#p652">pp652</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p653">653</a>). Pregabalin and other alpha-2-delta ligands reduce sensory symptoms and may also help concurrent neuropathic pain. Renal impairment increases concern about accumulation and sedation. Levodopa is reserved for occasional symptoms because augmentation can make symptoms start earlier, become more intense or spread. Opioids are reserved for refractory RLS.

> **Current practice: Pregabalin versus levodopa.** The AASM gives pregabalin a strong recommendation and suggests against standard use of levodopa. [AASM RLS guideline, recommendations 3 and 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/)

Dopamine-agonist augmentation risk **2% to 15%** per year, worse with higher dose/duration (<a href="?chapter=44#p652">p652</a>).

<a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-2">Table 44-2</a> lists **pramipexole 0.125–2 mg**, **ropinirole 0.25–4 mg** and **gabapentin enacarbil 600–1,200 mg** (<a href="?chapter=44#p653">p653</a>). <a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-2">Table 44-2</a> lists carbidopa/levodopa **25/100–25/200 mg** for infrequent use because of augmentation. <a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-2">Table 44-2</a> lists off-label gabapentin **100–1,800 mg** and pregabalin **300 mg** as studied doses. Adjust for renal function and monitor sedation. The table lists clonazepam **0.25–2 mg** and temazepam **15–30 mg**, with limited evidence and cognitive/fall risks. Its off-label opioid examples are codeine **30–60 mg**, tramadol **50–100 mg**, oxycodone **5–15 mg**, hydrocodone **5–15 mg** and methadone **5–10 mg**.

> **Current practice: Augmentation with dopamine agonists.** Gabapentin and gabapentin enacarbil are additional options when appropriate. Dopamine agonists can cause augmentation and behavioral disinhibition. US RLS labeling gives gabapentin enacarbil **600 mg daily** with normal renal function. FDA-labelled RLS dosing of pramipexole is 0.125 mg once daily 2–3 h before bedtime, titrated if needed to 0.25 then 0.5 mg; no evidence that 0.75 mg adds benefit — US prescribing information (pramipexole tablets, DailyMed), 2026; AASM conditionally suggests against standard pramipexole, transdermal rotigotine or ropinirole use in adults with RLS because of augmentation with longer-term treatment; evidence certainty is moderate for pramipexole/ropinirole and low for rotigotine; patients prioritizing short-term symptom relief over long-term adverse effects may still choose these agents - AASM 2025, recommendations 12-14, <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/">p144</a>

## REM behavior and other parasomnias

### Dream enactment, Parkinson disease and injury

A bed partner may describe shouting, punching or kicking in sleep. In REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), REM atonia fails and the sleeper can act out a dream. Ask about both injuries and near misses. Overnight video polysomnography with muscle recordings can document abnormal activity during REM and help distinguish obstructive respiratory arousals, nocturnal seizures and NREM arousal disorders. Isolated RBD can precede parkinsonism, cognitive symptoms or autonomic dysfunction for years. The chapter describes high conversion rates in longitudinal clinical cohorts. An individual time to conversion cannot be inferred from those cohorts. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p653">pp. 653</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p655">655</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#book-figure-44-8">Figure 44-8</a>.

Parkinson disease, hyposmia and constipation make the clinical context especially suggestive. Nightmares alone are insufficient to diagnose RBD. Ask what the patient physically did during the dream and obtain a bed-partner history. A hip fracture after falling out of bed gives a concrete treatment goal. Lower the bed, remove hazards close to the sleeper, pad sharp furniture and protect the bed partner. If medication is needed, the book favors melatonin in many older adults because clonazepam can worsen sedation and balance. Bupropion is an exception among the antidepressants commonly implicated as precipitants. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p654">pp. 654</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p655">655</a>.

In selected longitudinal cohorts, up to **90%** of people presenting with isolated RBD developed a clinically evident synucleinopathy (Parkinson, Lewy body dementia, MSA) over **10- to 15-year** follow-up (<a href="?chapter=44#p654">p654</a>).

Both examination questions offer melatonin under the brand Circadin. Hazzard includes melatonin and clonazepam among treatments for REM sleep behavior disorder (<a href="?chapter=44#p654">pp654</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p655">655</a>).

> **Current practice: Melatonin formulation.** The AASM conditionally supports **immediate-release melatonin** for RBD secondary to a medical condition. Evidence for prolonged-release melatonin is insufficient for a recommendation. A small Parkinson disease trial found no reduction in dream-enactment frequency with 4 mg prolonged-release melatonin compared with placebo. [AASM, recommendation 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071384/) · [Gilat et al., Parkinson disease trial](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.27886)

Prevalence ~**1%** over age 40, ~**2%** over age 60; male-predominant, typically presents mid-60s (<a href="?chapter=44#p654">p654</a>).

Diagnosis requires REM sleep without atonia plus dream-enactment behavior (<a href="?chapter=44#p654">p654</a>).

Melatonin is the primary first choice in most circumstances; avoiding benzodiazepines is generally preferable in older patients. In a single-center retrospective study, only melatonin was associated with reduced injuries/falls and had a better side-effect profile than clonazepam (<a href="?chapter=44#p655">p655</a>).

### Other parasomnias and circadian timing

NREM parasomnias can involve behaviors during incomplete awakening. Ask about timing, responsiveness, dream recall and drugs, and consider polysomnography if a seizure or RBD is plausible. In a circadian phase disorder, sleep can be consolidated when the person follows their preferred schedule. A diary with actigraphy can distinguish clock timing from insomnia. Morning bright light generally advances the clock, and evening light generally delays it. For advanced sleep phase with undesirably early waking, light later in the day can move sleep later. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p655">pp. 655</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p657">657</a>.

## Circadian timing and insomnia

**Escalating hypnotic use.** A hypnotic can shorten the wait for sleep yet worsen next-day balance. In a patient taking brotizolam nightly for years, review falls and dependence, agree on a gradual taper and offer CBT-I during withdrawal. CBT-I addresses the association between bed and wakefulness, inadequate sleep pressure and anxious beliefs about sleep. Simply switching hypnotics leaves these factors untreated. The 2019 AGS Beers criteria describe limited improvement in latency and duration with Z-drugs and clinically important risks of delirium, falls and fractures. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p659">pp. 659</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p662">662</a>. [Benzodiazepine deprescribing guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5951648/) · [AGS Beers criteria, 2019, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.15767)

CBT-I is first-line treatment for all adults with insomnia (<a href="?chapter=44#p659">p659</a>).

Sleep restriction: time-in-bed = average sleep time **+ 15 minutes**, advance 15 min once efficiency **80%–85%**, goal ~**8 hours** (<a href="?chapter=44#p662">p662</a>).

Drugs: Benzodiazepines ↓ sleep latency ~**10 minutes**, ↑ total sleep time **30 to 60 minutes**; avoid long-acting flurazepam/quazepam; all Beers-listed (<a href="?chapter=44#p659">p659</a>). Temazepam start **7.5 mg**, usual **7.5–30 mg**, half-life **8.8 h** — caution: psychomotor impairment, increased fall risk; eszopiclone start **1 mg**, usual **1–2 mg**, half-life **6 h** — caution: avoid high-fat meals, unpleasant taste/headache (<a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-5">Table 44-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#p660">p660</a>). Zolpidem/zaleplon — rapid onset, take immediately before bed; Beers-listed (<a href="?chapter=44#p659">p659</a>). Ramelteon (MT1/MT2 agonist) — few side effects; lack of amnestic properties makes it somewhat less effective for subjective improvement in sleep (<a href="?chapter=44#p660">p660</a>). Suvorexant and lemborexant are dual orexin receptor antagonists for insomnia. Hazzard describes **277 participants**, with sleep time increased by **about 30 minutes at two weeks** (<a href="?chapter=44#p660">p660</a>). Doxepin low-dose **3–6 mg** for insomnia vs **100–300 mg** as an antidepressant (<a href="?chapter=44#p661">p661</a>). Avoid diphenhydramine as primary therapy — long half-life, next-day sedation, rapid tolerance, dry mouth, urinary retention, delirium, decreased cognition (<a href="?chapter=44#p661">p661</a>).

> **Current practice: Suvorexant trial results.** The original trial randomized 285 people and 277 completed it. The placebo-adjusted gain was 28 minutes at week 4. [Herring et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31944580/).

**Nocturia with morning headache.** An older patient may wake after an apneic event and describe only the trip to the bathroom. Ask about unrefreshing sleep, witnessed pauses and daytime symptoms, and examine for predisposing factors. Difficult-to-control hypertension and obesity increase suspicion. A report of no snoring and a mildly enlarged prostate do not explain away the sleep-related pattern. Sleep testing answers the respiratory question, especially after bladder-directed therapy fails. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p646">pp. 646</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p649">649</a>.

**New insomnia on long-term medication.** Ask when each drug is taken and when the symptoms began. <a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-3">Table 44-3</a> includes beta-blockers in its alerting column. Of bisoprolol, enalapril, valsartan and doxazosin, review the beta-blocker as a possible contributor to poor sleep. Sedating daytime medications can also impair nighttime sleep indirectly by promoting naps. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p658">p. 658</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-3">Table 44-3</a>.

Advanced sleep-wake phase disorder affects ~**1%** of a middle-aged population; more common with aging (<a href="?chapter=44#p657">p657</a>).

Irregular sleep-wake rhythm common in Alzheimer, Parkinson, and Huntington disease; sundowning may be a phenotype of a circadian rhythm disturbance (<a href="?chapter=44#p657">p657</a>).

Hazzard describes tasimelteon as an MT1/MT2 agonist approved **only for non-24-hour sleep-wake rhythm disorder in adults** at the time of writing (<a href="?chapter=44#p657">p657</a>).

> **Current practice: Tasimelteon indications.** US labeling also includes nighttime sleep disturbances in Smith–Magenis syndrome, with formulation depending on age. [FDA Hetlioz prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/205677Orig1s013lbl.pdf).

The book classifies insomnia persisting **more than three months** as chronic and insomnia lasting **fewer than three months** as short term (<a href="?chapter=44#p657">p657</a>).

> **Current practice: Chronic insomnia criteria.** ICSD-3-TR requires sleep disturbance with daytime consequences on **≥3 nights/week for ≥3 months**, despite adequate opportunity. [VA/DoD 2025, Appendix F](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Guideline_final_20250915.pdf).

Prevalence up to **10%** in young adults, rising to **30% to 48%** after age 65; more common in women; up to **40%** of caregivers use sleep aids (<a href="?chapter=44#p658">p658</a>).

In one study, **a third** of older patients had persistent severe insomnia at 4-year follow-up; **more than 80%** of women over 85 report sleeping difficulties (<a href="?chapter=44#p658">p658</a>).

## Sleep in residential care

In moderate-to-severe dementia, impaired circadian signaling can combine with long daytime periods in bed, limited light or activity and nighttime disruption. Check the resident’s actual 24-hour routine with staff. Promote daytime light, activity within the resident’s abilities, regular waking and mealtimes, and a dark, quiet night. Advanced cognitive impairment may limit participation in formal CBT-I. Caregivers can deliver behavioral interventions. CPAP treats confirmed sleep-disordered breathing and sedatives can add confusion and falls. Hazzard 8e, <a href="?chapter=44#p662">pp. 662</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p663">663</a>, <a href="?chapter=44#book-table-44-9">Table 44-9</a>.

Nocturnal wandering and confusion are a leading cause of institutionalization; CPAP can be used successfully in mild-moderate dementia, and treating SDB can improve cognitive function (<a href="?chapter=44#p662">p662</a>–<a href="?chapter=44#p663">663</a>).

## Key numbers

- PLMS seen on PSG in up to **45%** of older adults, often not clinically significant (<a href="?chapter=44#p652">p652</a>).

- Stimulus control: get out of bed if not asleep within **20 minutes** (<a href="?chapter=44#p662">p662</a>).
