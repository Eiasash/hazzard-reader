# Chapter 12 Study: Age-Friendly Care

## Summary

**Use the framework as a set**
- Age-friendly care integrates **4Ms**: What Matters, Medication, Mentation and Mobility across care settings (p172).
- Apply them together to shift attention toward function, wellness and the person's goals; isolated disease plans can conflict (p172).
- High-functioning teams need shared purpose, clear roles, communication, mutual respect and recognition of each member's expertise (p171-172).
- Include older adults, families and caregivers as partners; the approach aims to improve experience, population health and cost of care (p172).

**Translate each element into care**
- What Matters: elicit and document specific health-outcome goals and care preferences, including end-of-life care, and use them consistently across providers (p172-173).
- For “What Matters” conversations, ask about preferred language and form of address, literacy, cultural beliefs and the desired role of family (Table 12-1, p174). The book lists hearing examinations separately among measures to maintain mobility (p176).
- Medication: prescribe when needed, review affordability and understanding, and avoid interference with mentation, mobility or patient priorities (p174-175).
- Physicians report limited time, poor awareness of medication harms, and concern about withdrawal or patient criticism as barriers to deprescribing. Review medicines with the team and involve the patient and caregiver (p174).
- Computerized support and STOPP/START can identify prescribing problems, but assessment and communication remain team responsibilities (p174-175).
- Mentation: prevent, identify and manage delirium, dementia and depression. Dementia is not normal aging (p175).
- Person-centered dementia care recognizes the individual, accepts the person's reality, supports meaningful engagement and relationships, and reassesses care (p175).
- Mobility: enable safe daily movement, strength and balance; address chronic-disease effects and use tailored physical-activity support (p176).

**Measure what changes**
- Integrate the framework into routine workflows and communicate across settings; use patient goals and functional outcomes to guide improvement (p172-177).
- The book's implementation examples report reduced utilization and cost in some systems; these are local results, not guaranteed effects everywhere (p176-177).
- Annual wellness visits can provide an opportunity to review the framework and engage families in a coordinated plan (p177).

### From a stated priority to a care plan

Ask which activity the person wants to keep doing and which parts of treatment are burdensome. Record the answer so the next clinician can act on it. In the priorities-based care example, patients with explicitly identified goals had fewer tests, referrals and procedures and were more likely to have unnecessary medicines stopped. The change in treatment burden was measured from the patient's perspective (p172–173).

An estimated **29%** of Medicare beneficiaries in **2015** filled a prescription for at least one drug on the contemporaneous Beers list of medicines to avoid. That figure describes dispensing. A review still needs the indication, expected benefit, harms and the person's goals. In a deprescribing intervention, pharmacists educated both the patient and prescriber, giving the patient a role in the decision (p174–175).

A depression screen can start a discussion about grief or persistent low mood. Arrange follow-up for assessment and treatment when indicated. When evaluating a local program, distinguish increased screening from changes in health. The Providence example reported a doubling of fall-risk and cognitive screening and a quadrupling of fall-risk interventions after staff training (p176–177).

### Why the four elements belong in the same plan

The age-friendly framework gives a shared clinical vocabulary to the people who contribute to an older adult's care. Its organizing question is whether the whole plan supports the outcomes the patient wants. A professionally appropriate intervention for one disease can still make it harder to walk, cloud thinking or impose work the person cannot sustain. Figure 12-2 places What Matters, Medication, Mentation and Mobility together. Each assessment should inform what the team does about the other three. A checklist with four completed boxes records activity; it does not show that anyone changed treatment in response (p171–173).

The Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Triple Aim brings together patient experience, population health and per-person cost. A useful intervention can affect all three, but the measures are not interchangeable. Asking patients what mattered to them measures whether the conversation happened. Reporting less burden or achieving a personal activity is an outcome from the patient's perspective. Reducing a hospital stay or avoiding an unnecessary test changes resource use. State which result was measured in an implementation example and whether there was a comparison group before claiming better health or lower costs (p172–173, p176–177).

The chapter gives the historical scale of the initiative and a growth target. Participation in an initiative is evidence of adoption, not proof that every older person in a participating organization receives an integrated plan on every encounter. A hospital, primary care clinic and home-based service may all work toward the same goals but need different workflows. A nurse seeing a patient several times daily may detect a change in attention; a community clinician who knows the baseline can interpret its novelty; family may know why the activity lost matters. The shared framework is meant to connect those observations across visits and settings (p171–173, p175–177).

### Team skills that make the clinical work possible

A high-functioning team needs both effective work and engaged relationships. The chapter describes effectiveness in terms of shared purpose, explicit roles, communication and the ability to recognize and respond to change. Engagement involves respect, trust, psychological safety and cohesion. Technical expertise is still necessary. A pharmacist may identify a sedating combination, a therapist observe instability and a caregiver describe the usual walking route. The team has learned little if each finding remains in a separate note and no one is responsible for reviewing the medicine, testing a practical alternative or following up on walking (p171–172).

Different professional perspectives can expose different causes. A clinician might first investigate disease, a social worker may identify an inaccessible home or unavailable transport, and a nurse may know what the patient actually does with a prescription. One discipline's observation need not displace the others. Clarify what all are trying to achieve and when to share information. Too little trust discourages speaking up; excessive emphasis on harmony can suppress an important disagreement. Invite a team member who observes an unsafe transfer or a change in thinking to describe it and ensure that someone can act on that report (p172).

Consider a transition after hospital treatment. Before discharge the patient explains that the important outcome is reaching the bathroom at home without help. A medicine given for an acute symptom may impair alertness; the distance and timing of toileting may exceed what was required on the ward. Ask the patient and family how movement will work at home, reassess the medicine and who will supervise its use, and determine what change in attention should trigger contact. This example applies all four elements to one decision rather than creating four unrelated referrals (p172–176).

### Turning What Matters into an outcome and a preference

A value such as independence is a starting point. Ask which activity expresses that value, what is preventing it, and which treatments or tradeoffs the person would accept. Remaining able to prepare a meal, visit family or sleep without morning grogginess gives the team a goal that can be revisited. Pair it with a preference: one patient may accept frequent visits to maintain mobility, while another places greater value on time at home. If preferences and goals appear to conflict, discuss the tradeoff rather than assuming that accepting the goal implies acceptance of every proposed intervention (p172–173).

Document the answer in language the next clinician can use. “Wants to do well” supplies little basis for choosing between options. A specific outcome and the burdens the person wishes to avoid can guide medication adjustment, diagnostic testing and the design of physical activity. The chapter describes priorities-aligned care in which patients were more likely to have unnecessary medicines stopped and had fewer diagnostic tests, referrals and procedures. At **6 months**, treatment burden scores in the priorities group had fallen **5 points** from baseline. This measures change in perceived workload; it is not a count of adverse events prevented or an assurance that less testing always improves care (p173).

A conversation can fail before goals are elicited if language, hearing, literacy or assumptions about authority prevent participation. Table 12-1 starts with preferred form of address and preferred language; ask whether an interpreter or trusted person would help. Pace the discussion, notice nonverbal cues and check that the patient feels safe asking questions. A history of trauma, distrust of institutions or concern about the side effects of a proposed treatment can alter willingness to disclose preferences. Seek permission before raising sensitive subjects and make confidentiality clear (p173–174).

Cultural background can shape how decisions are made and how illness is understood, but it does not tell the clinician what this particular patient wants. Ask about alternative therapies, the role of family or community and whom the person wants present. Someone may value joint decision-making, while another prefers a private discussion before bringing in relatives. Respect both approaches, and do not allow family involvement to erase the patient's own priorities. Distinguish culturally appropriate communication from an unrelated list of routine health checks: the book discusses hearing examinations as part of maintaining mobility, while the What Matters table addresses how the conversation is conducted (p174, p176).

Advance care planning is one application of What Matters rather than its full scope. Explore wishes about serious illness, acceptable treatment and the information or support needed by the person who may speak on the patient's behalf. The chapter describes a trial in which trained facilitators helped patients communicate and document preferences; wishes were more often known and respected than in the usual-care group. The mechanism is shared understanding and availability of the information when decisions arise. Completion of a form without discussion, or a conversation that cannot be found at transition, is a much weaker basis for claiming goal-concordant care (p173–174).

### A safe medicine is a medicine that still fits this person

Start with the actual regimen and the reason for each medicine. Check what the patient can afford, understands and can take as intended. A patient may have filled a prescription but not taken it, may be taking an old medicine in addition to a replacement, or may be limiting doses because of cost. An electronic list is useful for reconciliation but cannot describe whether the treatment interferes with the patient's desired activity. A sedating medicine may help one symptom and simultaneously reduce attention, stability and confidence in walking. Ask the patient and caregiver about both effects (p174–175).

The chapter's US estimate for dispensing of a Beers-list medicine reflects a specific year and list. It does not declare every included prescription inappropriate for every individual. STOPP can flag potentially inappropriate medication and START can flag an indicated treatment that appears to be missing. A computerized alert helps identify a review opportunity. It cannot determine whether a medicine should be stopped abruptly, tapered, substituted or continued. Resolve the indication, risks, alternatives, patient's preference and responsible clinician; communicate the resulting decision to people who might otherwise restart the old regimen (p174–175).

Why can deprescribing fail even when everyone recognizes the risk? Clinicians may lack time or information about harms, be uncertain about withdrawal, or fear that a patient will interpret stopping treatment as abandonment. A patient may assume a long-standing prescription will be necessary indefinitely. The book describes interventions that educate both patient and prescriber, including pharmacist involvement, so that the patient can ask about change and the prescriber can respond with a supervised plan. Ask which symptom the patient fears will recur and agree what to monitor afterward. Fewer medicines can be a result, but the aim is a regimen that supports the person's goals with an acceptable burden (p174–175).

### Mentation: distinguish the time course and the response required

The chapter groups delirium, dementia and depression under Mentation because all can affect the person's ability to function and engage in care. They require different responses. New fluctuating inattention during illness warrants prompt assessment for delirium and reversible precipitants; a longstanding cognitive decline calls for a baseline, a dementia evaluation and support for everyday needs. Persistent low mood or loss of interest merits assessment for depression even if a chronic medical illness also affects energy and sleep. A patient can have more than one of these problems. The framework is useful only if the finding leads to an appropriate evaluation and response (p175–176).

Table 12-2 describes person-centered care in dementia. Know the person's history, routines, preferences and abilities; attend to their experience of events and keep opportunities for meaningful engagement and relationships. Responsive care can change as dementia progresses. A team may learn that the patient becomes calmer with a familiar activity or that a specific approach makes dressing less distressing. Share the observation and ask whether the response is reproducible. Preserving participation can matter even when a cognitive score cannot improve. Include family as knowledgeable partners while continuing to address the person directly (p175).

Delirium often becomes visible through a change from baseline rather than a single poor score. Ask the family, nurse or caregiver what the person was like before admission and how attention or arousal changed over time. In a postoperative patient, orienting cues, appropriate sensory aids, mobilization, sleep support, nutrition and hydration can address several modifiable contributors at once. The chapter describes the tailored, family-involved Hospital Elder Life Program as an example. Family participation supplements clinical assessment; a familiar person may notice a change that staff miss, but the team remains responsible for evaluating illness and medicines contributing to delirium (p175–176).

A positive depression screen identifies a need to ask more, not an established diagnosis or reason for an automatic prescription. Distinguish persistent symptoms with functional effects from a transient reaction while taking grief and distress seriously. Establish whether the patient feels safe, what supports exist, and how mood affects appetite, sleep, activity and willingness to engage. A mobility plan that assumes motivation and confidence may be impossible until mood or fear of falling is addressed. The chapter gives an estimated depression prevalence range of **10%–30%**; the wide range reinforces the need to specify setting and case definition when interpreting a screening program (p176).

### Mobility as a daily outcome

Find out what movement matters to the person: standing from the bed, transferring to a chair, walking outdoors, reaching the toilet or keeping up with a chosen social activity. Describe baseline ability, aids and assistance, then determine what has changed. A requirement to move safely each day is different from merely recording whether the patient has fallen in the previous year. Mobility can be limited by a painful chronic disease, reduced balance or strength, visual problems, low mood, cognitive impairment, medication effects or hazards in the environment. Several may be present in the same patient (p175–176).

Exercise can support balance, gait and strength when it fits the person and setting. A group class may increase participation for someone who values social contact. A home program may be more realistic if transport is difficult and a caregiver can assist. Check vision, hearing, footwear and the medications that affect alertness or postural stability. The chapter uses a benzodiazepine as an example of a sedative associated with falls. Link that fact to the patient's actual walking difficulty and review the indication; it does not supply a universal drug discontinuation schedule. Arrange follow-up to find out whether the chosen change improved function (p176).

### Interpreting the Hartford and Providence examples

The Hartford program established a delirium pathway across disciplines with administrative support, repeated assessment and a registry. It added screening **three times a day** after the initial screen and coupled detection with interventions addressing mobility, nutrition, sleep and engagement. A positive screen triggered care; collecting a result alone would not explain a change in the hospital course. The implementation also depended on the work of nurses, other clinicians and volunteers, and on a process for communication across transitions. A hospital copying the screening schedule without the capacity to assess and manage positive findings would be implementing a different intervention (p177).

Table 12-3 compares hospitalizations with and without delirium in that institution. Mean length of stay was **12 versus 4 days**, and mean total cost was **$31,284 versus $8,900**. The text reports discharge home for **30% versus 70%**, and mortality of **10% versus less than 1%**, respectively. These are between-group observations; patients with delirium may differ in illness and baseline function. The report separately describes a reduction in length of stay among delirium patients from **16 to 10.6 days** after implementation and average annual savings of **$6.5 million** over the reported program period. Keep the comparisons separate: the between-group cost difference is not the savings attributable to each patient screened (p177).

Providence integrated falls screening and action, outpatient mobility, a dementia pathway, medication education and a guide to What Matters conversations. It then trained providers across **12 primary care clinics**. Screening for fall risk and cognitive impairment doubled and fall-risk interventions quadrupled. The chapter also reports fewer high-risk prescriptions and a **2%–7% reduction in hospitalizations** at participating clinics. Interpret these measures according to their denominators: the number offered a screen, interventions performed and admissions are different things. Doubling detection can initially increase the number of patients documented as at risk even if underlying risk has not changed (p177).

### Measuring whether the plan reaches the patient

An annual wellness encounter can provide a defined opportunity to ask What Matters, review medication, assess mentation and agree on safe mobility. The visit creates value only if the information is used later. When the person changes setting, communicate their stated objective, the medicine changed and why, the baseline cognitive and mobility status, and who will review the outcome. A receiving clinician can then tell whether a change represents deterioration, recovery or an intended adjustment. The chapter's local utilization and financial results illustrate what systems measured, rather than predicting savings for every practice (p172–177).

To assess a program, combine a process with an outcome that reflects the original objective. If a clinic wants fewer medication-related falls, measure completed reviews and actions as well as subsequent falls and the patient's ability to move. If a hospital wants earlier recognition of delirium, count screening and response to new findings, then look at recovery, discharge destination and burden. If the objective is care aligned with priorities, ask the patient or caregiver whether the resulting plan actually supported the activity that mattered. A reduction in tests is favorable when the tests were unlikely to contribute to that goal; it is not a reason to omit necessary evaluation (p173–177).

## Exam traps

- What Matters includes everyday health outcomes as well as future and end-of-life preferences (p172–173).
- An electronic alert identifies a medicine for review. Its presence alone cannot decide what this patient should take (p174–175).
- A higher screening rate measures a change in care delivery. It does not count falls prevented (p177).
