# Chapter 12 Study: Age-Friendly Care

## Summary

**Use the framework as a set**
- Age-friendly care integrates **4Ms**: What Matters, Medication, Mentation and Mobility across care settings (p172).
- Apply them together to shift attention toward function, wellness and the person's goals; isolated disease plans can conflict (p172).
- High-functioning teams need shared purpose, clear roles, communication, mutual respect and recognition of each member's expertise (p171-172).
- Include older adults, families and caregivers as partners; the approach aims to improve experience, population health and cost of care (p172).

**Translate each element into care**
- What Matters: elicit and document specific health-outcome goals and care preferences, including end-of-life care, and use them consistently across providers (p172-173).
- For “What Matters” conversations, ask about preferred language and form of address, literacy, cultural beliefs and the desired role of family (Table 12-1, p174). The book lists hearing examinations separately among measures to maintain mobility (p176).
- Medication: prescribe when needed, review affordability and understanding, and avoid interference with mentation, mobility or patient priorities (p174-175).
- Physicians report limited time, poor awareness of medication harms, and concern about withdrawal or patient criticism as barriers to deprescribing. Review medicines with the team and involve the patient and caregiver (p174).
- Computerized support and STOPP/START can identify prescribing problems, but assessment and communication remain team responsibilities (p174-175).
- Mentation: prevent, identify and manage delirium, dementia and depression. Dementia is not normal aging (p175).
- Person-centered dementia care recognizes the individual, accepts the person's reality, supports meaningful engagement and relationships, and reassesses care (p175).
- Mobility: enable safe daily movement, strength and balance; address chronic-disease effects and use tailored physical-activity support (p176).

**Measure what changes**
- Integrate the framework into routine workflows and communicate across settings; use patient goals and functional outcomes to guide improvement (p172-177).
- The book's implementation examples report reduced utilization and cost in some systems; these are local results, not guaranteed effects everywhere (p176-177).
- Annual wellness visits can provide an opportunity to review the framework and engage families in a coordinated plan (p177).

### From a stated priority to a care plan

Ask which activity the person wants to keep doing and which parts of treatment are burdensome. Record the answer so the next clinician can act on it. In the priorities-based care example, patients with explicitly identified goals had fewer tests, referrals and procedures and were more likely to have unnecessary medicines stopped. The change in treatment burden was measured from the patient's perspective (p172–173).

An estimated **29%** of Medicare beneficiaries in **2015** filled a prescription for at least one drug on the contemporaneous Beers list of medicines to avoid. That figure describes dispensing. A review still needs the indication, expected benefit, harms and the person's goals. In a deprescribing intervention, pharmacists educated both the patient and prescriber, giving the patient a role in the decision (p174–175).

A depression screen can start a discussion about grief or persistent low mood. Arrange follow-up for assessment and treatment when indicated. When evaluating a local program, distinguish increased screening from changes in health. The Providence example reported a doubling of fall-risk and cognitive screening and a quadrupling of fall-risk interventions after staff training (p176–177).

## Exam traps

- What Matters includes everyday health outcomes as well as future and end-of-life preferences (p172–173).
- An electronic alert identifies a medicine for review. Its presence alone cannot decide what this patient should take (p174–175).
- A higher screening rate measures a change in care delivery. It does not count falls prevented (p177).

## Recall drill

**1. What are the four elements of age-friendly care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>What Matters, Medication, Mentation and Mobility. Apply them together across settings. (p172–173)</details>

**2. What question can turn a general preference for independence into a treatment goal?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask which activity the person most wants to keep doing and what aspects of care make that harder. Record the specific goal and align decisions with it. (p172–173)</details>

**3. What changed in the priorities-based care example?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Patients were more likely to stop unnecessary medicines and underwent fewer tests, referrals and procedures than those receiving usual care. (p173)</details>

**4. What communication preferences should be clarified before eliciting goals?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Preferred language, form of address, literacy needs, cultural beliefs and how the person wants family involved. (p174)</details>

**5. Why might a correctly indicated medicine still conflict with the care plan?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Its effects on alertness, safe movement or treatment burden may interfere with the patient's goals. Also check access, understanding and ability to take it safely. (p173–175)</details>

**6. What did the 2015 Medicare figure of 29% measure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The estimated proportion who filled at least one prescription on the 2015 Beers list of drugs to avoid in older adults. (p174)</details>

**7. What practical barriers can keep clinicians from deprescribing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Limited time or awareness of harms, concern about withdrawal and worry about the patient's reaction can all delay a review. (p174)</details>

**8. How can a pharmacist involve the patient in deprescribing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Discuss risks and alternatives with the patient and communicate the same information to the prescriber so they can reach a shared decision. (p175)</details>

**9. What problems does the Mentation element address?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Delirium, dementia and depression. Assessment and follow-up depend on which problem is suspected. (p173, p175–176)</details>

**10. How does person-centered dementia care change a difficult interaction?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Recognize the person's own experience and relationships, find a meaningful way to engage, and reassess the response. (p175)</details>

**11. Name two depression screening instruments discussed in the chapter.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The Patient Health Questionnaire and Geriatric Depression Scale. A concerning result calls for follow-up assessment and a care plan. (p176)</details>

**12. Why include vision or hearing care when the person's priority is mobility?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter includes sensory assessment among ways to support safe movement and independence alongside activity, strength and balance work. (p176)</details>

**13. What distinguishes a functioning team from a collection of referrals?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Shared goals, clear roles, communication and mutual respect let different disciplines contribute to the same plan with the patient and caregiver. (p171–172)</details>

**14. What did staff training change in the Providence example?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The clinics reported more screening for falls and cognitive impairment and more fall-risk interventions. These were implementation outcomes. (p177)</details>

**15. Which routine primary care encounter can bring the elements together?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An annual wellness visit can bring patient goals, medication review, mentation and mobility into a coordinated discussion with the patient and family. (p177)</details>
