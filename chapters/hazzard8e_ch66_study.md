# Chapter 66 Study: General Topics in Geriatric Psychiatry

## Summary

**Assessment & psychosis**
- Assess medical illness, medications, cognition, mood, substance use and social context; brief cognitive screens differ in scope and do not replace evaluation (p1024–1026).
- New psychosis can accompany delirium, mood or neurocognitive disorders, as well as a primary psychotic illness (p1026).
- Antipsychotics require individualized risk–benefit discussion and monitoring; older adults are vulnerable to adverse effects, and dementia-related use has mortality concerns (p1027–1028).

**Mood & suicide**
- Depression is common and associated with suicide risk; late-life mood presentations require medical and psychiatric assessment (pp1021, 1028–1031).
- Ask directly about suicide, intent, means and protective factors while establishing rapport; older patients may be reluctant to disclose thoughts (p1029–1030).
- High acute suicide risk requires emergent mental-health evaluation. Reduce access to lethal means, treat the underlying disorder and involve support people (p1030–1031).

**Anxiety & sleep**
- Distinguish primary anxiety from medical illness, medication effects, intoxication or withdrawal; symptom timing matters (p1032).
- Psychotherapy and SSRIs/SNRIs are central treatments. Geriatric starting doses are generally half those for younger adults (p1034).
- The book describes maximal SSRI antianxiety effect after **6–12 months** at a therapeutic dose; buspirone may take **2–4 weeks** (p1034).
- Benzodiazepines warrant great caution: cognitive effects, dependence and falls. TCAs add anticholinergic/cardiac risks (p1034).
- Sleep complaints are often multifactorial; review pain, nocturia, breathing, drugs and substances before attributing them to aging (p1034–1035).
- CBT for insomnia has better long-term outcomes than medication; CPAP treats sleep-disordered breathing (p1035–1036).

**Substances, personality & adaptation**
- Alcohol and benzodiazepine misuse can worsen cognition, falls and mental illness; withdrawal may be hazardous (p1037–1038).
- Distinguish longstanding personality patterns from active illness; treat comorbid disorders and target functional problems (p1039–1040).
- Successful aging can coexist with illness: support adaptation, resilience, physical/cognitive activity and social connection (p1040–1041).

### Persistent worry in a resident with mild dementia

Generalized anxiety often presents as worries about health, family or finances that are difficult to control. Restlessness, poor concentration and sleep disruption can accompany the worry. Establish the time course and baseline with both patient and caregivers. Ask whether pain, cardiopulmonary disease, an activating medication or withdrawal explains a new change before diagnosing a primary anxiety disorder (p1031–1033).

The 2020 question describes continued distress after group treatment. Citalopram represents the SSRI choice among the answers; the chapter supports SSRIs or SNRIs and psychotherapy for persistent anxiety. Oxazepam can impair cognition and balance or produce dependence. Quetiapine adds antipsychotic risks, and donepezil does not target this persistent-worry syndrome. Review potentially aggravating medication before adding another. The book's interval for maximal SSRI effect does not describe the time to first improvement (p1027–1028, p1033–1034).

> Current practice (not the book): Citalopram has dose-dependent QT effects. US prescribing information limits the dose to 20 mg once daily after age 60 and calls for attention to QT-prolonging co-medications, potassium and magnesium. - [Celexa prescribing information, 2023](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4259d9b1-de34-43a4-85a8-41dd214e9177).

### Sorting new psychosis by its time course

Ask for an informant's account of prior function and the onset of changes. An abrupt, fluctuating illness demands evaluation for delirium. Psychosis emerging during a mood episode differs from a longstanding psychotic disorder. Delusional disorder can consist of persistent delusions without the disorganized speech, behavior or prominent hallucinations associated with schizophrenia. Consider medications, sensory limitations and medical illness throughout the evaluation (p1025–1027).

### Who appears in the epidemiologic figures

Figure 66-1 describes a demographic projection for the United States, not the prevalence of mental illness. Its bars show **49.2 million** people aged 65 or older in 2016, **15%** of the population, and project **94.7 million**, **23%**, for 2060. The caption separately quotes a projection of 92 million. Both communicate an expanding need for care; the values are projections from the cited material. Figure 66-2 describes living arrangements in 2012. Living with a partner was reported for **72% of men and 49% of women**, while **21% of men and 34% of women** lived alone. A household category does not determine whether an individual feels lonely or can obtain help (p1021–1023).

Figure 66-3 uses yet another denominator: the proportion of total disability-adjusted life years at each age attributable to specified groups of disorders. Mental and substance-use disorders account for a larger fraction at younger adult ages; the share due to Alzheimer disease and other dementias rises steeply later in life. Read the age band and legend before interpreting the graph. A smaller proportion attributable to one condition when other diseases become common does not make that condition clinically insignificant in an older patient (p1022–1023).

### Loss, adaptation and ageism

Erikson's late-life task of integrity versus despair concerns how a person understands the course of their life. Retirement, bereavement, disability and changing roles can prompt reassessment, while meaningful relationships and valued activity can sustain well-being. Aging alone does not establish depression, anxiety, paranoia or inability to benefit from treatment. Ask what a loss meant to this person and how they have coped with earlier changes. A patient may report considerable physical disability and still describe a satisfying day. Another may remain functionally independent while experiencing severe distress (p1023–1025, p1040–1041).

Distinguish social isolation, which can be described by contacts and activities, from loneliness, the person's experience of inadequate connection. Living with family does not rule out loneliness. A person living alone may have close and reliable contacts. The chapter links loneliness with mood symptoms, substance use, poorer nutrition and inactivity, and describes longitudinal associations with cognitive decline. During the COVID-19 pandemic, separation from family, disruption of routines and barriers to services compounded existing vulnerabilities. Asking about a specific lost activity or missed appointment is more informative than assuming everyone in the same living arrangement experienced the same burden (p1024–1025).

### Choosing an assessment and recognizing delirium

Begin with the change being evaluated, its time course and an informant's account of usual function. A brief cognitive screen can identify people who need further assessment. The Mini-Cog, Memory Impairment Screen and GPCOG can be used in that role; broader assessments such as MMSE, MoCA and SLUMS sample multiple domains. A score can change because of attention, language, education, hearing, mood or acute illness. Choose a test the patient can actually perform, record relevant limitations and ask how the finding relates to managing medicines, finances and other activities (p1025–1026).

Choose symptom-specific tools when the history suggests them: PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, and measures targeted to mania or trauma-related symptoms when appropriate. Formal neuropsychological testing can characterize a pattern that a short screen cannot resolve. Imaging and other tests address particular differential diagnoses. The immediate question in a newly confused or psychotic older adult is whether attention and awareness have changed acutely and fluctuate. A single clear period or a pre-existing dementia diagnosis should not end the investigation of delirium. Review medical illness and substances, including recent prescriptions, dose changes and withdrawal (p1025–1027).

### Psychotic syndromes and diagnostic error

Distinguish a longstanding schizophrenia-spectrum disorder from a new psychotic syndrome in late life. The book estimates that only about **20%** of people with schizophrenia have onset after age 40. A late presentation is possible, but a first episode in an older person calls for a careful medical, medication and cognitive assessment. Delusional disorder can present with persistent delusions while organized speech and functioning outside the delusional concern are relatively preserved. A history of prior episodes, accompanying mood changes and the evolution of function help distinguish these presentations from psychotic features of a mood or neurocognitive disorder (p1026–1027).

Psychosis can occur in dementia. The chapter estimates that **41%** of people with Alzheimer disease experience psychotic symptoms at some point. An informant who knows the person's usual behavior is particularly valuable: ask exactly what the person said or appeared to see, when it started, whether it fluctuates and whether it causes fear or risk. Look for a treatable precipitant before attributing the change to progression. The authors report that unsuspected medical conditions may contribute to as many as **34%** of admissions for behavioral symptoms in older people in cited studies. The estimate supports looking; it does not diagnose the cause of a particular episode (p1026–1027).

### When an antipsychotic is considered

Establish a target symptom and the harm or distress it causes. Adjust the environment and caregiving approach where possible, and assess both the risk of treatment and the risk of leaving severe symptoms untreated. Age-related changes in distribution, clearance and dopaminergic sensitivity can make older patients more vulnerable to extrapyramidal symptoms. Sedation, orthostatic effects and parkinsonism may together impair walking. A lower extrapyramidal burden with some second-generation agents does not eliminate the risk of falls, metabolic change, stroke or excess mortality in dementia-related psychosis (p1027–1028).

Aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine and risperidone were compared in a study of **332** outpatients over age 40 with psychotic symptoms and varied diagnoses. Metabolic syndrome developed in **36% over one year**, and more than half stopped the assigned agent within six months. Reassess a short-term response before continuing a prescription indefinitely. The chapter's general starting principle is about **25%–50%** of the younger adult starting dose followed by slow titration according to tolerability. It names other atypicals, including ziprasidone, paliperidone, iloperidone, asenapine and lurasidone, without a universal indication-specific dose for each. Document the rationale, monitor adverse effects and define when the continuing need will be reviewed (p1027–1028).

> Current practice (not the book): Brexpiprazole has a US indication for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer disease. That indication is specific to agitation and the antipsychotic boxed warning about increased mortality in older patients with dementia-related psychosis remains. - [FDA, 2023](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-drug-treat-agitation-symptoms-associated-dementia-due-alzheimers-disease).

### Mood symptoms and immediate safety

Assess depression in the context of losses, medical illness, function and medications. Ask about depressed mood, loss of interest, hopelessness and what the patient expects of the future. Ask directly about thoughts of suicide without treating endorsement or denial as the whole assessment. Clarify frequency, intent, immediacy, previous attempts, substance use, support and reasons for living. A patient may first disclose distress to a primary care clinician or describe sleep, pain or somatic concerns before discussing mood. Listen without rushing to reassure or to equate grief with a depressive disorder (p1028–1031).

Table 66-1 presents the PROSPECT approach. Use a calm, direct, collaborative conversation; elicit both the precipitating problem and protective relationships. If acute risk is high, arrange emergency mental-health assessment, ensure supervision and address access to lethal means. For ongoing management the table describes reassessing hopelessness, suicidal thoughts and substance use at each treatment contact, **at least weekly contact** while ideation persists, and limiting prescription quantities to **one week** until risk decreases. These outpatient measures require a suitable level of safety and follow-up. A promise or signed agreement does not establish that a patient in immediate danger can safely leave (p1030–1031).

### Anxiety syndromes: worry, avoidance, panic and trauma

Ask about both distress and the behavior that prevents distress. A person who never leaves home may report few panic episodes while having lost nearly all community activity. The chapter gives an estimated **5%–15%** prevalence of full-criteria anxiety disorders in later life. Roughly half of late-life anxiety diagnoses are generalized anxiety disorder and about **40%** specific phobia. Fear of falling can occur with or without a previous fall. Ask whether a reasonable precaution has become restriction out of proportion to the current risk and whether loss of activity has further reduced confidence (p1031–1033).

In generalized anxiety, persistent difficult-to-control worry spans different issues and can disturb sleep and concentration. A specific phobia centers on a particular stimulus or situation. Panic attacks are discrete episodes; a first episode with chest or respiratory symptoms in an older adult still requires evaluation for medical causes. Agoraphobic avoidance reflects fear of situations from which escape or help would be difficult. Obsessions and repetitive compulsions, or intrusive trauma memories and avoidance, point to different syndromes. The patient may have had these problems for decades without treatment, or a new illness, medication or loss may have changed their expression (p1032–1034).

Psychotherapy, especially cognitive-behavioral and exposure approaches when suitable, and SSRIs or SNRIs are central options for persistent syndromes. The chapter gives about **half the younger adult starting dose** as the initial geriatric principle. Its **6–12 months** refers to maximal SSRI antianxiety effect at a therapeutic dose; it does not justify postponing early review for response, falls, hyponatremia, bleeding or interaction with other drugs. Buspirone is discussed for generalized anxiety with a gradual response over **2–4 weeks**. Gabapentin and pregabalin are discussed as possible alternatives in selected patients. TCAs can add anticholinergic effects or problematic cardiovascular effects. Benzodiazepines can impair cognition and balance and can lead to dependence. These distinctions explain the SSRI answer in the earlier chapter-level exam vignette above (p1033–1034).

### Sorting the sleep complaint before prescribing

Ask about bedtime, actual sleep onset, awakenings, final waking, naps, daylight exposure, activity, substances and the sleeping environment. A bed partner can report breathing pauses or movements unknown to the patient. Pain, nocturia, depression, medication timing and institutional routines can all fragment sleep. Normal aging can advance the timing of the sleep period and reduce its continuity. It does not make every distressed sleeper a candidate for a sedative. A diary helps distinguish inability to get to sleep from going to bed early and obtaining most sleep before the desired waking time (p1034–1036).

Snoring, witnessed pauses and daytime sleepiness suggest sleep-disordered breathing and can warrant formal sleep assessment. In restless legs syndrome, an urge or discomfort makes rest difficult, particularly in the evening, and movement offers relief. Periodic limb movements occur during sleep and can be observed or measured even when the patient reports a different complaint. REM sleep behavior disorder instead involves acting out dreams, with risk to the patient or partner; consider the association with neurodegenerative disease. Address bedroom safety and ask about medications and other causes. Circadian rhythm disturbance requires attention to timing, light and routine rather than simply increasing the time spent in bed (p1035–1036).

The book describes ropinirole and pramipexole for restless legs and limb movements, and clonazepam for REM sleep behavior disorder with particular concern about daytime sedation in older adults. For insomnia it discusses zolpidem, zaleplon and eszopiclone; short-term efficacy does not remove risks to gait, alertness and cognition. Ramelteon acts at melatonin receptors. Start with changes directed at the history and behavioral approaches to insomnia, then reassess the specific complaint. The chapter provides no standard milligram schedule for these drugs that can be applied across all of these sleep diagnoses (p1035–1036).

> Current practice (not the book): For adult restless legs syndrome, AASM advises against routine long-term use of pramipexole or ropinirole because augmentation can make symptoms start earlier or become more intense. Its recommendations favor gabapentin-class options for appropriate patients and include assessment of iron status. - [American Academy of Sleep Medicine guideline, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/).

### Alcohol, sedatives and other substances

Ask about actual use, timing, reasons for use and the last dose. Include prescribed sedatives and opioids, alcohol, cannabis, nicotine and other substances. Changes in drinking may follow retirement, pain, isolation or bereavement. Collateral information can be important when recall is poor or the patient reports fewer drinks than caregivers observe. Falls, sleep disturbance, altered mental status and missed doses of other medicines can be consequences or clues. The immediate task after interruption of regular heavy alcohol or benzodiazepine use may be recognizing withdrawal and arranging appropriate monitoring (p1036–1038).

For alcohol use disorder, the chapter discusses psychosocial treatment and names naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. Naltrexone reduces the rewarding effects of alcohol and requires attention to concurrent opioid use; acamprosate is directed at reducing return to drinking, with limited geriatric evidence discussed here. Disulfiram produces an aversive reaction and may interact with warfarin, phenytoin, isoniazid or diazepam; blood pressure effects and adverse reactions limit its usefulness in older people. Hazzard provides the actual alcohol-treatment drug regimens in Chapter 23, Table 23-4, rather than in Chapter 66. Management of withdrawal serves a different purpose from relapse prevention. The chapter discusses lorazepam, oxazepam or temazepam in older patients where a benzodiazepine is indicated and emphasizes monitoring for oversedation and respiratory effects (p1037).

A long-standing prescription for insomnia can become dependence even if it began as prescribed. Review tolerance, current benefit and coadministration with alcohol, opioids or other sedatives. When reducing a benzodiazepine, withdrawal risk makes a supervised taper and follow-up important. For opioid use disorder the book discusses methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone, with limited studies confined specifically to older adults. Distinguish treatment of the disorder from management of pain and acute intoxication. Ask about cannabis in nonjudgmental terms, including the reason it is used and possible cognitive or functional effects. For tobacco, behavioral support and nicotine replacement can be considered along with bupropion or varenicline; monitor anxiety, mood and tolerability in the individual treated (p1037–1038).

### Longstanding personality patterns and successful aging

Establish that the pattern is longstanding and evident across settings. A new change in judgment, disinhibition or suspiciousness in late life needs assessment for medical, cognitive or mood disorders. Cluster A includes paranoid, schizoid and schizotypal patterns; cluster B includes antisocial, borderline, histrionic and narcissistic patterns; cluster C includes avoidant, dependent and obsessive-compulsive personality patterns. A trait that allowed someone to manage independently can become more disruptive after loss of mobility or the need for personal care. Distinguish the person’s longstanding coping style from the understandable distress of losing control (p1039–1040).

With marked interpersonal instability, consistent responses across caregivers, clear boundaries and agreed goals can reduce contradictory demands on staff and improve continuity. Take distress and self-harm concerns seriously. Psychotherapy and a collaborative care plan address the pattern; if a drug is used, identify the specific target symptom and comorbid disorder. Chronic use of multiple sedating drugs to manage interpersonal conflict can worsen function without addressing the precipitating situation. A treatment agreement can clarify expectations for patient and team. It is separate from assessing immediate safety (p1039–1040).

The chapter closes with a distinction relevant to every preceding section. The original Rowe–Kahn model combined absence of disease or disability, preserved physical and cognitive function and continued engagement. Many older people with illness nevertheless describe themselves as aging successfully. Ask about adaptation, relationships, learning, meaning, resilience and what the patient hopes to do next. These outcomes can improve during treatment even when a diagnosis persists. They also provide a reason to pursue treatment for distressing psychiatric symptoms regardless of chronological age (p1040–1041).

## Exam traps

- Distinguish living alone from perceived loneliness, and past projections from measured disease prevalence (p1022–1025).
- A cognitive screening score obtained during an acute change in attention is not a baseline dementia diagnosis (p1025–1026).
- Reduced risk of parkinsonism with an atypical antipsychotic does not remove its metabolic, cerebrovascular, fall or mortality risks (p1027–1028).
- A treatment agreement does not answer the question of immediate suicide risk or replace an emergency assessment (p1030–1031).
- Distinguish an urge to move at rest from movements during sleep and from dream enactment (p1035–1036).
- A medicine appropriate for alcohol withdrawal can worsen chronic insomnia, falls and dependence when continued without a separate indication (p1036–1038).
- A lifelong pattern of coping and a first episode of disinhibition in later life require different diagnostic histories (p1039–1040).


- Anxiety in an older person may be secondary to medication, illness, intoxication or withdrawal (p1032).
- The maximal effect of a scheduled anxiolytic is distinct from rapid relief of distress (p1034).
- Wandering accompanying worry does not by itself establish a need for an antipsychotic (p1027–1028, p1034).

## Recall drill

**1. What should be established before labeling new psychosis as primary?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The baseline, time course, medical and drug exposures, mood state and cognitive trajectory, using collateral history when available (p1025–1027).</details>

**2. How does delusional disorder differ from schizophrenia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Persistent delusions predominate without the broader pattern of disorganization and other characteristic symptoms of schizophrenia (p1026).</details>

**3. Why is an antipsychotic decision particularly consequential in dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Older adults are vulnerable to adverse effects, and treatment of dementia-related psychosis carries excess mortality risk. Weigh indication and alternatives (p1027–1028).</details>

**4. What should an interview cover when suicide risk is suspected?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask directly about thoughts, intent, plans, access to means, prior behavior and available protective supports (p1029–1031).</details>

**5. What response is appropriate to high acute suicide risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Arrange emergent mental-health evaluation, protect the patient and engage appropriate supports (p1030–1031).</details>

**6. What symptom distinguishes generalized anxiety from ordinary transient concern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Persistent excessive worry that is hard to control and interferes with everyday life (p1032–1033).</details>

**7. Why investigate physical causes of newly appearing panic symptoms in late life?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cardiopulmonary or neurologic disease and medication or substance effects can mimic a panic episode (p1032–1033).</details>

**8. Which class best fits persistent generalized anxiety in the older exam resident after limited psychotherapy benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An SSRI, represented by citalopram in the choices. The chapter also discusses SNRIs and continuing appropriate psychotherapy (p1033–1034).</details>

**9. What medication review should precede a new anxiolytic?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Look for unnecessary drugs or adverse effects that can provoke anxiety or confusion. The chapter gives anticholinergic bladder treatment as an example (p1034).</details>

**10. How does the chapter approach initial anxiolytic dosing in older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Generally start at about half the usual younger adult starting dose. Assess benefit, tolerance and the individual agent's limits (p1034).</details>

**11. Is buspirone an immediate rescue medicine in this discussion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. The book describes <strong>2–4 weeks</strong> to maximal efficacy in generalized anxiety (p1034).</details>

**12. Why be cautious with benzodiazepines in late-life anxiety?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>They can impair cognition and driving, increase falls and cause physical dependence (p1034).</details>

**13. Which approaches have a durable role in insomnia management?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Investigate perpetuating conditions and use cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia. Consider sleep-disordered breathing and other primary sleep disorders (p1034–1036).</details>

**14. How can a longstanding personality pattern be distinguished from a newly active illness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Establish lifelong interpersonal patterns and assess recent changes, comorbid mood or anxiety illness, delirium and cognitive change (p1039–1040).</details>

**15. What can successful aging mean in someone with chronic psychiatric illness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Adaptation, resilience, meaningful social participation and functional gains may coexist with an ongoing diagnosis (p1040–1041).</details>
