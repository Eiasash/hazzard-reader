## Recognize the syndrome before treating the behavior

Depression, anxiety, apathy, hallucinations and disinhibition can emerge before or after cognitive decline becomes obvious. The chapter links these symptoms to vulnerability in brain circuits controlling behavior, compounded by illness, pain, sensory deficits and the demands of the surroundings. Their presence is associated with faster decline, greater caregiver burden and a greater likelihood of institutional placement. Improvement in one target symptom does not establish improvement in cognition or independence. Hazzard pp. 919–920, 927–928. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-482e5db0580ad7a0c6dcd21c" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Get the time course from someone who knows the person.** Years of decline with wandering or social disinhibition can fit dementia. Marked new sleepiness over two days or rapidly changing agitation warrants an assessment for delirium, including attention, arousal, medication changes, pain and acute illness. Profound sadness and suicidal thoughts require separate urgent assessment. Use examination and focused tests to investigate plausible causes. The NPI-Q can describe symptoms and caregiver distress but cannot identify their cause. Hazzard pp. 920–923. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-9fd00abfaa6396324e34c1d1" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Ask what the hallucination means to this patient.** Formed visions of people or animals, cognitive fluctuations, parkinsonism and dream enactment favor dementia with Lewy bodies. A calm person seeing something others cannot see may need reassurance and observation. Distressing or dangerous symptoms need further evaluation. Review agents such as amantadine that can worsen hallucinations. Donepezil or another cholinesterase inhibitor may help cognition and hallucinations. Dopamine-blocking antipsychotics can cause profound rigidity or a severe sensitivity reaction. Hazzard pp. 923–924, 928 and 984. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-b6e0367710cf1836980a73db" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

## DICE in a care interaction

During a difficult bath, ask the caregiver to describe the moment that went wrong, what preceded it, what the person did and how each responded. **Investigate** means looking for a cause that can change: painful movement, constipation, cold, fear, loss of privacy, unfamiliar instructions or a medication effect. **Create** a plan with the person, caregiver and team: try a familiar time, offer a choice, give one instruction at a time and pause when fear rises. **Evaluate** asks both whether the plan was tried and whether distress decreased. If the plan was impractical, find out why. Hazzard p. 924, Table 60-4. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-18490c5933b53a866c445587" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

The June 2025 bathing vignette cites the International Psychogeriatric Association article. Its cycle is Investigate, Plan and Act. It discusses DICE as a compatible method. Someone who can still be reassured during dressing or bathing gives the team a reason to investigate that interaction before adding a sedative. See Figure 1 and the Investigation section of the IPA consensus article, and Hazzard pp. 924–926 for related teaching. <span data-practice-ids="mcq-9fd9a6436651576bca84dab2" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Support the caregiver as part of the intervention.** In-home occupational assessment shows which tasks the person can do with cues and which require supervision. Identify who can relieve a caregiver who is losing sleep. A respite service provides substitute care for a defined period. A safety plan for someone who wanders must work even when the person cannot ask for help. Hazzard pp. 924–926, Tables 60-5 and 60-6.

## Decide whether an antipsychotic trial is justified

Clarify the target: a distressing delusion, repeated physical injuries or an immediate threat changes the decision more than a nonspecific report of “agitation.” Treat delirium and other reversible causes and adapt the setting. NICE permits an antipsychotic when there is risk of harm or severe distress from agitation, hallucinations or delusions. Family distress also calls for support, but burden alone is insufficient. Explain the likely size of the benefit and the risks to the patient and caregiver. Hazzard pp. 925 and 927. NICE NG97, recommendations 1.7.1–1.7.8. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-c6a327d1e984cf14664a4c22 mcq-2da4c054f7061a52e843c385" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Define success and when to stop.** For example, fewer episodes of hitting during necessary care with preserved wakefulness is a meaningful response. Start one medication at a low dose and titrate toward the minimum effective dose. Watch gait, alertness, orthostatic symptoms, stiffness, cognition and function. After four weeks at an adequate dose without significant benefit, taper and withdraw. Assess at least monthly during tapering and for at least four months afterward. For ongoing treatment, NICE calls for reassessment at least every six weeks. Continue behavioral care throughout. Hazzard pp. 926–928, Table 60-7. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-eb1d5bba9a6717079d54d5d1" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Recognize drug-specific harms.** In dementia, antipsychotic exposure increases mortality, including cardiovascular deaths. Haloperidol has substantial extrapyramidal risk, can lower the seizure threshold and can cause orthostatic hypotension. Olanzapine can increase appetite, weight and glucose, with antimuscarinic effects at higher doses. Quetiapine can cause sedation, orthostasis and antimuscarinic effects at higher doses. The June 2022 anticholinergic item gave credit for every choice. It supplies no reliable potency ranking. Hazzard pp. 927–928, Table 60-8. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-4ea0e38022bf9d5645f8e014" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**US approvals after publication.** Brexpiprazole was approved in 2023 for agitation associated with dementia due to Alzheimer disease and carries the antipsychotic boxed mortality warning. In 2026 the FDA approved extended-release dextromethorphan with bupropion for the same indication. Both are scheduled treatments. These indications do not generalize to isolated wandering or all types of dementia. [FDA brexpiprazole approval](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-drug-treat-agitation-symptoms-associated-dementia-due-alzheimers-disease) · [FDA Auvelity label, sections 1.2 and 2.3](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/215430s009s018lbl.pdf)

## Separate major depression, apathy and medication toxicity

Crying, withdrawal, irritability and new anxiety can represent depression, but apathy and cognitive decline can produce overlapping symptoms. Check duration, functional effect, enjoyment, sleep, appetite and suicidal thoughts with the patient and informant. A screening score signals the need for assessment. An SSRI such as escitalopram can target a depressive disorder when medication is warranted. Donepezil targets symptomatic Alzheimer disease. The June 2023 item accepted both choices. Antidepressant benefit for depression specifically in dementia is inconsistent, and NICE favors psychological approaches for mild to moderate depression in mild to moderate dementia. Hazzard pp. 923, 928–931. NICE NG97, recommendations 1.7.11–1.7.12. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-45b29368078d5d087a1a0b91" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

For an older adult with major depression, mild anxiety and ischemic heart disease, sertraline is a reasonable first drug. It has little anticholinergic activity. Bupropion may be activating, and comorbid kidney disease calls for dose caution. Amitriptyline adds orthostasis and anticholinergic and cardiac effects. An antipsychotic is a poor initial choice for uncomplicated major depression. Hazzard pp. 929–930. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-3c902dfe8cf32cf96518b8b1" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Think in mechanisms.** Confusion with urinary retention suggests muscarinic blockade: paroxetine stands out among commonly used SSRIs, while escitalopram has relatively little anticholinergic activity. Amitriptyline and promethazine also add substantial burden. New confusion alone might reflect hyponatremia. Agitation accompanied by clonus, hyperreflexia and autonomic disturbance instead suggests serotonin toxicity, which can occur with citalopram especially alongside another serotonergic drug. Hazzard pp. 922, 929–930, Tables 60-3 and 60-9. AGS Beers Criteria 2023, Table 7. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-57825e4b2e4819d0b41d9393 mcq-4641bad3c4f4169ea022ed51 mcq-aa5c10df9c19aad52cd3cfa8" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

SSRIs and SNRIs can lower serum sodium through inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. Consider the drug and other contributors if new confusion or gait instability follows initiation. Both mirtazapine and bupropion were accepted as lower-risk options in the September item. Neither guarantees normal sodium. Mirtazapine can increase appetite and daytime sleepiness, even at a low dose. Hazzard pp. 929–930, Table 60-9. <span data-exam-ids="mcq-63bf3798074f1dec36283b45" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Read the ECG from the beginning of QRS to the end of T.** Citalopram prolongs ventricular repolarization in a dose-dependent manner. The label limits dosing to 20 mg daily above age 60. Hypokalemia and other QT-prolonging drugs increase concern. In CitAD, a target of 30 mg daily improved selected agitation outcomes but caused cognitive and cardiac adverse effects. The trial found no significant improvement in daily functioning or rescue lorazepam use. The subsequent S-CitAD trial did not find significant improvement in agitation at 12 weeks with escitalopram and found QT prolongation. Hazzard pp. 929–930. [CitAD trial, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4086818/) · [S-CitAD trial, primary outcome and safety](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12951298/) <span data-exam-ids="mcq-6655a23a9c4d6fc4c36f3cf9" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Lithium and a new thiazide.** Sodium loss can reduce renal lithium clearance. New tremor, falls, altered consciousness or clonus warrant prompt evaluation of lithium level, electrolytes and kidney function. A routine urine toxicology screen does not measure lithium. For agitation in Alzheimer disease, the completed Lit-AD trial found no significant advantage over placebo on its primary agitation and aggression measure. Hazzard p. 931. [Lithium prescribing information, sections 5.1 and 7.1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=7dc9c6d2-6d9a-49e4-a8ab-437b0ed5f84e&type=display) · [Lit-AD results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34059401/)

## Summary

**Epidemiology & framework**
- Neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) occur in up to **98%** of dementia patients over the course of illness (p919); depression, apathy, and anxiety are the most common NPS in dementia (p919).
- New late-life NPS lasting **over 6 months**, without dementia and not explained by another psychiatric disorder = mild behavioral impairment (MBI) (p919).
- NPS are associated with higher mortality, faster cognitive decline and institutional placement (pp. 919–920).
- 4 NPS domains: affective/motivational, psychotic, disturbances of basic drives (sleep/eating), and disinhibited/socially inappropriate behavior.

**Diagnosis & differential**
- Get a reliable informant history — the patient often cannot self-report accurately (p920).
- **Rule out delirium first** for any acute or fluctuating change in cognition/behavior (p923).
- NPI = most-used research tool for NPS; NPI-Q = brief clinic version, measures **12 NPS** domains plus informant distress (p922).
- Medication review is essential: the anticholinergic-risk/delirium med list (**Table 60-3**, p922) includes non-obvious agents **digoxin** and **furosemide** alongside the classic anticholinergics (TCAs, diphenhydramine/hydroxyzine/promethazine, oxybutynin, scopolamine).
- New-onset acute NPS work-up: CBC, chem panel, LFTs, UA/culture; consider TSH, B12, folate, tox screen, ECG; chest x-ray if respiratory signs; imaging particularly if focal signs; EEG if seizure suspected; CSF rarely needed — indicated if meningitis/encephalitis suspected (p922). Interpret urine findings in context. With delirium and bacteriuria alone, and no urinary symptoms, fever or hemodynamic instability, assess other causes and observe rather than treating bacteriuria. [IDSA, recommendation V.1](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/asymptomatic-bacteriuria/).
- Depression affects up to **50%** of dementia patients and may go undetected — in dementia it is more likely to present with agitation, irritability, and anxiety (p923).
- Psychosis occurs in **20% to 30%** of AD patients vs **over 50%** of DLB/Parkinson disease dementia patients; visual hallucinations (more common in DLB) may be the most clinically useful feature to distinguish DLB from AD (p923–924).

**Management framework**
- DICE approach: Describe, Investigate, Create, Evaluate (p924).
- MIND at Home study: **99%** of community-dwelling dementia patients had unmet care needs (p924); **60% to 70%** of older adults with AD and other dementias live in the community, cared for by family and friends (p924).
- In the US about **25%** of people with dementia receive care from nonfamily caregivers (eg, home health aides), who may not always receive the necessary dementia-care training (p925).
- **Nonpharmacologic interventions are first-line** — exception is emergency situations where the safety of the patient or others is compromised (eg, severe agitation) (p925).
- Psychotropics only after nonpharmacologic approaches fail, EXCEPT 3 cases: clear-cut major depression; psychosis causing/risking harm; aggression causing/risking harm (p925).
- Psychotropics are routinely prescribed despite safety/efficacy concerns: in newly admitted nursing-home residents, only **12%** of new admissions got nonpharmacologic treatment in the first 3 months, while **71%** received at least one psychotropic (p927).

**Drugs — antipsychotics** (all carry a black-box mortality warning in dementia)
- No significant benefit after a **4-week period** → taper and withdraw; reassess at least monthly while tapering and for at least **4 months** after stopping (p927).
- For significant delirium-related agitation requiring short-term medication, the chapter gives these low PRN doses at six-hour intervals (p923). Haloperidol **0.25–0.5 mg** orally (IV only with appropriate monitoring), quetiapine **12.5–25 mg** orally, olanzapine **2.5 mg** orally or **1.25–2.5 mg** IM, or risperidone **0.25–0.5 mg orally**. There is no approved IV risperidone formulation.
  > Current practice (not the book): risperidone is not given IV; Risperdal Consta is IM only — "Do not administer intravenously" — Risperdal Consta prescribing information (Janssen).
- Risperidone **0.25–1 mg**/day — most effective for psychosis and agitation per AHRQ review; dose-dependent EPS, ↑cerebrovascular risk (p928).
- Olanzapine **2.5–10 mg**/day — most effective for agitation per AHRQ review; sedation, weight gain, glucose effects (p928).
- Quetiapine **12.5–150 mg**/day — sedation, orthostatic hypotension; with clozapine, less likely than other antipsychotics to worsen parkinsonian symptoms (p928).
- Aripiprazole **5–10 mg**/day — most effective for overall NPS per AHRQ review; lower EPS, nausea (p928).
- Haloperidol **0.5–5** mg/day — high EPS risk; should not be a first-line neuroleptic in nonemergent situations (p928). Retrospective cohorts: higher mortality than risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, ziprasidone, or quetiapine (p927).
- Clozapine **6.25–75 mg**/day — lower motor risk than potent dopamine blockers, but it can still cause serious harm. Evidence of benefit comes from Parkinson disease psychosis. Efficacy and tolerability specifically in DLB remain uncertain. Seek specialist input if persistent dangerous or severely distressing psychosis warrants a trial. [DLB Consortium consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5496518/). Use is limited by neutropenia/agranulocytosis — weekly blood draws in the first **6 months** (p928).
- Pimavanserin **34 mg**/day — FDA-approved for Parkinson disease psychosis; confusion, edema, hypotension (p928); Current practice (not the book): brexpiprazole received FDA approval in May 2023 for agitation associated with Alzheimer dementia; its boxed warning states increased mortality in older patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotics - AGS Alternatives 2025, Table 3 footnote e, p12; FDA announcement, 11 May 2023, approval and boxed-warning paragraphs

**Drugs — antidepressants**
- Citalopram — initial **5–10** mg, max **20** mg/day; CitAD trial showed benefit for agitation, but QTc prolongation and worsened cognition may limit its use (p929).
- Sertraline — initial **25** mg, max **200** mg/day; despite early promise, not shown superior to placebo for depression in dementia (p929).
- Escitalopram — the S-enantiomer of citalopram. S-CitAD, ongoing when p930 was written, later found no significant benefit for agitation at 12 weeks. [S-CitAD trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12951298/).
- Paroxetine — anticholinergic properties, should be avoided in older adults (p930); anticholinergic effects may increase delirium risk, and may cause withdrawal effects due to short half-life (p929).
- Mirtazapine — sedating at lower doses; in the sleep pilot, 8 received mirtazapine and 16 placebo (24 total). Nighttime sleep did not improve and daytime sleepiness increased. Page 930 misstates the treatment-group size. [Scoralick et al., methods and results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26818096/).
- Bupropion — avoid with a history of seizures or psychotic symptoms (p930).
- Nortriptyline — therapeutic window **50–150 ng/mL**; anticholinergic effects, hypotension, sedation (p929).
- Desipramine — the other preferred TCA in Table 60-9; therapeutic window **150–300 ng/mL** (higher than nortriptyline's — don't conflate the two), similar side-effect profile (p929).
- TCAs overall are limited by a high adverse-event burden compared to SSRIs/SNRIs (p930).

**Drugs — other / adjuncts**
- Benzodiazepines are used in **8.5% to 20%** of AD patients despite weak efficacy evidence; avoid, except emergencies (severe agitation with risk of harm) or failure of alternative pharmacologic interventions; if used, sparingly, short-acting preferred; glucuronidated agents (oxazepam, temazepam, lorazepam) preferable with complex medical comorbidities — no active metabolites (p931).
- Meta-analyses: donepezil, galantamine, and memantine beat placebo in reducing emergence of NPS (modest vs neuroleptics); AChI + memantine combination beats monotherapy and placebo; not first-line for acute NPS of moderate or greater severity, but reasonable for chronic NPS (p931).
- Methylphenidate improved apathy in AD in randomized trials; modafinil did not in one small RCT (p931).
- A Cochrane review found low-certainty evidence that melatonin doses **up to 10 mg** may have little or no sleep benefit (p931); one small trial of melatonin **3 mg** helped agitation/sundowning (p932); ramelteon showed no sleep benefit (p932).
- Anticonvulsants ("mood stabilizers") are generally disappointing: valproate is ineffective and poorly tolerated; carbamazepine helped in one small trial but is a potent enzyme inducer with marrow-toxicity risk (p930).

**Exam traps / book notes**
- Every drug/dose table in this chapter is flagged as not checked for Israeli registration, availability, or dosing — use the printed (US) values as given for board purposes (p928).
- The chapter's own text lists cholinesterase-inhibitor side effects as "eight loss" — a printing error for "weight loss" (correctly spelled in Table 60-9, p929); left as printed on p931.
- The 2026 exam paper misspells drug names (OLAZAPINE, Quentapine, Bopropion) — these are Olanzapine, Quetiapine, and Bupropion; don't let the spelling throw you (see reproduced questions below).

**11. Which antipsychotic has relatively low motor toxicity, and what limits extrapolating its Parkinson psychosis evidence to dementia with Lewy bodies?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Citalopram; limited by QTc prolongation and worsened cognition (p929).

**Q9** — sourced to p. 925. A 90-year-old woman, known to have Alzheimer-type dementia for about 7 years. Her family report that she was seen in the emergency department because of behavioural disturbances manifested as restlessness, shouting and delusions. Delirium was ruled out, as were an infectious process, pain, urinary retention, constipation and metabolic disturbances. The family finds it hard to care for her. Behavioural-environmental treatment failed. What is the recommended approach to continuing treatment?

- א. An atypical antipsychotic such as OLAZAPINE (sic) at a low dose for a short time  ← **key**
- ב. A benzodiazepine such as CLONEX for calming, long term
- ג. An antihistamine such as PROMETHAZINE before sleep
- ד. A typical antipsychotic such as HALOPERIDOL at a low dose, regularly

> **As printed on the paper.** "OLAZAPINE" is the paper's spelling; the book prints "Olanzapine" (Table 60-8, p928).

> Book: p925: "Due to the inherent risks of using medications to treat NPS in dementia, nonpharmacologic approaches should be first-line therapy. Psychotropics should be used only after other efforts have been made to mitigate NPS, with three exceptions: (1) clear-cut major depression; (2) psychosis causing harm or with significant potential of harm to self or others; and (3) aggression causing harm or risk of harm to self or other." Outside the pages the examiners cited, p927: "treatment should be initiated at a low dose and titrated to the minimum effective dose as tolerated." and "if there is no significant response after a 4-week period, the medication should be tapered and withdrawn."

**Q52** — sourced to p. 929. A 78-year-old man with ischaemic heart disease, CKD stage 3 and osteoarthritis, diagnosed with major depression. His PHQ-9 score is 17. He has marked anhedonia, psychomotor retardation and low energy. He reports mild anxiety, without panic attacks. There is no psychosis and no suicidality. He prefers drug treatment. Which drug treatment is the most appropriate choice for the depression he has?

- א. Sertraline  ← **key**
- ב. Quentapine (sic)
- ג. Bopropion (sic)
- ד. Amitriptyline

> **As printed on the paper.** "Quentapine" and "Bopropion" are the paper's spellings; the book prints "Quetiapine" (Table 60-8, p928) and "Bupropion" (Table 60-9, p929).

> Book: p929: Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors "are considered first-line therapy for late-life depression and are generally well tolerated compared to other classes of antidepressants such as TCAs." Table 60-9 (p929) lists sertraline under "SELECTIVE SEROTONIN REUPTAKE INHIBITORS" (initial daily dose 25 mg, maximum 200 mg).

For the wandering example below, preserve immediate emergency egress. A person who cannot unlock the door or summon help needs supervision and a workable fire-escape plan.

> **Disorientation or confusion with person/object recognition**
>
> - Provide reminders, cues, or prompts
> - Identify individuals if patient does not remember names or has aphasia
> - Use environmental cues (eg, calendars, keeping blinds raised during the day, keeping lights off at night)
> - Keep objects for individual tasks in separate, labeled containers
> - Use simple visual reminders (eg, pictures, arrows pointing to the bathroom)
>
> **Confusion/overwhelmed with tasks and environment**
>
> - Eliminate noise and distractions while patient is engaged in an activity
> - Break each task into very simple steps
> - Offer simple choices (no more than two at a time)
> - Provide simple verbal commands, using only one or two steps at a time
> - Remove unnecessary items from the room while patients is engaged in a task
> - Provide daily, structured, and predictable routines
>
> **Wandering or inability to respond appropriately to an emergency**
>
> - Provide 24-hour supervision for the patient
> - Place locks on all exits
> - Develop an emergency plan. Inform neighbors, fire department, and police of the patient’s condition
> - Provide patient with medical alert and safe return ID bracelets
>
> **Nighttime wakefulness**
>
> - Encourage good sleep hygiene (avoid stimulating activity, reduce caffeinated and alcoholic beverages that may affect sleep, limit daytime napping)
> - Create a quiet routine for bedtime
> - Evaluate environment for modifiable elements, such as temperature, noise level, light, shadows, and bed comfort
>
> **Anxiety, irritability, repetitive questioning, frustration**
>
> - Use a relaxed and reassuring voice and light touch to help calm or redirect
> - Avoid negative words and tone
> - Allow patient sufficient time to respond to questions
> - Help patient with aphasia find the words to express him/herself
> - Allow extra time for tasks and activities
> - Distract the patient as necessary
> - Understand that behaviors are not always intentional
>
> **Boredom and restlessness**
>
> - Create a structured, daily schedule that includes entertainment and exercise
> - Provide meaningful activities that draw on preserved capabilities and interests
> - Engage patients in activities involving repetitive motion (eg, folding towels, putting items in containers)
>
> **Delusions and hallucinations**
>
> - Go along with patient’s point of view of what is true. Avoid arguing or trying to reason as this can make the situation worse
> - Ignore symptoms that are not distressing the person
> - Distract the patient as necessary
>
> **Apathy**
>
> - Give the patient a job at home, such as folding laundry
> - Focus on the process of doing things rather than the results
>
> **Disinhibition**
>
> - Distract from an inappropriate topic by calmly and firmly changing the subject
> - Avoid television shows with violence or sexual content
