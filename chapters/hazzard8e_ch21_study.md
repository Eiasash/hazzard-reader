# Chapter 21 Study: The Patient Perspective

## Summary

**Listen to what patients experience**
- The US National Poll on Healthy Aging surveys adults aged **50-80**; the chapter draws on selected reports, not a single universal patient profile (p287).
- Primary care clinicians are trusted sources. Elicit concerns that may remain unspoken, including sexual health, emergency preparation and prescription costs (p287, p300).
- Brain-health concerns can coexist with little discussion with clinicians; ask about memory concerns and information needs (p287-288).

**Medicines and overlooked symptoms**
- Medication review should include actual use, nonprescription products, multiple prescribers/pharmacies, adverse effects and affordability (p288).
- Explain antibiotic indications, harms and resistance; unnecessary prescriptions and leftover use remain concerns (p288-289).
- Most respondents discussed **how often to take** an opioid with the prescriber. Fewer discussed side effects, when to reduce the dose, overdose or addiction. Ask about leftover pills and safe disposal (p289–290).
- The poll asked about marijuana’s **pain-relief benefits**: 31% thought it definitely relieved pain and 38% thought it probably did. These are respondents’ beliefs (p290).
- Invite sexual-health discussions. Ask about urinary incontinence: some women do not raise it because they consider it mild or a normal part of aging (p290–292).
- Dental coverage/costs affect access. Explain why a requested test or treatment may provide little benefit rather than assuming patients want more care (p292).

**Access, decisions and daily life**
- Telehealth and patient portals can improve access but technical barriers, privacy, examination limits and unequal uptake matter (p293).
- Discuss expected health-care costs around retirement. In the poll, 13% of adults aged 50–64 went without medical care because of cost in the prior year. Some postponed retirement to retain employer-sponsored coverage (p294).
- Advance planning links care to values and chosen decision makers.
- Screen for food insecurity and connect with assistance. Emergency plans should address supplies, evacuation and communication (p296-297).
- Pets may support companionship and activity but also create cost, mobility or fall concerns; dementia caregivers may need more support and training (p297-298).
- Loneliness increased in early pandemic surveys. Home/neighborhood resources affect isolation, safe activity and ability to follow public-health advice (p298-300).

### Read the denominator before using a poll result

The National Poll on Healthy Aging typically sampled approximately **2,000** US adults aged **50–80** per wave, with typical completion rates of **60%–80%**. Some questions went only to a narrower age or symptom group. Among those with a family history the proportion was **10%**. Asking about worries can reveal a concern that has never been voiced in a visit (p287–288).

Among women reporting urinary leakage, **41%** described it as a problem and **31%** reported leakage almost daily. These are percentages among women with leakage, not prevalence in all women. The commonest explanation for silence was that the leakage did not seem bad enough. A simple question about bother can be more informative than waiting for the patient to request treatment (p291–292).

Preparedness also depends on what has to keep working. Among respondents using essential equipment requiring electricity, only **25%** had an alternative power source. Ask a person using such equipment what would happen during an outage, alongside questions about food, water, medicines and contact plans (p297).

### What a patient poll adds to the clinical record

The National Poll on Healthy Aging asked US adults about experiences, beliefs and decisions that are often absent from a medical record. A chart can reveal that an antibiotic was prescribed without revealing that tablets remain at home for a future illness. It can record a cognitive-screening result without recording fear of dementia, and list insurance without showing that a patient deferred treatment because of cost. The chapter uses these differences to suggest questions a clinician can ask. The surveys do not supply diagnoses, drug efficacy estimates or prescriptions for an individual respondent (p287–289, p300).

Read every result with four details in mind: the people eligible for the question, its wording, its time window and whether the percentage refers to all respondents or a subgroup. A percentage among women who already report leakage is not the prevalence of leakage among all women. A question about whether a clinician offered telehealth asks something different from whether the patient used it. A report about people aged 50–64 may address work and insurance circumstances distinct from those of people aged 65–80. Table 21-1 describes the respondent demographics (n = 2,023); the data were collected at different times rather than in one continuous clinical cohort (p287–288, p291–294).

Recruitment of approximately **2,000 people per wave** and typically **60%–80% completion** support population-level descriptions within the poll design; they do not remove nonresponse or recall limitations. Many results concern events over the previous year or two. A respondent's report of a conversation need not capture everything a clinician documented, but it does tell us something about what the patient remembered or understood. When surveys report different rates in separate calendar periods, consider changes in availability, policy or daily life before interpreting them as a change in an individual's preference (p287–288, p293, p298–299).

### Hidden concerns, supplements and prescribed medicines

For adults **50–64**, worry about dementia was more common in those with a family history than those without, **66% versus 28%**. It was also more common among those who had cared for someone with dementia. Yet only **5%** of the age group had discussed dementia prevention with their physician, rising to **10%** among those with family history. Many respondents were doing puzzles, taking vitamins or trying fish oil in the hope of helping memory. The finding is a communication gap. It neither validates a supplement nor implies that a person who reports worry already has cognitive impairment (p287–288).

A useful conversation starts with what prompted the concern. A parent may have recently received a diagnosis, a spouse may have noticed a change, or the patient may have bought a supplement after seeing advertising. Distinguish concern about future risk from a present complaint deserving assessment. Ask what the person uses, what benefit they expect and whether cost or an interaction matters. Responding only with a normal brief cognitive score can leave the original worry untouched; accepting the person's account of benefit from a supplement as proof of prevention answers a different question (p287–288).

In an April 2017 wave, **69%** of older adults had seen at least two doctors during the preceding year and **21%** had used more than one pharmacy during the preceding two years. People may reasonably use different pharmacies because of cost or convenience, but no single dispensing list then shows the whole regimen. Among adults aged 50–80, **27%** described prescription costs as a burden. Ask about drugs not bought, doses stretched and products obtained elsewhere. A prescription reconciliation based only on what a clinician intended to prescribe can miss an affordability problem and expose the patient to further escalation (p288).

Among respondents taking two or more prescription drugs, **24%** had ever received a comprehensive medication review. A refill encounter need not include evaluation of the whole regimen. Clarify how often each medicine is actually taken, what the patient believes it does, whether it causes troublesome symptoms and which clinician or pharmacist maintains the list. Include supplements and products the patient does not regard as medicines. A review can uncover a lower-cost alternative or an interaction as well as a medication that should stop; invite the patient to identify the question they most want the review to answer (p288).

### Antibiotics, opioids and cannabis: what happens after the visit

Within the preceding two years **48%** of respondents aged 50–80 reported filling an antibiotic prescription. Of those who filled one, **13%** had leftovers, and of those keeping leftovers, **60%** did so in case of another infection. Each percentage describes a smaller nested population. Across all respondents, **19%** reported ever taking an antibiotic without first talking to a clinician. Ask what happened to dispensed tablets and why they were retained. A person who anticipates difficulty reaching medical care may see leftovers as a practical safeguard, so counsel about appropriate evaluation and access as well as safe disposal (p288–289).

The opioid poll similarly follows a prescription beyond dispensing. Figure 21-1 separates the instructions patients remember about dose frequency from discussion of adverse effects, reduction of the dose, overdose or dependence. Someone can correctly state the dose interval while remaining unsure when to stop, what sedation to report, or how to dispose of unused tablets. Review actual use, concurrent medicines and storage, and identify who will advise the patient if pain persists. The poll's finding about counseling measures what respondents recall being told; it is not a comparison of analgesic efficacy or a way to infer misuse simply from unused tablets (p289–290).

For medical cannabis, October 2017 respondents reported both experience and opinion. **6%** reported using it medically, whereas **70%** expressed interest in discussing its medical uses with a clinician. Only **21%** recalled their primary clinician asking about use. Respondents who thought it definitely or probably relieves pain were expressing beliefs. Asking what product is being used and for which symptom supplies a more useful starting point than assuming the respondent is either an advocate or a nonuser. An interest in discussing the issue may reflect curiosity, a family member's advice, concerns about adverse effects or a desire for alternatives to existing pain treatment (p290).

### Symptoms people do not necessarily volunteer

Among adults **65–80**, **40%** reported current sexual activity and **76%** agreed that sex could be important to a romantic relationship at any age. Yet **17%** had discussed sexual health with a health professional in the preceding two years; among those who had discussed it, **60%** initiated the conversation themselves. A patient who does not raise a concern is not necessarily uninterested. Offer privacy and neutral wording, ask whether function or a medication effect has changed, and respect a preference to decline. Do not infer sexual activity, partnership or preferences from age or the presence of illness (p290–291).

In the women's continence survey, **46%** reported urinary leakage in the preceding year. Now change the denominator: among those with leakage, **41%** considered it a problem, **31%** leaked almost daily and **34%** had spoken to a doctor. Bother, frequency and disclosure measure different things. A woman can have frequent leakage she has incorporated into her routines, or have infrequent accidents that prevent travel or social activity. Ask about timing, practical effects and interest in treatment rather than deciding from frequency alone how problematic it is (p291–292).

Reasons for silence included feeling the problem was not sufficiently severe, competing concerns during a visit, perceiving leakage as a normal part of aging, embarrassment and not being asked. Normalizing the **conversation** is helpful; normalizing the symptom as inevitable can discourage evaluation. Ask about barriers to reaching a toilet and the cost or inconvenience of managing leakage. The most informative follow-up may be the activity the person avoids. A sign on a questionnaire should lead to a discussion of diagnosis and options rather than an assumption that every respondent wants more appointments or a medication (p291–292).

Dental and oral health affect eating, appearance, social comfort and ability to speak confidently. In the survey of adults **65–80**, **25%** described oral health as fair or poor and **27%** reported embarrassment about their teeth. Denture ownership does not establish that all lost teeth were replaced or that the appliance works well. Some people delay dental appointments because of cost, uncertainty about coverage, difficulty finding a dentist or transport. A discussion with someone approaching retirement should ask how regular dental care will continue, rather than assuming future coverage from their present medical insurance arrangement (p292).

### More care, less care or the right explanation?

The chapter's overuse questions explore whether patients perceive low-value testing and treatment and how they react when a clinician explains the decision. A patient requesting imaging may be communicating fear that a diagnosis is being missed; refusing a test without addressing that fear need not reassure them. Likewise, a patient declining a recommended investigation may be concerned about cost, discomfort, uncertain benefit or what will happen after an abnormal result. Ask what the patient hopes the test will establish and explain how the result would or would not alter management. These conversations are as relevant to avoiding unnecessary services as to completing important ones (p292–293).

Telehealth illustrates the difference between access and preference. In May **2019**, **14%** of respondents said their providers offered telehealth, rising to **62%** in June **2020**. The proportion who had ever used it rose from **4% to 30%**. During the early pandemic, some appointments were available only remotely or were converted from in-person visits. Increased use thus reflected both availability and circumstances. Satisfaction with avoiding travel does not establish that someone prefers a remote visit for a new physical symptom that requires examination (p293).

Ask about the task and the technology. A video review of a stable treatment plan may save a lengthy journey, whereas a physical finding may be difficult to evaluate remotely. Hearing, vision, language, bandwidth, devices, privacy and the availability of help at the time of the visit all influence feasibility. Patient portals can provide access to test results, messages and appointment information, but an unactivated account is not necessarily a refusal of digital care. Determine whether the patient knows how to use it and whether another communication route is needed. Keep a workable telephone option when a portal becomes a barrier to timely advice (p293–294).

### Emergency care, insurance decisions and advance planning

In June 2020, **26%** of adults aged 50–80 reported an emergency department visit during the preceding two years. The reported proportions were higher in the older age stratum and among people rating their physical or mental health as fair or poor. These are utilization descriptions. A visit may reflect an acute emergency, direction from a clinician, difficulty obtaining timely outpatient care or uncertainty about which symptom is dangerous. Ask what led to the visit and what the patient understood on discharge. A message saying “avoid the ED” cannot replace an accessible plan for the next urgent change (p294).

Among adults nearing Medicare age, continuity of insurance can influence work and retirement decisions as well as use of medical services. Others faced uncertainty about costs or went without care. Ask specifically about an upcoming change in eligibility, prescription access, dental care and out-of-pocket costs. A patient who cannot afford the next refill may not raise this during a visit focused on laboratory results. Connect the person with informed advice about their own coverage rather than treating figures from this US survey as a universal entitlement (p292, p294).

Advance care planning involves at least three related tasks: clarifying preferences, sharing them with people likely to participate in a decision and documenting how decisions should be made if the person cannot speak for themselves. A discussion with family, identification of a surrogate and an accessible written directive are not equivalent accomplishments. Figure 21-6 asks about these separate activities. Explore whom the person trusts and whether that individual knows their values. An advance directive stored somewhere inaccessible may be less useful at a crisis than expected, while prior conversations can help a surrogate apply preferences to an unfamiliar situation (p294–295).

### Daily life can make an otherwise reasonable plan impossible

Table 21-2 divides everyday ageism into messages about aging, interpersonal experiences and beliefs people have internalized (p294-295). Examples include hearing that older people cannot learn technology, experiencing condescension or assuming a health problem is unavoidable because of age. A clinical conversation can reinforce those messages if a new symptom is explained by age without evaluation. Ask the patient directly what they want to try and what support would help. Survey associations between greater reported ageism and poorer health should not be interpreted as proof of the direction of causation in any particular encounter (p295–296).

Food insecurity is a household resource problem, not simply a patient's food preference. In the poll **14%** reported household food insecurity over the previous year; among that group **42%** reported severe food insecurity. Insecure households more often reported fair or poor physical and mental health. A dietary plan may be medically sensible and financially impossible. Ask whether food ran short or the household could obtain enough of the foods recommended, and what transport, community assistance or practical changes may help. An adequate weight or a household that sometimes purchases fresh food does not settle the question of reliable access (p296).

Emergency preparedness requires more than a general intention to stay safe. Determine whether the patient can receive alerts, contact someone, reach an evacuation destination and maintain medication and necessary supplies. Among respondents who needed medicines or health supplies, **82%** reported a seven-day supply of medicine and **72%** a seven-day supply of health supplies. The equipment-dependent subgroup had another need: electricity. A prescription refill and a backup power source address different risks. Ask who would help if usual transport, payment or household support were interrupted and where written information could be found (p296–297).

Pets can give structure, companionship, activity and a reason to leave the home. In the survey **55%** of older respondents had a pet. Among pet owners, **88%** said pets helped them enjoy life and **79%** reported less stress. A benefit endorsed in a survey does not show that obtaining a pet will treat loneliness for everyone. Some owners face expense, fall risk or anxiety about who will care for the animal during hospitalization. An otherwise acceptable rehabilitation or evacuation plan can be unacceptable if it ignores that responsibility; ask about it rather than interpreting hesitation as unwillingness to receive care (p297–298).

### Caregiving, isolation and the built environment

In the dementia-caregiver subgroup, both positive and negative experiences were common: **85%** found caregiving rewarding and **78%** found it stressful; **62%** reported both. The categories overlap. Many combined caregiving with paid employment and had already provided support for years. Identify the tasks the caregiver performs, their training, backup and opportunities for rest. Someone who values caring for a parent can simultaneously need help with behavioral symptoms, medications or time away. Asking only whether caregiving is rewarding is therefore an inadequate screen for support needs (p298).

Loneliness is subjective and need not coincide with living alone. The surveys compared companionship, perceived isolation and frequency of social contact. In June 2020, **48%** felt more isolated than before the pandemic, **8%** felt less isolated and **44%** felt much the same. Ask whether contact is frequent enough and meaningful to the person, whether hearing or mobility makes it difficult, and what kind of connection they would welcome. A video call may help one person and be unusable for another. Separate the distribution in the early pandemic from an assumption about how an individual feels now (p298–299).

The built-environment questions clarify why identical public-health advice was not equally feasible. Among those living with others **18%** reported no safe place within the home to isolate. Among adults aged 50–80, **85%** reported an outdoor space for contact with neighbors, **72%** a view of nature and **68%** a green space within walking distance. Accessible outdoor space was associated with outdoor activity; it was not randomly allocated. Before advising walking or meeting outdoors, ask about stairs, nearby space, assistance, safety and the practical meaning of “walking distance” for this person. The patient's environment can explain a barrier that is invisible during an office visit (p299–300).

### What the poll figures actually compare

- **Figure 21-2** asks about approaching a clinician *if a serious condition might respond* to medical cannabis. **44%** said definitely, **26%** probably, **17%** were unsure and **13%** said no. It measures willingness to ask under that condition, distinct from both current use and belief in pain relief (p290).
- **Figure 21-3** reports that **76%** of adults aged 65–80 valued sex in a romantic relationship at any age. Among those **in a romantic relationship, 54%** were sexually active. **Figure 21-4** shows strategies reported by women managing leakage: **59%** pads or protective garments, **38%** pelvic-floor exercise, **16%** limiting fluids and **15%** changing clothing choices to conceal accidents. Ask which strategies the patient uses; this is a description of behavior, not a recommendation to restrict fluids (p291).
- **Figure 21-5** separates concerns about remote visits. In June 2020, **75%** cited the inability to perform an examination, **67%** perceived lower quality, **45%** less personal connection, **25%** difficulty seeing or hearing clinicians and **24%** privacy. A technical fix alone will not answer a patient who needs examination (p293).
- **Figure 21-7** compares food-insecure and food-secure respondents. In that order, **45% versus 14%** reported fair/poor physical health, **24% versus 5%** fair/poor mental health and **43% versus 20%** fair/poor diet. **Figure 21-8** measures separate preparations: **55%** had a seven-day food and water supply, **44%** had local emergency alerts and **40%** had discussed evacuation with family or a friend (p296–297).
- **Figure 21-9** describes pet owners: **79%** reported reduced stress, **73%** a sense of purpose, **65%** greater connection and **64%** help being active. **Figure 21-10** compares October 2018 with March–June 2020: lack of companionship rose from **34% to 41%**, perceived isolation **27% to 56%**, and infrequent contacts **28% to 46%**. These three indicators are related, but someone with frequent contact can still feel lonely (p298–299).

## Exam traps

- Concern about dementia, discussion of prevention with a clinician and actual risk of dementia are different outcomes (p287–288).
- Separate a belief about marijuana's effectiveness from clinical evidence of analgesia (p290).
- Being offered a telehealth visit and having used one were measured separately in the survey (p293).
