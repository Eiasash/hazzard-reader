# Chapter 21 Study: The Patient Perspective

## Summary

**Listen to what patients experience**
- The US National Poll on Healthy Aging surveys adults aged **50-80**; the chapter draws on selected reports, not a single universal patient profile (p287).
- Primary care clinicians are trusted sources. Elicit concerns that may remain unspoken, including sexual health, emergency preparation and prescription costs (p287, p300).
- Brain-health concerns can coexist with little discussion with clinicians; ask about memory concerns and information needs (p287-288).

**Medicines and overlooked symptoms**
- Medication review should include actual use, nonprescription products, multiple prescribers/pharmacies, adverse effects and affordability (p288).
- Explain antibiotic indications, harms and resistance; unnecessary prescriptions and leftover use remain concerns (p288-289).
- Most respondents discussed **how often to take** an opioid with the prescriber. Fewer discussed side effects, when to reduce the dose, overdose or addiction. Ask about leftover pills and safe disposal (p289–290).
- The poll asked about marijuana’s **pain-relief benefits**: 31% thought it definitely relieved pain and 38% thought it probably did. These are respondents’ beliefs (p290).
- Invite sexual-health discussions. Ask about urinary incontinence: some women do not raise it because they consider it mild or a normal part of aging (p290–292).
- Dental coverage/costs affect access. Explain why a requested test or treatment may provide little benefit rather than assuming patients want more care (p292).

**Access, decisions and daily life**
- Telehealth and patient portals can improve access but technical barriers, privacy, examination limits and unequal uptake matter (p293).
- Discuss expected health-care costs around retirement. In the poll, 13% of adults aged 50–64 went without medical care because of cost in the prior year. Some postponed retirement to retain employer-sponsored coverage (p294).
- Advance planning links care to values and chosen decision makers. Everyday ageism includes messages, interpersonal experiences and internalized beliefs (p294-295).
- Screen for food insecurity and connect with assistance. Emergency plans should address supplies, evacuation and communication (p296-297).
- Pets may support companionship and activity but also create cost, mobility or fall concerns; dementia caregivers may need more support and training (p297-298).
- Loneliness increased in early pandemic surveys. Home/neighborhood resources affect isolation, safe activity and ability to follow public-health advice (p298-300).

### Read the denominator before using a poll result

The National Poll on Healthy Aging typically sampled approximately **2,000** US adults aged **50–80** per wave, with typical completion rates of **60%–80%**. Some questions went only to a narrower age or symptom group. Among adults aged 50–64, **5%** had discussed dementia prevention with a physician. Among those with a family history the proportion was **10%**. Asking about worries can reveal a concern that has never been voiced in a visit (p287–288).

Among women reporting urinary leakage, **41%** described it as a problem and **31%** reported leakage almost daily. These are percentages among women with leakage, not prevalence in all women. The commonest explanation for silence was that the leakage did not seem bad enough. A simple question about bother can be more informative than waiting for the patient to request treatment (p291–292).

Preparedness also depends on what has to keep working. Among respondents using essential equipment requiring electricity, only **25%** had an alternative power source. Ask a person using such equipment what would happen during an outage, alongside questions about food, water, medicines and contact plans (p297).

## Exam traps

- Concern about dementia, discussion of prevention with a clinician and actual risk of dementia are different outcomes (p287–288).
- Separate a belief about marijuana's effectiveness from clinical evidence of analgesia (p290).
- Being offered a telehealth visit and having used one were measured separately in the survey (p293).

## Recall drill

**1. Who was represented by the poll described in this chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>US adults aged <strong>50–80</strong>, with some reports limited to particular age or symptom groups. Interpret each question using its own denominator. (p287)</details>

**2. What mismatch did the brain-health poll bring to light?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Many respondents worried about dementia but few aged 50–64 had discussed prevention with a physician, even among those with a family history. (p287–288)</details>

**3. How can care from several prescribers or pharmacies affect medication review?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Each clinician or pharmacist may see an incomplete list. Reconstruct what the patient actually takes, including nonprescription items. (p288)</details>

**4. Why ask about leftover antibiotics?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Some respondents saved them for a future illness or another person. Ask how they intend to use or dispose of them and explain appropriate use. (p288–289)</details>

**5. Which topics were less often discussed than opioid dosing frequency?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Adverse effects, when to reduce the dose, overdose and addiction. Include storage and disposal of leftovers in the conversation. (p289–290)</details>

**6. What can the marijuana poll tell a clinician about pain treatment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>What respondents believed about pain relief and whether they disclosed use. It cannot establish the efficacy of an analgesic. (p290)</details>

**7. Which two urinary-leakage estimates have different denominators?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Among surveyed women, <strong>46%</strong> reported leakage in the prior year. Among those reporting leakage, <strong>31%</strong> had episodes almost daily. (p291)</details>

**8. What commonly kept women from raising urinary leakage with a clinician?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Many felt it was not serious enough, had other issues to discuss or did not consider it a health problem. (p291–292)</details>

**9. What might be missed if a clinician assumes a patient is asking for another test?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The patient may be concerned about harm, cost or unnecessary testing. Discuss what the result would change and why a test may offer little benefit. (p292)</details>

**10. Why distinguish the offer of telehealth from actual uptake?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A service can be available while technology, privacy or the need for an examination makes a visit difficult to use. (p293)</details>

**11. What did the poll find about costs before Medicare age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Among respondents aged 50–64, <strong>13%</strong> had gone without medical care because of cost in the previous year. Employer insurance also influenced retirement decisions. (p294)</details>

**12. What are the three forms of everyday ageism discussed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ageist messages in the environment, ageism in interactions and ageist beliefs a person comes to hold about themself. (p295)</details>

**13. What is the financial dimension of food insecurity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Difficulty obtaining food because money is short. Ask about access to food and use of assistance programs. (p296)</details>

**14. Which emergency-preparedness question matters particularly for electrically powered medical equipment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask about backup power. In the relevant poll subgroup, only <strong>25%</strong> reported an alternative source. (p297)</details>

**15. How should the 2020 finding on isolation be interpreted?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It describes self-reported isolation during the early pandemic compared with before it. Individual experiences varied, including respondents reporting no change. (p299)</details>
