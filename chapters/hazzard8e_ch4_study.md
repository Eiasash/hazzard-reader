# Chapter 4 Study: Psychosocial Aspects of Aging

> Excluded from the IMA required reading list (P005-2026); not cited in the 2020-2026 past papers.

## Summary

**Life course, relationships and resilience**
- Chronologic age does not define a uniform psychosocial stage. Culture, prior experience, biology and available resources shape aging and responses to loss (p65-67).
- Social integration and satisfying relationships support meaning and well-being; low social activity and loneliness are associated with worse health and function (p68).
- Loneliness is perceived isolation, distinct from simply counting contacts. Explore relationship quality as well as network size (p68).
- Families remain central sources of long-term support. Caregiving depends on patient needs, family resources, relationships and the wider social setting (p69).

**Stress and emotional life**
- Prolonged stress can overwhelm regulatory capacity and affect decision making and physical health. Assess both major events and continuing strain (p70-71).
- Caregiving stress has immune/neuroendocrine correlates; personality and coping also influence vulnerability and adjustment (p71).
- Emotional trajectories vary with age, health and disability; older age does not imply uniformly poorer mood or satisfaction (p71-73).
- Retirement is not uniformly harmful: control over timing, resources and replacement roles influence adaptation (p78).
- Widowhood, caregiving and raising grandchildren can bring substantial strain. Assess support, financial pressures, depressive symptoms and burden (p78).

**Support participation and patient-defined goals**
- Aging in place requires accessible housing, community resources and social support; home preference alone does not meet changing care needs (p73-76).
- Community agencies, villages and naturally occurring retirement communities may support independence; volunteer activity and lifelong learning can promote engagement (p76-77).
- Sensory, physical or cognitive barriers may hinder informed decisions without removing interest in participating. Ask whether patients feel informed (p78-79).
- Quality of life extends beyond health status; meaningful goals and valuation of life should guide care alongside functional assessment (p79).
- Advance planning clarifies values and future preferences. Persistent grief or existential distress may need targeted help; dignity therapy may support meaning near end of life (p79-80).
- Include mood, social support, finances, home safety, caregivers, burden, spirituality and decision-making arrangements in comprehensive geriatric assessment (p80).
