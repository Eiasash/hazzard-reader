## Choosing initial treatment at 135/85 or 165/90

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7559bbef5d36d19293f61fdb" data-exam-read-page="1221" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Classify the confirmed average.** Table 79-1 gives normal as systolic below 120 **and** diastolic below 80, elevated as 120–129 **and** below 80, stage 1 as 130–139 **or** 80–89, and stage 2 as at least 140 **or** at least 90. Use the higher category if the two numbers differ. An independent 68-year-old at 135/85 falls into stage 1 on both numbers (p. 1221).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7559bbef5d36d19293f61fdb" data-exam-read-page="1225" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Treat the patient with uncomplicated stage 1 hypertension.** Table 79-3 starts with a six-month trial of exercise, sodium reduction and weight loss if overweight. Continue those measures if a medicine is added. Reassess risk and response: an established cardiovascular or renal indication can change the initial plan, and persistent pressure above the goal can justify a low-dose thiazide-type drug. A monthly reading by itself treats neither excess sodium nor inactivity (pp. 1224–1225).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ba95508abaa2e8c6c5b63ba6" data-exam-read-page="1225" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Treat sustained stage 2 hypertension.** At 165/90 after months of lifestyle changes, a 75-year-old will usually need two agents from the outset, according to the chapter. Low doses with complementary effects can achieve more pressure reduction while limiting dose-related adverse effects. A thiazide-type diuretic plus an ACE inhibitor is one example. Both earlier and later exam cases included osteoporosis: thiazides can improve bone mineral density, so the bone history does not exclude them. Hyponatremia, gout, potassium loss and troublesome urinary frequency can alter that choice (pp. 1225–1226).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ba95508abaa2e8c6c5b63ba6" data-exam-read-page="1224" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Choose the combination by the individual's other conditions.** Reduction of pressure is the main determinant of vascular benefit. Once the indication to treat is clear, a second agent should fit coexisting illness, adverse effects, cost and the person's ability to take it every day. A long-acting dihydropyridine such as amlodipine is a reasonable initial choice in the older case. Assess ankle edema and standing pressure. Doxazosin carries an orthostatic burden and, in ALLHAT, more heart-failure admissions than chlorthalidone (pp. 1224–1226).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ba95508abaa2e8c6c5b63ba6" data-exam-read-page="1223" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Separate a patient's goal from a trial target.** The 2017 US guideline discussed in the chapter recommends a systolic goal below 130 mm Hg for ambulatory community-living adults aged 65 or older with an average systolic pressure of at least 130. The prose on p1223 summarizes the goal as 130 mm Hg; pp1220 and 1225 specify **below 130**. [ACC/AHA 2017, section 10.3.1](https://doi.org/10.1161/HYP.0000000000000065). Age 75 alone does not require a target below 120. When intensification to a seated goal causes weakness, fatigue or light-headedness on standing, reassess supine and standing readings and consider using the upright pressure to guide treatment (p. 1223).

## When a high diastolic pressure changes the workup

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-5c4353506d173329433ddcfd" data-exam-read-page="1222" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**150/110 in an 85-year-old is an unusual pattern.** Most older patients have essential hypertension, typically systolic-predominant. A marked or newly elevated diastolic pressure warrants particular attention to renovascular disease. A renal artery duplex can assess for stenosis. Check technique and the time course, review NSAIDs and corticosteroids, and look for target-organ injury. The history determines which additional tests will answer a clinical question (pp. 1222–1223).

Sleep testing follows clues to obstructive sleep apnea, such as snoring and witnessed pauses. Spells of headache, sweating and palpitations raise suspicion of catecholamine excess. Progressive proximal weakness, bruising or broad purple striae can justify testing for hypercortisolism. These tests are targeted investigations. The published stem supplies only the blood-pressure pattern (p. 1222).

> Current practice (not the book): Use plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines for suspected catecholamine excess. Overnight dexamethasone suppression is one initial test for suspected hypercortisolism. [Pheochromocytoma guideline, recommendation 1.1](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/99/6/1915/2537399) · [Cushing syndrome guideline, recommendations 3.2–3.4](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/diagnosis-of-cushing-syndrome).

## Cardiovascular benefit, frailty and time to benefit

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-7faf3ea2990c59570295210c" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Give the absolute effect and its time horizon together.** Six randomized target trials included 27,414 people aged 60 or older, mean age 70. Intensive treatment lowered the hazard of a major cardiovascular event by 21%. Preventing one event per 500 took an estimated 9.1 months, one per 200 took 19.1 months, and **one per 100 took 34.4 months** (95% CI 22.7–59.8). The estimates refer to groups, with different pressure targets across trials. A fixed five-year survival threshold is unwarranted. Harms can occur during titration, no time-to-harm estimate was calculated, and people over 80 were underrepresented. [Chen et al., Table 2 and Limitations](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2791680).

**Who was in SPRINT?** Among 2,636 ambulatory participants aged 75 or older, targets below 120 versus below 140 systolic yielded relative reductions of 34% in the cardiovascular composite and 33% in all-cause mortality over a median 3.14 years. The corresponding numbers needed to treat were 27 and 41. Measured frailty or slow gait did not identify a subgroup that lost that benefit. Overall serious adverse events and injurious falls were similar across groups, and health-related quality of life was preserved. Monitor for hypotension, renal injury and electrolyte disturbance (pp1222–1223).

> Current practice (not the book): Nursing-home residents and people with dementia, diabetes, prior stroke, standing systolic pressure below 110 or a life expectancy under three years were excluded. A patient with advanced dependency and recurrent aspiration needs a discussion of prognosis, burdens and goals. [SPRINT, participants aged 75 or older, Methods and Results](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.7050).

**Cognitive outcomes require their own endpoint.** SPRINT-MIND found less mild cognitive impairment and less of the composite of MCI or probable dementia. Its 17% relative reduction in probable dementia alone did not reach statistical significance at the follow-up reported in this chapter. STEP, a younger Chinese cohort aged 60–80, also found fewer cardiovascular events with a lower systolic target. Differences in participants and measurement methods limit direct comparison (pp. 1223, 1227).

## Choosing a drug when another condition matters

**Previous myocardial infarction.** In a patient with hypertension and established coronary disease, ACE inhibition can address both blood pressure and secondary prevention. Follow creatinine and potassium after starting. Hazzard reserves beta blockers for compelling indications, specifically secondary prevention after MI or in coronary artery disease. Diabetes alone does not favor beta blockade, a diuretic in kidney disease is especially useful for volume overload, and verapamil or diltiazem can worsen reduced systolic function (pp. 1225–1226).

> Current practice (not the book): Long-term beta blockade for a prior MI may be reassessed after one year in patients without reduced ventricular function, angina, arrhythmia or uncontrolled hypertension. [AHA/ACC chronic coronary disease guideline, sections 4.3.2–4.3.3](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001168).

**A thiazide can do too much.** Chlorthalidone has greater milligram potency and a longer half-life than hydrochlorothiazide. Diuresis can aggravate nocturia or incontinence, and thiazides can cause low sodium, low potassium, hyperuricemia and impaired glucose tolerance. A fall, low potassium and a symptomatic postural drop prompt review of the diuretic and volume status. If treating potassium depletion, reassess renal function and potassium after replacement, especially when an ACE inhibitor is continued (pp. 1225–1226).

**Choose a tolerable regimen.** For a patient who accepts oral medication but previously developed thiazide-associated hyponatremia, an ACE inhibitor or ARB may fit if renal function and potassium permit. Dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers can cause edema. Centrally acting drugs can cause sedation, and direct vasodilators can produce marked orthostasis. An alpha blocker may help urinary obstruction but can worsen standing dizziness. A once-daily regimen, medication review and a follow-up plan matter as much as the initial prescription (pp. 1225–1227).

## Measurement, physiology and follow-up

**Why systolic and pulse pressures rise.** Stiff large arteries buffer the stroke volume less effectively, raising systolic pressure and widening the systolic–diastolic difference. Reduced baroreceptor sensitivity impairs rapid compensation after standing or a meal. Lower renal sodium excretion promotes volume expansion, and impaired nitric oxide–mediated dilation adds resistance. These mechanisms explain why hypertension and symptomatic postural drops can coexist. In Framingham, nearly nine in ten initially normotensive 55-year-olds developed hypertension during the following 20–25 years (pp. 1219–1222).

**Establish the pressure you intend to treat.** Allow five minutes seated, feet supported, with the correct cuff. The chapter describes averaging at least nine readings over three office visits, or using home or 24-hour ambulatory measurements. An automated unattended series reduces observer error. High office and lower home readings suggest a white-coat effect. Check supine and standing readings to detect orthostasis, and assess renal function, proteinuria, retinal changes and evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy when estimating risk (pp. 1222–1223).

**Follow the treated patient.** With very high pressure, acute neurologic, cardiac or aortic injury changes management urgently. In its absence, rapid normalization can impair perfusion. Reduce pressure in stages. When pressure has remained below an individualized goal after lifestyle changes, consider a supervised medication reduction with planned follow-up. In TONE, modest sodium restriction and weight loss reduced the need to restart medication. Home readings, a practical regimen and team follow-up can help distinguish sustained control from missed doses or an inadequate regimen (pp. 1223–1227).

## Israeli context

## Summary

**Mechanisms & risk**
- Arterial stiffness drives age-associated systolic and pulse-pressure elevation; reduced baroreflex sensitivity increases variability and postural/postprandial hypotension (p1219, p1221).
- Renal sodium retention, sympathetic activity, central adiposity and inactivity contribute. High blood pressure should not be dismissed as normal aging (p1219, p1221).

**Diagnosis**
- Measure after at least **5 minutes** seated rest with feet on the floor, appropriate cuff and standardized technique (p1222).
- Diagnose from repeated averages, not a single value; the chapter describes multiple readings over separate visits or **24-hour** ambulatory/home measurements (p1219, p1222).
- The chapter's cited guideline lowered the systolic diagnostic threshold to **130 mm Hg** (p1220).
- Assess renal disease/proteinuria, retinal and cardiac effects, cardiovascular risks, frailty and comorbidity (p1223).

**Targets & management**
- For ambulatory community-living older adults, p1223 summarizes the goal as **130 mm Hg**. Pages 1220 and 1225, and the 2017 guideline, specify **below 130** if average systolic pressure is at least 130. Substantial comorbidity or limited life expectancy calls for individualized judgment.
- SPRINT compared systolic targets below **120** versus **140 mm Hg**. In participants at least **75**, intensive treatment reduced the primary cardiovascular outcome by **34%** and all-cause mortality by **33%** (p1223).
- Support sodium reduction, weight management and physical activity; lifestyle changes remain useful alongside medication (p1224).
- Select medication using comorbid indications, tolerability, cost and regimen simplicity; achieved BP reduction matters more than class alone (p1224–1225).

**Drugs & cautions**
- Thiazide-type diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs and calcium-channel antagonists are options; ACE inhibitors/ARBs are often used with diabetes or heart failure (p1225).
- Beta blockers are not initial therapy solely for hypertension in older adults without a compelling indication (p1225).
- Monitor supine and upright pressure: treatment-related hypotension may present as weakness or fatigue rather than classic dizziness (p1223).

**Exam traps**
- If seated systolic pressure cannot fall below **130 mm Hg** without postural hypotension, consider a standing-pressure-based target (p1223).
- SPRINT's dementia reduction was not statistically significant, although mild cognitive impairment was reduced; do not describe definite dementia prevention (p1223).
- Individual treatment goals still matter even when trials demonstrate benefit in community-dwelling frail subgroups (p1223).
