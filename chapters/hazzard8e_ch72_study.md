# Chapter 72 Study: Ethical Issues

## Summary

**Recognizing ethical conflict**
- Ethical dilemmas involve uncertainty or disagreement about the right action, often because stakeholders hold conflicting values (p1097–1098).
- Clarify whether the issue is ethical, legal or another kind of problem; assemble medical facts, patient preferences, others' perspectives and relevant ethics knowledge (p1098).
- Do not assume values from age, diagnosis or background. Shared decisions combine the patient's goals with clinician information and recommendations (p1098).

**Capacity & surrogate decisions**
- Capacity is assessed for the specific decision. A capacitated patient may choose an option the team or family considers unsafe (p1097, p1099).
- When a patient chooses home despite risk, explore support and risk reduction rather than treating disagreement as incapacity (p1099).
- If capacity is lacking, involve the appropriate surrogate. Known wishes guide substituted judgment; when wishes are unknown, weigh benefits, burdens and risks in the patient's best interests (p1102–1103).
- Surrogate decision-making can be stressful; recommendations should help the surrogate represent the patient rather than merely choose from an unexplained menu (p1098, p1102–1103).

**Disclosure & end-of-life care**
- Offer the opportunity to receive diagnostic information, while asking how much the patient wants to know. A family's request for nondisclosure should prompt exploration of the patient's preferences (p1101).
- Withholding and withdrawing life-prolonging treatment are ethically equivalent, though withdrawal may be psychologically harder (p1103).
- Palliative sedation is a last-resort response to refractory distress in terminal illness with comfort-focused goals, after interdisciplinary evaluation (p1105).
- Sedation should be proportionate to the duration of symptoms and not expected to shorten life; existential suffering alone remains controversial and needs specialist assessment (p1105).

**Safety, systems & exam traps**
- Driving concerns involve confidentiality, patient safety and public safety; the chapter notes that reporting obligations vary by US state (p1098).
- Moral distress occurs when clinicians feel compelled to provide care they believe wrong; unresolved conflict can lead to burnout (p1097).
- Under resource scarcity, use transparent allocation principles and triage committees; the chapter rejects excluding patients solely by age (p1098–1109).
- Dementia does not automatically settle capacity, disclosure preferences or treatment goals; these require individualized assessment (p1099, p1101–1103).
