## Recognizing cognitive decline in daily life

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-6512e0a92fe1fe2be71f47ee mcq-a1dae018a6c6f55cc5b317e8 mcq-969d468917902ea022b54f50" data-practice-ids="mcq-6d95836c1f971f2123c4f768 mcq-98ec853f03c4ddcd93ba163a mcq-a0fa9b557074f9332ce13a1b mcq-2ef61450ff3feeb769b59483 mcq-a68f3e9d6b27e3350372761f mcq-1c4533a4cd0e8662d06d60b3"></span>

Start with what the patient used to do, what now takes longer and who has had to take over. In mild cognitive impairment (MCI) there is a reported change and objective impairment in at least one domain, judged against age and education. Complex tasks may take longer or require a list, but the patient remains independent. Repeated medication or financial errors that require another person to take responsibility change the functional diagnosis. June 2026 Q36 tested this distinction (p895–896, Tables 59-1 and 59-2).

A single screening score gives a level of performance. Interviewing the patient and someone who knows them establishes whether that level represents decline. An academic may start from a high baseline, and sensory loss or limited schooling may affect performance. June 2026 Q82 uses Harrison's example of abstract thinking: identify the relationship between two objects. The chapter describes the role of a MoCA in a wider assessment (p901–906).

A gradual decline over months or years supports probable Alzheimer dementia. Acute fluctuation calls for evaluation of delirium and its cause, followed by reassessment of cognition after recovery. Depression can coexist with AD and may present with irritability, altered appetite or sleep. Ask about previous function and the time course before attributing a change to dementia (p895, 900–905).

Approximately 12% to 15% of people with MCI progress to dementia each year in the studies cited. This is a group rate that warrants follow-up. June 2026 Q73 instead uses Jack et al., Table 6. Stage 2 allows symptoms with objective performance in the expected range. Stage 3 requires objectively impaired performance with independence preserved. The final key accepts both: the vignette supplies no normative context for its MoCA score (p907 and the exam's supplied article 4).

## Reading the pattern and the biomarkers together

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-4dff765078655193f686e847 mcq-e571d83442c0d0404eb7e495 mcq-50913261c9d08f2875dc46ce mcq-892f3ac99385c931f627310d mcq-5df16a1d0d61c7ad67f08df6 mcq-a27bfbe9359d6d1da40dbdc6" data-practice-ids="mcq-910c7b9e34f0328d4580d477"></span>

Alzheimer disease usually begins with progressive loss of memory for recent events. Frontotemporal dementia can instead cause early language dysfunction with relative sparing of memory, or early disinhibition, loss of empathy and repetitive behavior. These two presentations explain the different FTD answers in June 2023 Q33 and September 2024 Q15 (p908, Table 59-5).

Fluctuating attention, recurrent formed visual hallucinations and spontaneous parkinsonism favor Lewy body disease. A vascular lesion can instead produce an abrupt or stepwise change or a subcortical profile with slowed processing and poor attention. Relate the timing, deficits and imaging to each other. A lacune on a scan alone does not establish vascular dementia or lost independence. Under the VasCog criteria in June 2026 Q56 and Q65, vascular and Alzheimer contributions may both be present. The infarcts support the vascular component, while the type of tau assay and the APOE genotype matter to a biological attribution (p895, 908; supplied article 2, Boxes 2–3).

Clinical diagnosis describes the syndrome, while biomarkers can identify pathology. Hazzard's 2018 research framework groups amyloid, pathological tau and evidence of neuronal injury as AT(N). Injury may have causes other than AD. June 2026 Q23 instead uses the later Jack criteria: an accurate plasma p-tau217 test is a Core 1 marker, tau PET is Core 2, and structural atrophy or neurofilament light is less specific. The IWG article used in Q43 reserves presymptomatic AD for a cognitively normal person with a very high lifetime risk, such as the person with Down syndrome in the vignette (p908–910; supplied articles 3 and 4).

June 2022 Q36 accepts both the specified CSF finding and chronic ischemic MRI changes. The cognitive history does not establish either result by itself. CSF amyloid deposition is generally associated with lower Aβ42 or a lower Aβ42 to Aβ40 ratio, while AD-related phosphorylated tau rises. MRI can identify a coexisting vascular contribution (p906, 908–910).

## Interpreting family history, age and risk

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-187eadc07d0f6464af5a1c18 mcq-6983d574baa602f3d7b1fc45 mcq-68133d1fcd7810143b5b5659" data-practice-ids="mcq-f1b068fb860ad775dde65af8"></span>

Age is the strongest established risk factor discussed in Hazzard. A family with younger-onset disease in successive generations raises a different question: a pathogenic mutation in APP, PSEN1 or PSEN2 can cause autosomal dominant AD. Among the familial mutations grouped in the chapter, PSEN1 accounts for about 78%. APP maps to chromosome 21, which also explains the relevance of APP dosage in Down syndrome. September 2024 Q10 officially cites p896. The mutation distribution that answers it is on p897–898 (p894–895, 897–898).

APOE on chromosome 19 modifies susceptibility to late-onset AD. The ε4 allele increases risk in a dose-dependent fashion, while ε2 is relatively protective. An ε4 allele does not establish a diagnosis or make onset inevitable. May 2024 Q71 contrasts a susceptibility gene with the disease-causing mutations seen in some younger-onset families (p895, 897–898).

## Choosing symptomatic therapy and recognizing toxicity

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-432e69e7294ba1333bbb87f6 mcq-4de2f99d12c0f96618a8056a mcq-d867a75a5001c634eaea9903 mcq-dfce84d3a202c484afe8b6bc mcq-f7dd1def9d494cc738077131 mcq-333c6cc4d497b022ec3bf4c1 mcq-987cf6b109f3fe9f8d976b83" data-practice-ids="mcq-0971fea2ad0a50b32c98cc69 mcq-aa26bdef996a98dd1c649114 mcq-b779bb4d9377055ce33a959e mcq-141fb224311d4c71ea10eacd mcq-08e57c41fba38974834cfcc7 mcq-dcd1c4f9584716e2e478c212"></span>

Cholinesterase inhibitors raise acetylcholine at synapses. A patient with established Alzheimer dementia may stabilize or decline more slowly. Hazzard reports measurable cognitive improvement in 18% to 48% of treated patients, while most have no readily noticeable improvement. These agents have not been shown to delay progression from MCI to dementia. June 2026 Q5 asked what to tell the family, and June 2023 Q59 distinguished treatment of AD or Lewy body dementia from treatment of memory complaints alone (p910–912).

Ask about pauses, fainting, conduction disease and weight loss before titrating. The medication table associates donepezil with insomnia as well as bradycardia, heart block and syncope. A dose taken with food may reduce gastrointestinal effects. Taking it fasting is the false claim in June 2023 Q99. June 2022 Q39 accepts the three cholinesterase inhibitors after appeal, consistent with a class concern about bradycardia. June 2026 Basic Q102 names Chapter 59 in the official list but gives [printed p945–946 in Chapter 61](?chapter=61#p945). The donepezil adverse-effect row relevant to the tracing is on p912 (p911–913, Table 59-7).

Memantine blocks NMDA receptors and is described for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer disease. In a patient unable to tolerate a cholinesterase inhibitor because of gastrointestinal effects or bradycardia, the chapter allows memantine as the initial agent. Headache is listed for memantine. Drug stage, tolerance and symptoms explain the June 2023 and September 2024 answers. Combining both classes has not demonstrated superiority to either alone, and additional evidence is needed for earlier AD. December 2021 Q14 accepted all four options after appeal, including claims that extend beyond these treatment data (p911–914).

## Reducing risk, supporting function and interpreting treatment effects

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d174954cc165e4a4bf4cb9e3 mcq-2a6291bad9a3b4e07c81b32e mcq-be30faa89f4f223edef3ac59 mcq-4bec111cf773419535c13cb7 mcq-26ba0a6e4969ec4edfbd0987" data-practice-ids="mcq-825df84a1ac736a4d39649d1 mcq-41b2b9e9aed4c3e21ea1a031 mcq-7c85e40d5ccc8aaa1481c211 mcq-cae00a287bb5d67f443b04bb"></span>

Midlife hypertension, obesity and physical inactivity are associated with later AD. Shared risk factors can accumulate. In the vascular-risk cohort cited in 2020 Q83, decline was faster particularly among APOE ε4 carriers. A genetic association does not remove opportunities to address smoking, hearing impairment or vascular risk. June 2023 Q56 asks about the largest weighted contribution among the midlife factors offered in the 2020 Lancet Commission: hearing loss. Population-attributable estimates describe a population under assumptions, not an individual response to treating hearing loss (p895–897, 915; Livingston et al., 2020, Table 2).

Intensive blood-pressure treatment in SPRINT-MIND reduced incident MCI, a different endpoint from a conclusive reduction in dementia. The 2020 Commission emphasizes treating hypertension, while the review cited in June 2026 Q16 distinguishes observational associations from randomized proof that modifying a single exposure prevents dementia. June 2025 Q61's accepted option emphasizes inherited risk. The cited 2024 Commission states that overall risk remains modifiable irrespective of APOE status (p915; Livingston et al., 2024, p573; supplied article 5).

Select activities the person can still perform and enjoys, adapting the physical and cognitive demands to their ability. Frustration with a task that depends on a lost skill is a reason to change the task. Exercise, social activity and cognitive engagement may offer benefit, while respite and support address caregiver strain (p915).

Amyloid removal and a clinically important change in function are distinct endpoints. Hazzard describes variable clinical findings and imaging abnormalities with aducanumab. June 2025 Q15 asks about a later lecanemab trial. At 18 months both group means worsened on CDR-SB, by 1.21 points with lecanemab and 1.66 with placebo. The treatment difference represents slower decline. It does not establish that an individual will improve from baseline (p911, 914; van Dyck et al., Table 2).

## Care in advanced dementia and hospice assessment

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-99b3495842400fac1e566ca7 mcq-7f8661f001992e760b345025" data-practice-ids="mcq-14495b2b993ed80a455dcf47 mcq-afc59f4904977cff5ceae519 mcq-22ad374d60860dc3b36fced5"></span>

Progressive dysphagia, poor intake and infection are common in advanced disease. Discuss prognosis and the patient's goals with the caregiver. In Mitchell's advanced dementia article, FAST 7c entails loss of independent ambulation. Hospice guidelines combine advanced functional decline with at least one qualifying complication. In the circumstances specified in its table, substantial weight loss or serum albumin below 2.5 g/dL can document impaired nutrition. These criteria estimate eligibility and cannot predict precisely whether an individual will die within six months. The September 2024 question supplies the advanced stage and asks which complication qualifies. May 2024 Q33 names Mitchell in its stem but the sheet gives X for chapter and page (p916; Mitchell, Table 1).

When pneumonia occurs, consider relief of suffering and possible prolongation of life separately. Antibiotic treatment was associated with longer survival in an observational study, while greater comfort did not follow. Eating problems and infections are the common complications in June 2022 Q46. Tube feeding has not been shown to extend survival, improve comfort or reduce aspiration in advanced AD. Hand feeding as tolerated and a simplified regimen can align care with comfort (p916; Mitchell, clinical complications).

## Summary

**Definition & epidemiology**
- AD = most common neurodegenerative disorder / cause of dementia in older adults.
- **>6 million Americans**, **>44 million worldwide** have AD or a related dementia (p894).
- Prevalence rises steeply with age: **~3% (65–74y), 17% (75–84y), 32% (85y+)** (p894).
- **Late-onset (sporadic) AD = >95%** of all AD cases (p894); familial (younger-onset, <65y) AD = **1–5%** (p897).
- Median survival from diagnosis to death: **7–10 years** (p901).
- AD is the most common dementia in the United States, accounting for **50–90%** of all dementia cases (p907).

**Genetics & risk factors**
- Familial AD genes: *APP*, *PSEN1*, *PSEN2*. **PSEN1 = 78%** of familial AD mutations (most common) (p897); familial AD onset typically **late 40s–early 50s** (p898).
- *APOE* ε4: **one copy → 4× risk, two copies → 12× risk** of AD (p895). Framingham: **55%** of ε4 homozygotes vs **27%** heterozygotes vs **9%** noncarriers developed AD by age 85 (p895). ε2 allele is protective.
- Vascular risk factors (high midlife cholesterol/BP, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, obesity, inactivity) all raise AD risk. Midlife obesity RR **1.60** (95% CI 1.34–1.92); midlife physical inactivity RR **1.82** (95% CI 1.19–2.78) (p896).
- **About 1/3 of AD cases worldwide** might be attributable to potentially modifiable risk factors (p896).
- TBI: WWII veterans with moderate TBI had **2×** AD risk, severe TBI with loss of consciousness **4×** (p896).
- Late-life depression history → AD risk **OR 1.65** (95% CI 1.42–1.92) (p896); >30% of AD patients develop depression during the illness (may be the first symptom).
- Low educational attainment: pooled **RR 1.80** (95% CI 1.45–2.27) for AD (p897).

**Pathophysiology (brief)**
- Hallmarks: amyloid (Aβ) plaques + neurofibrillary tangles (tau); neuritic plaques = subset with dystrophic neurites.
- Cerebral amyloid angiopathy co-occurs in **80–90%** of AD patients (sporadic CAA) — can cause lobar hemorrhage/microbleeds (p898).
- 2012 NIA-AA neuropathologic "ABC score" (**A**myloid/**B**raak/**C**ERAD) grades autopsy findings as none/low/intermediate/high (p899).
- **10–30%** of clinically diagnosed AD-dementia patients lack amyloid/tau pathology at autopsy — clinical diagnosis and biological (autopsy) diagnosis are not the same thing (p908). 2018 AT(N) framework reframes AD as a biological (amyloid+tau), not purely clinical, diagnosis.

**Clinical presentation**
- Most common: **amnestic presentation** — progressive recent memory loss, often unrecognized by the patient (p900).
- Nonamnestic presentations (language, visuospatial, executive dysfunction) are more often missed/delayed in diagnosis.
- Late stages: falls, dysphagia, incontinence, immobility; death commonly from pneumonia, urosepsis, dehydration, pressure sores.

**Evaluation**
- NIA-AA dementia criteria (Table 59-1): decline from previous level that interferes with function, not explained by delirium or major psychiatric disorder; history from patient and a knowledgeable informant + objective cognitive assessment; impairment involves at least two cognitive or behavioral domains (p895).
- NIA-AA MCI criteria (Table 59-2): concern about change noted by the patient, an informant or a clinician; lower performance in ≥1 domain; **preserved independence in functional abilities**; not dementia (p896).
- Reversible-cause workup: meds (anticholinergic/sedating), depression, hearing loss, OSA, alcohol, vascular disease, delirium.
- Labs: B12, folate, TSH, CBC, electrolytes, LFTs, 25-OH vitamin D; HIV/syphilis if atypical; thiamine if heavy alcohol use (p906).
- Brain CT or MRI recommended in patients with documented cognitive impairment (p906).
- **MCI noted in up to 15–20%** of older adults; progresses to dementia at **~12–15%/year** (p907).

**Drugs**
- **AChEIs** (mainstay): **donepezil (Aricept)**, **galantamine (Razadyne)**, **rivastigmine (Exelon)** — the text says start low with dose increases approximately every 2 months (p911), while Table 59-7 lists drug-specific steps: donepezil **4–6 wk** before 5→10 mg (the 10→23 mg step after **3 months** on 10 mg) (p912), galantamine **≥4 wk** per step (p912), rivastigmine **≥2 wk** per oral step or **4–6 wk** for the patch (p912); **18–48%** show cognitive improvement, most others just have slowed decline (p911). Cautions: GI upset (take with food); bradycardia/AV block/syncope/weight loss are triggers to deprescribe.
- **Memantine (Namenda)** — NMDA-receptor antagonist, approved for **moderate-to-severe AD**; combo with an AChEI has not been shown to be superior to either drug alone (p911).
- **Aducanumab** — FDA accelerated approval **June 2021** (p911); anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody, **FDA-labeled only for MCI due to AD and mild AD** (not moderate-to-severe AD) (Table 59-7, p914). One trial: **22%** reduced cognitive decline (CDR-SB) over **18 months**; no statistically significant benefit in the other trial. ARIA-E/ARIA-H in **34–36%** of high-dose patients, **~80%** of those asymptomatic (p911).

**Prevention, safety, and end-of-life**
- **SPRINT-MIND**: SBP goal **120 mmHg** (vs 140) → **19%** reduction in incident MCI (p915).
- Nonpharmacologic care: exercise, cognitive/social stimulation, safety review (driving, firearms, stove), caregiver support/respite.
- Behavioral symptoms: nonpharmacologic management first-line (see Ch. 60 for detail).
- **Feeding tubes are not recommended** in advanced AD — they do not prolong survival or improve comfort, and have not been shown to reduce pressure sores, infection, or aspiration risk (p916).
