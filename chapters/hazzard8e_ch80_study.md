## Airflow, trapped air and gas transfer

**Assessment**

- Spirometry measures exhaled flow and volume; a lower FEV1/FVC ratio indicates greater obstruction. Spirometry alone cannot measure total lung volume (p1230).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-122d9fc74517c768b3d882c2" data-practice-ids="mcq-5687d58f50abb0a5affaee3d" data-exam-read-page="1231" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

- With aging, FEV1 falls more than FVC; the ratio declines. Opposing lung and chest-wall changes leave total lung capacity relatively constant (p1231).

From a maximal inspiration the patient blows out as hard and completely as possible. FEV1 is the volume out in the first second. FVC is the volume of the entire forced breath. Weaker recoil and earlier airway closure disproportionately reduce early emptying. A smaller fraction of the breath therefore leaves in the first second.

> Current practice (not the book): Compare the patient's ratio with its age-appropriate lower limit and interpret it alongside symptoms and exposures before diagnosing airway disease. [ERS/ATS lung function interpretation standard, Table 1](https://doi.org/10.1183/13993003.01499-2021).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e2505429368dfde81cea15ac" data-exam-read-page="1231" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Residual volume (RV) is what remains after the hardest possible expiration. When the lung loses recoil, small airways close earlier and trap gas. RV rises and there is correspondingly less air available for the vital capacity maneuver. Functional residual capacity (FRC) is measured at the end of quiet breathing. A loss of inward recoil can shift that resting volume upward, the choice in June 2022 Q40. Full inflation is a third condition: reduced inward recoil favors a higher TLC, while vertebral height loss and stiffer rib joints restrict expansion (Ch 80 pp1230–1231; Ch 81 p1237 on increased RV and FRC).

> Current practice (not the book): The size and detection of the FRC change vary by population. [Quiet and maximal expiration in older and younger adults, Table II](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/104825).

**Exam traps**

- Aging does not simply reduce every lung volume: total lung capacity remains relatively constant while airflow and diffusion decline (p1231).

Spirometry cannot measure the air that never leaves the lungs. Gas dilution or body plethysmography is needed for static lung volumes. A low FVC alone therefore cannot confirm restriction. Measure TLC when the distinction matters (pp1230–1231).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b51ad3fe1826ffb8741047b1" data-exam-read-page="1231" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

- Diffusing capacity declines with capillary loss and ventilation–perfusion mismatch (p1231).

DLCO estimates gas transfer using a small inhaled concentration of carbon monoxide and its uptake into blood. Loss of pulmonary capillary surface reduces uptake. Differences in regional ventilation and perfusion further impair efficient gas exchange. A healthy older person may have a lower expected DLCO than a young adult. A low TLC with a low DLCO and progressive exertional symptoms suggests parenchymal disease (p1231).

## Elastin, airway clearance and immune defense

**Aging & physiology**

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b51ad3fe1826ffb8741047b1" data-exam-read-page="1230" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

- Fewer naive T cells, impaired ciliary clearance and altered extracellular matrix increase vulnerability to infection and stress (p1230).

Less elastin and proportionately more collagen in the extracellular matrix reduce the ability of lung tissue to return to its previous shape. In the conducting airways, less effective ciliary beating slows the transport of mucus and inhaled particles. These are separate defects: recoil helps empty the lungs, while airway cilia move secretions toward the pharynx (p1230).

Repeated antigen exposure expands the pool of memory T cells. As naïve T cells become fewer, responses to unfamiliar antigens are less effective. Less efficient clearance can coexist with greater innate inflammation and oxidative injury. This explains why June 2026 Q42 pairs the true change in elastin with distractors that reverse the changes in memory cells, ciliary movement and diffusion (pp1230–1231).

- Senescence limits tissue repair; a persistent senescence-associated secretory phenotype promotes inflammation and fibrosis (p1230).

Senescent cells stop dividing and resist apoptosis. A short-lived secretory response can help wound healing, but persistent signaling sustains inflammation and fibrosis. Oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, DNA damage and telomere shortening all favor senescence. Accelerated cell turnover or an inherited short-telomere syndrome can further limit the reserve for tissue repair (pp1229–1230).

## Recognizing and treating idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

- IPF is more common after **60**; consider it with insidious progressive exertional dyspnea, cough and basal fine crackles (p1231).

- IPF usually reduces total lung capacity and diffusing capacity. Chest CT may show the usual interstitial pneumonia pattern: reticulation, honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis (p1231).

In December 2021 Q34, a 70-year-old lifelong nonsmoker develops slowly progressive dyspnea, fine bilateral crackles and hypoxemia with a normal echocardiogram. The examination shows a frontal chest radiograph with bilateral interstitial markings. Ask about drugs, occupational exposures and inhaled antigens before calling fibrosis idiopathic. High-resolution CT characterizes the pattern. In Figure 80-2, the basal, subpleural honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis reflect architectural distortion from fibrosis (pp1231–1232).

**Management & drugs**

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-d8222a690714331a4dd2ad76" data-exam-read-page="1231" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

- Refer suspected or confirmed IPF to pulmonology. Nintedanib and pirfenidone slow progression of restriction but do not reverse existing scarring; nausea and diarrhea may limit treatment (p1231).

The benefit of nintedanib is a slower loss of FVC during follow-up. It does not imply that dyspnea will promptly improve or that existing scar disappears. Chronic steroids have no established survival benefit in stable IPF (p1231). Preventive antibiotics do not treat fibrosis.

> Current practice (not the book): Oxygen corrects hypoxemia, but the mortality evidence for long-term oxygen in severely hypoxemic COPD cannot simply be applied to IPF. [ATS guideline on home oxygen, Question 4](https://doi.org/10.1164/rccm.202009-3608ST).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-86b262840f3c7de29023078e" data-exam-read-page="1231" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

- Antifibrotic initiation and continuation require individual decisions; immediate symptom or quality-of-life improvement may not occur. The chapter does not specify their doses (p1231).

The chapter lists gastrointestinal adverse effects, including nausea and diarrhea, with antifibrotic therapy (p1231).

> Current practice (not the book): Diarrhea is especially common with nintedanib and nausea with pirfenidone. At follow-up ask about food and fluid intake, weight loss and missed doses. Many patients can continue with supportive measures or a dose adjustment, although some need to stop. June 2025 Basic Q119 asks for recognizable adverse effects. A promise that side effects will be minor offers less information to the individual patient. [TSANZ position statement, adverse effects of antifibrotic therapy](https://doi.org/10.1111/resp.14656).

- IPF is primarily fibrotic: the chapter centers treatment on antifibrotics rather than chronic immunosuppression; consider transplant evaluation for suitable patients (p1231).

Progression can continue despite treatment, and an abrupt deterioration can be life-threatening. Look for precipitants such as infection or pulmonary embolism. Consider transplant assessment early enough for an eligible patient to undergo evaluation. The chapter describes median survival after diagnosis in terms of a few years, with substantial variation between individuals (p1231).

> Current practice (not the book): **United States drug update.** The US label now also lists nerandomilast, a phosphodiesterase 4 inhibitor, for adults with IPF. FIBRONEER-IPF compared the change in FVC after 52 weeks. The adjusted mean loss was 106 mL with the 18 mg twice-daily regimen and 170 mL with placebo. The trial shows slower loss of capacity. [US prescribing information, August 2026, sections 1.1 and 14.1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=fa1b14c6-957f-d326-5099-911bfe33e391).

## Summary

- Lung function reflects lung tissue, airways, respiratory muscles, chest wall and cardiovascular function; normal decline need not reach symptomatic disease (p1229).

Lung development establishes peak reserve in young adulthood. A lower peak or a faster subsequent decline brings a person to the threshold for exertional symptoms sooner. Figure 80-1 distinguishes these trajectories. New dyspnea warrants evaluation of the airways, parenchyma, muscles, chest wall and cardiovascular system (pp1229–1230).

In severe COVID-19, impaired adaptive clearance may coexist with excessive innate inflammation. Diffuse alveolar damage can progress to respiratory failure, disordered coagulation and multiorgan dysfunction. The chapter uses this infection to connect a less effective response to a new antigen with a more injurious inflammatory response (pp1231–1232).

- Avoid tobacco and pollution; address vaccination, healthy weight, cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis. Consider oxygen supplementation if hypoxic (p1229, p1232–1233).

Truncal obesity adds to the work of the diaphragm. Vertebral fractures can shorten the thorax and further limit expansion. Manage heart failure or other cardiac contributors to exertional symptoms. The chapter discusses influenza, pneumococcal disease and COVID-19 as preventable causes of additional lung injury (pp1232–1233).

- Consider pulmonary/cardiac rehabilitation after acute illness in patients with chronic cardiopulmonary disease; recovery may otherwise be slow and incomplete (p1233).
