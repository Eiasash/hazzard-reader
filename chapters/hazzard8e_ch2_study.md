# Chapter 2 Study: Demography and Epidemiology

> Excluded from the IMA required reading list (P005-2026); not cited in the 2020-2026 past papers.

## Summary

**Population aging and its measurement**
- Improved survival and declining birth rates reshape age distributions; population aging differs across countries and historical periods (p23-25).
- Interpret remaining life expectancy at a specified age separately from life expectancy at birth; survival curves reflect changing mortality across the lifespan (p25-26).
- Distinguish age effects, period effects shared during an event, and birth-cohort effects from shared historical exposure (p26-27).
- The dependency ratio compares conventional working/nonworking age groups; it does not establish an individual's actual work or economic dependence (p27).
- US older populations are becoming more diverse. Aggregate survival gains coexist with racial, ethnic and socioeconomic differences in morbidity and disability (p27).

**Disease burden, function and cognition**
- Hypertension is the most common chronic condition among older Medicare beneficiaries; chronic diseases commonly coexist rather than appearing in isolation (p29-31).
- In **2018**, multiple chronic conditions affected **64%** of adults **65 or older**, versus **33%** at **45-64**. Comorbidity relates to an index disease; multimorbidity describes coexisting conditions (p31).
- Sensory loss contributes to impaired function and independence; utilization and mortality statistics alone do not describe the full burden (p32-33).
- Disability concerns difficulty with daily activities; ADLs capture basic self-care, IADLs more complex independent living. Performance tests add information (p33-34).
- Disablement links pathology, impairment, functional limitation and disability; personal and environmental factors modify the pathway. Disability may develop gradually or abruptly and can improve (p36-37).
- Cognitive and physical function interact. Screening instruments differ by domain and sensitivity; education and ceiling effects can complicate interpretation (p40).

**Modifiable context**
- Biomarkers may reflect disease processes or exposures, but clinical interpretation requires context rather than treating every age association as causation (p40-42).
- Physical activity, smoking, alcohol, nutrition and other behaviors influence later-life health; socioeconomic resources shape opportunities to adopt them (p42-43).
- Marginalization, discrimination, neighborhood design and social connections influence disability and survival. Address both individual risks and environmental barriers to participation (p43-44).

### Reading survival and disability curves

Rectangularization describes a change in the shape of the survival curve as more people survive to advanced age. Compression of morbidity asks when disease or disability begins relative to death. The chapter describes evidence for late disability among many centenarians, while recognizing continued debate about compression in the population as a whole (p25–26).

A population can age through better survival, fewer births or both. The US demographic dependency ratio rose from **49 in 2010** to **54 in 2019**. The calculation groups children and older adults together relative to the working-age population. Its economic interpretation is limited because some older adults work and because assets and transfers also matter (p27–28).

Measure the level at which a difficulty occurs. Impaired balance or slow gait can precede difficulty walking outdoors, which in turn can prevent independent shopping. The Short Physical Performance Battery samples standing balance, a **4-meter** walk and chair stands. Functional limitations can emerge abruptly after stroke or accumulate gradually. Repeated assessment can show recovery that a single disability count would miss (p33–34, p36–37).

### Population growth, population aging and remaining survival

Begin with the denominator. An increase in the number of older people can occur in a growing population without an increase in their proportion. Population aging means that the age distribution itself has shifted. Declining fertility narrows the base of the age distribution, while improved survival allows more members of earlier birth cohorts to reach later ages. Table 2-1 combines observed and projected US counts: the share aged **65 or older** rose from **4.1%** around the start of the twentieth century to about **12%** around the start of the next. Observed past change and a projection built from assumptions should not be reported as the same kind of evidence (p23–24).

Figure 2-1 compares older population shares across countries and projected dates. Countries can reach a similar older share by different routes and at different speeds. Rapid reductions in mortality and fertility can allow a transition within a few generations, leaving less time to develop long-term support and health services. The absolute number of older adults depends on population size as well as the percentage. A country with a relatively small older share may therefore need services for more older people than a much smaller country whose percentage is higher (p23–25).

Figure 2-2 illustrates the shape of survival curves. When many early deaths are prevented, a larger proportion of a birth cohort survives into late life and deaths become more concentrated at older ages. This rectangularization of survival does not specify when chronic illness or disability begins. Compression of morbidity would require onset to move closer to death, shortening the period spent ill or disabled. It is possible to live longer while also living longer with disease, so compare disease-free or disability-free time when answering a question about healthy life expectancy (p25–26).

Life expectancy at a given age is conditional on having reached that age. In Table 2-2's US **2017** figures, the all-race estimates at birth were **76 years for males** and **81 years for females**. At **65**, remaining life expectancy was **18 years for males** and **21 years for females**; at **85**, the corresponding values were **5.9 and 7 years**. Subtracting eighty-five from life expectancy at birth would give a nonsensical answer because the older person has already survived risks that contributed to the estimate at birth. These are population averages that vary with health and other circumstances (p25).

Extreme longevity requires accurate age documentation and an appropriate comparison cohort. The chapter describes geographic clusters of unusually long-lived people but notes that proposed explanations such as activity, diet and social connection are not validated causal accounts of a particular cluster. In the New England centenarian study, siblings of centenarians had **8–17 times** the likelihood of reaching **100** compared with others from the same birth cohort. Family aggregation can motivate genetic research but does not exclude shared environment, and determinants of exceptional survival need not explain most variation in ordinary lifespan (p26).

### Age, historical period and generation

Figure 2-3 separates age effects from period and birth-cohort effects. An age effect describes a change associated with getting older. A period effect arises when an event or change in care affects people of many ages at roughly the same calendar time. A cohort effect follows people born during a particular period who shared an exposure or opportunity. A single comparison of people aged forty and eighty cannot uniquely assign the difference to biological aging, because they also belong to different cohorts. Repeated observations across ages, dates and cohorts provide a stronger basis for interpretation (p26–27).

Figures 2-4 and 2-5 show that the composition of the older US population changes as well as its size. The first plots men per **100 women** across older age ranges, while the second shows projected changes in racial and ethnic composition. A sex ratio is not a survival probability, and the percentage of older people belonging to a demographic group is not that group's risk of disease. Differential survival, migration, cohort size and socioeconomic opportunity can all affect the population from which disease and disability data are drawn (p27–28).

Counts of people in conventional working-age and dependent-age bands are useful for planning but do not directly measure financial dependence. Some older people remain employed, provide unpaid care or draw on accumulated resources. Some people in the conventionally productive age range are not employed. Retirement patterns vary by sex, cohort and economic conditions, while widowhood and living arrangements affect the availability of support. Ask what the dependency ratio actually counted before using it to make a claim about who supports whom in a household (p27–28).

### Rates, counts and rankings in mortality and morbidity

Table 2-3 gives several answers to the question “How large is heart disease as a cause of death?” Among US adults aged **65 or older** in **2017**, the reported rate was **1,021 deaths per 100,000 population**, accounting for **25% of deaths** in that age group. For malignancies the corresponding figures were **841 per 100,000** and **21% of deaths**. The rate uses everyone in the specified population as its denominator; the proportion uses only those who died. A cause can represent a higher share of deaths because another cause became less common, even when its own rate is unchanged (p29).

Figure 2-6 separates age-specific rates for leading causes rather than assigning the all-older-adult rate to each age band. Figure 2-7 adds calendar time, sex and age for cancer mortality. An increase in total deaths can accompany declining age-specific mortality when the number of people surviving to high-risk ages rises. For comparisons across places or eras with different population structures, state whether the rates are crude, age specific or adjusted. A ranking alone does not reveal whether the absolute risk in the group changed (p29–30).

For chronic disease, distinguish incidence from prevalence and duration. New cases add to prevalence, and death or recovery removes cases. Effective treatment that prolongs survival after diagnosis can raise the number living with disease even if incidence falls. Conversely, low recorded prevalence can reflect failure to recognize disease, differences in coding or exclusion of institutionalized people. Table 2-4 describes conditions per **100 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 or older** in the specified year, with hypertension the commonest. The table describes that covered population, not every older adult worldwide (p29–31).

Multimorbidity counts coexisting chronic conditions without designating one primary illness. Comorbidity is defined in relation to an index disease. In the chapter's **2018** comparison, **64%** of adults aged **65 or older** had multiple chronic conditions compared with **33%** of those aged **45–64**. Figure 2-8 follows accumulation over time across racial and ethnic groups. Observed differences raise questions about lifelong exposures, access and survival; they are not proof that a group identity itself produces a particular trajectory. The number of diagnoses also does not specify their severity or functional consequences (p31).

### What service and prescription data can and cannot show

Figure 2-9 shows prescription use over a defined recent period by age. The chapter reports that about **two-thirds** of adults aged **65 or older** used at least **three drugs**, and **40%** used at least **five**. Medication count reflects disease burden and care processes and can flag interaction or treatment burden, but does not by itself demonstrate inappropriate prescribing. It also matters whether a survey counts prescriptions dispensed, medicines reported as taken or all products actually used. Those measures need not identify the same people (p31–32).

Table 2-5 reports visits, not unique patients. In the **2016** US data, people aged **75 or older** had **547 outpatient visits per 100 people per year**, and **54% of visits** were for chronic problems. This does not imply that 547 percent of patients visited or that every patient had the same frequency. A subgroup with very frequent visits can increase the average while another subgroup faces serious access barriers. Table 2-6 uses the first-listed diagnosis for hospitalizations, so it does not enumerate every disease contributing to each admission (p32–33).

Figures 2-10 and 2-11 deal with different measures. The cardiovascular figure plots prevalent heart failure, stroke and coronary disease by age and sex. The cancer figure shows incidence rates for particular cancers in men and women by age. High prevalence may reflect many long-term survivors, whereas a high incidence rate refers to new events over time. Sensory impairments show why neither death nor hospitalization captures the full clinical burden: untreated hearing or visual loss can restrict communication, mobility and participation while never becoming the first-listed reason for hospital admission (p32–35).

### Measuring function and following transitions

Ask precisely what the respondent was asked. Having difficulty bathing, needing someone to assist with bathing and not attempting to bathe are related but distinct states. Instrumental tasks such as shopping, managing medicine and handling money make cognitive and environmental demands beyond those of basic self-care. A person may report no problem shopping because someone else has always done it. Pair self-report with direct performance when feasible and learn what help or equipment the person already uses (p33–35).

The Short Physical Performance Battery samples standing balance, usual walking across **4 meters** and repeated chair rises. These tasks assess capacity under test conditions rather than actual participation at home. A person who walks adequately along a corridor may still be unable to shop if streets are unsafe or public transportation inaccessible. In the opposite direction, an aid or an adapted environment can preserve daily independence despite low measured capacity. Describe impairment, task limitation and reliance on assistance separately (p33–34, p36–37).

Figure 2-12 compares disability status across Medicare beneficiaries by age, sex and racial or ethnic group and includes institutional care. Figure 2-13 separates prevalent mobility disability from incidence in people who were initially nondisabled and from subsequent transitions. These denominators matter. Higher prevalence can result from more new cases, a lower probability of recovery, or survival with disability for a longer interval. A cross-sectional survey cannot reveal which of these pathways explains the difference (p34–36).

In one cohort with initial impairment, **2 years** later **4% had died**, **9% had declined further**, **50% remained impaired** and **37% had recovered**. Thus a disability count is a snapshot of movement into, through and out of impaired states. Death is an alternative outcome, not merely a person missing from follow-up. If only survivors are examined, the apparent probability of recovery will be conditional on surviving, and may differ from the probability the original patient or cohort would want to know (p36–37).

The disablement pathway moves from disease or injury to an impairment, an altered capacity to perform a task and potentially a restriction in everyday activities. Both the person and the environment modify each step. A stroke can produce an abrupt change; accumulating disease and reduced reserve can produce gradual loss. Figure 2-14 follows contrasting patterns of disability near death and shows why the approach to a medically similar endpoint need not look alike. Planning for a person with longstanding severe dependence differs from planning for someone who had little disability until a final rapid decline (p36–37).

Figure 2-15 relates the number of chronic conditions to predicted limitations across age and sex groups. It illustrates aggregation of risk rather than a deterministic conversion of one diagnosis into one lost activity. Figure 2-16 then asks how the number of people needing ADL help changes as cohorts age. Even a declining individual probability at a given age can leave more people requiring care if the group reaching that age grows. For services planning, examine both age-specific rates and the projected absolute number needing assistance (p38–39).

### Cognition and biomarkers in population studies

A brief cognitive instrument samples selected domains; results depend on the tasks, education, language, sensory function and severity range represented. A ceiling effect can mask differences among people at a higher level of function. Clinical dementia involves more than a low screening score, and a diagnosis recorded in an administrative database reflects recognition and coding as well as disease. A study using face-to-face assessment can therefore yield a different estimate from a claims analysis without either being a simple arithmetic error (p39–40).

Figure 2-17 pools age- and sex-specific dementia prevalence from European cohorts. The shape of the curve shows the importance of age structure: a sample containing more very old participants can have higher overall prevalence even if each age-specific rate matches another sample. Survival after diagnosis also matters. A cross-sectional difference between women and men is not, on its own, evidence of a difference in incidence. For an individual with fluctuating attention or sensory impairment, a population prevalence does not substitute for assessing the immediate clinical presentation (p39–40).

A biomarker can indicate exposure, a biological response, disease or prognosis. A marker associated with age is not automatically a cause of aging or a validated surrogate for disability or survival. Composite measures combine information across several systems; physiological tests, imaging and molecular profiles capture different aspects of reserve or pathology. An epigenetic clock trained to estimate chronological age can predict what it was trained to predict without proving that a treatment which changes its value prolongs healthy life. Look for longitudinal prediction of meaningful outcomes and independent validation (p40–42).

Exposure measurement can also determine which explanation looks plausible. A blood test captures a particular biological state at the time of collection. A gait test can integrate the consequences of multiple chronic conditions, deconditioning and neurological or sensory impairment. A neighborhood measure may reflect exposures shared by many residents over years. If all three predict later disability, they need not be competitors for the title of “the cause.” They can lie at different stages of the same pathway. Ask whether the study measures a pre-existing risk factor, early manifestation of disease, functional consequence or protective adaptation, and whether repeated measurements demonstrate the proposed order of events (p36–37, p40–44).

### Behavior, socioeconomic resources and environment

Table 2-7 compares behavioral factors in US middle-aged and older groups in **2018**. In the survey, **13%** of those aged **45–64** and **6.3%** of those aged **65 or older** reported everyday smoking; former smoking was **26%** and **40%**, respectively. This is a cross-sectional comparison of survivors. Differences can reflect quitting, cohort history and differential mortality, not a simple within-person effect of age. Survey definitions also matter: any alcohol use in the last month is a different measure from a high weekly quantity or drinking heavily on one occasion (p42).

Education, income, neighborhood resources, discrimination and social networks influence exposure, access to prevention, probability of diagnosis and chances of maintaining function after disease begins. An exercise recommendation is difficult to implement where sidewalks are unsafe or a person has no affordable transport. Conversely accessible housing and practical help can reduce the disability produced by the same physical impairment. Do not reduce an observed socioeconomic difference to individual choices when environments and opportunities differ (p42–44).

When comparing decline across racial or ethnic groups, ask which exposures, social conditions, migration histories and health-care barriers are represented by the categories used. Marginalization and structural disadvantage can operate over the life course, and groups include substantial variation within them. A population association can identify a need for equitable intervention without predicting an individual's abilities or preferences. In a clinical assessment, ask what obstacle actually limits participation; in a population study, define the exposure and denominator clearly enough to distinguish that obstacle from differences in age, case finding and survival (p31, p42–44).

## Exam traps

- Life expectancy at birth and remaining life expectancy after reaching an older age have different denominators (p25–26).
- A large number of people with a disease can reflect longer survival after onset as well as new cases (p23–26).
- A transition into disability may be followed by recovery. Cross-sectional prevalence does not show the direction of change (p36–37).

## Recall drill

**1. Which two demographic changes increase the proportion of older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Declining fertility reduces the relative number of younger people, and improving survival allows more people to reach advanced ages. (p23–25)</details>

**2. What does rectangularization of the survival curve describe?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>More of the population surviving until late life before deaths become concentrated at older ages. (p25)</details>

**3. What would constitute compression of morbidity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A later onset of morbidity or disability relative to the end of life, concentrating the period lived with illness or disability nearer death. (p26)</details>

**4. Why use remaining life expectancy for someone already aged 65?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>That person has survived risks occurring before that age. A figure calculated at birth includes those earlier deaths. (p25–26)</details>

**5. How does a cohort effect differ from a period effect?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A cohort shares exposures associated with its birth period. A period effect reflects an event or circumstance experienced across age groups at a particular time. (p26–27)</details>

**6. Why is the age-based dependency ratio an imperfect estimate of financial burden?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Many people classified by age as dependent still work, and assets and transfers also affect dependence. (p27–28)</details>

**7. Which chronic condition was most common among older Medicare beneficiaries in the chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Hypertension. The distribution derives from a particular covered population, and several conditions can coexist in one person. (p29–31)</details>

**8. How do comorbidity and multimorbidity differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Comorbidity is considered relative to an index disease. Multimorbidity describes multiple conditions without designating one as primary. (p31)</details>

**9. What age contrast in US multiple chronic conditions did the chapter report for 2018?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary><strong>64%</strong> among adults 65 or older, compared with <strong>33%</strong> among adults 45–64. (p31)</details>

**10. Why can shopping reveal difficulty before a person needs help dressing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Shopping is an instrumental activity that combines mobility, planning and community access. Dressing is a basic self-care task. (p33)</details>

**11. What three types of performance does the SPPB sample?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Standing balance, gait speed over <strong>4 meters</strong> and repeated chair stands as a measure of lower-extremity strength. (p34)</details>

**12. How do progressive and catastrophic disability differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>One develops gradually. The other can begin suddenly after an acute event such as a stroke. (p37)</details>

**13. Why repeat functional assessment after an episode of disability?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Disability can improve and recur. Measuring only at onset may wrongly imply permanent dependence. (p36–37)</details>

**14. Why interpret a near-perfect cognitive screening score cautiously?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A ceiling effect can obscure subtle differences. Education, the domains assessed and physical or sensory function affect interpretation. (p39–40)</details>

**15. How can a neighborhood change disability without changing a diagnosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Transport, street design and social resources can facilitate or restrict activity and participation for someone with the same underlying impairment. (p43–44)</details>
