# Chapter 102 Study: Back Pain and Spinal Stenosis

## Summary

### Epidemiology & classification
- Prevalence of back pain in older adults ranges **27% to 86%** depending on population/criteria; Israeli cohort: **44%** of 70-year-olds, **58%** of 77-year-olds; Framingham (ages 68–100): **22%** reported back pain on most days (p1629).
- Second most common reason for a physician visit; annual all-cause costs related to back pain exceed **$350 billion** (p1629).
- Classification: acute **< 4 weeks**, subacute **4–12 weeks**, chronic **> 3 months** (some definitions **> 6 months**); back pain is typically recurrent/episodic, not constant (p1630).
- Recurrence risk factors: female sex, depressive symptoms, **two or more** chronic conditions, self-reported arthritis (p1629).

### Red-flag / systemic causes (must exclude before calling it "nonspecific")
- **Tumor**: gradual, progressive, nonpositional pain, worse at night, lasting **> 4 weeks**, with systemic/constitutional symptoms (p1630–1631).
- **Infection**: can mimic mechanical disc disease; fever **can be absent** in older adults; risk factors include prosthetic valves, indwelling catheters, IV drug use; if osteomyelitis/discitis found, image the **entire neural axis** (cervical, thoracic, lumbar MRI with/without contrast) to rule out noncontiguous infection (p1631).
- **Vertebral compression fracture (VCF)**: most common osteoporotic fracture type; only **30% to 40%** of all VCFs are symptomatic; pain typically resolves in **4–6 weeks**; **> 20%** of patients with a fracture sustain another fracture within **5 years** (Table 102-2, p1631).
- **Osteoporotic sacral (insufficiency) fracture**: usually older women; sacral pressure very painful; **~50%** have a preceding fall; pain resolves spontaneously in **4–6 weeks** (p1631).
- **Sciatica**: pain radiating down one leg below the gluteal fold; in younger adults **50%** have full resolution within **6 weeks**, and abrupt-onset sciatica in older adults has a similarly good natural history; sciatica arising as part of the lumbar spinal stenosis syndrome (gradual progression, shorter and shorter periods of standing/walking) may have a more prolonged, persistent course (p1632).

### Lumbar spinal stenosis
- Congenital stenosis (short pedicles) presents in the **30s–50s**; acquired stenosis is more common, typically **≥ 50 years** (p1632).
- Most common level: **L4-L5**, followed by **L3-L4** and **L5-S1**; lateral recess stenosis at L4-L5 most often causes an **L5 radiculopathy** (p1632).
- Classic pattern: symptoms **worse with extension** (standing, walking, downhill) and **relieved with flexion** (sitting, bending forward, leaning on a cart — "shopping cart sign") (Table 102-3, p1632).
- Sciatic-type leg pain on walking is also part of the classic picture, but it may be **incomplete** (eg, calf pain — "pseudoclaudication") (Table 102-3, p1632).
- Distinguishing test: neurogenic claudication is **worse on treadmill, relieved on bicycle** (spinal flexion increases canal diameter); vascular claudication is bad on **both** (p1636).
- MRI is the standard imaging modality; CT gives more detail than plain films but is a **poor modality for soft tissue**; CT myelography is infrequently used routinely but can help with lumbar deformity (eg, scoliosis) or patients unable to tolerate MRI (p1636).

### Mechanical low back pain & DISH
- A good deal of back pain in younger individuals is probably caused by displacement of the outer annulus fibrosis or inner nucleus pulposus; **herniation of the nucleus pulposus is less common beyond age 55** as the nucleus loses water content (p1633).
- Mechanical LBP: intermittent, sharp, comes on/subsides rapidly, worse going from supine to sitting or with bending; weakness of L4, L5, S1-innervated muscles (Table 102-4, p1633).
- Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH): flowing calcification along **≥ 4 contiguous vertebral bodies**, preserved disc height, no apophyseal ankylosis — often mislabeled as disc degeneration/osteoarthritis; **no therapy needed** (p1633, p1639).

### Evaluation
- Passive hip ROM benchmarks: **40°** abduction before the pelvis tilts, **> 100°** flexion, **50°–60°** external rotation, **20°** internal rotation (p1634).
- L4/L5 tested via hip abduction (gluteus medius), foot dorsiflexion (tibialis anterior), and great toe extension (extensor hallucis longus); L5/S1 tested via hip extension and foot eversion (p1634–1635; Figures 102-1 to 102-3).
- Imaging red flags (Table 102-7, American College of Radiology, p1636): age **> 70**, significant trauma (or milder trauma if age **> 50**), unexplained weight loss/fever, immunosuppression (eg, diabetes), history of cancer, osteoporosis/prolonged glucocorticoid use, progressive/disabling focal neurologic deficits, symptom duration **> 6 weeks**, IV drug use.
- Imaging is a poor discriminator alone: in asymptomatic patients without back pain, only **36%** of lumbar MRIs read as normal — **52%** showed a disc bulge, **27%** a disc protrusion; in a separate asymptomatic cohort **> 60** years old, **36%** had a herniated nucleus pulposus, **21%** spinal stenosis, **93%** degenerative disc disease (p1636).
- Classic epidemiology: Kellgren & Lawrence found disc degeneration in **89%** of men and **57%** of women aged 55–64 (p1636).

### Management
- Etiology-specific: tumor/infection therapy is straightforward, albeit often difficult; management of VCF, lumbar stenosis, and mechanical LBP is less clear (p1638).
- VCF: activity modification, analgesics (acetaminophen, NSAIDs, mild opioids, tramadol); calcitonin (**2- to 4-week** course) gives some additional analgesia in the acute stage; complete bed rest not recommended; avoid long-term bracing (p1638).
- Vertebral augmentation (vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty): evidence is low quality; one nonrandomized kyphoplasty study (pain > 1 year) of **40** treated vs **20** control patients showed fewer new fractures and better pain scores at **1 year**; reserve for persistent severe pain despite conservative therapy (p1638).
- Lumbar spinal stenosis: conservative therapy first (PT, analgesics, ± epidural steroid injection); a **400**-patient RCT found **minimal/no short-term benefit** of epidural steroids plus lidocaine over lidocaine alone; SPORT trial (younger patients, average age in the 40s) showed surgical benefit from 3 months sustained to **2 years**, but by **8-year** follow-up **52%** of the nonoperative arm had crossed over to surgery (p1639).
- Surgical complication rates for lumbar decompression: **1.8% to 18%** (wound infection, durotomy, nerve root injury, vascular complications, epidural hematoma, implant failure, adjacent-segment degeneration, nonunion) (p1639).
- Mechanical LBP: isometric abdominal strengthening, back protection maneuvers, lumbar sacral corsets (avoid prolonged use — disuse/deconditioning); spinal fusion reserved for intractable severe LBP with radiographic progressive deformity, dynamic instability, or pars interarticularis defects (p1639).
- DISH needs no therapy; sacral insufficiency fracture pain resolves in 4–6 weeks with analgesics, and antiresorptive therapy should be started to prevent future fractures; sciatica usually improves without therapy — surgery only if it persists despite conservative trials (p1639–1640).
- Prevention/risk factors for disabling back pain: female sex, multiple comorbidities (including arthritis), depressive symptoms, poor grip strength (marker of global weakness), hip weakness, obesity (p1640).

## Drill

**1. What percentage of vertebral compression fractures are actually symptomatic?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only about 30% to 40% (p1631).

</details>

**2. Roughly what proportion of patients who sustain one vertebral fracture will have another within 5 years?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Over 20% (p1631).

</details>

**3. What historical clue helps differentiate an osteoporotic sacral (insufficiency) fracture, and how often is there a preceding fall?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Persistent low back/groin/buttock pain with marked tenderness on sacral pressure; about 50% of patients had a preceding fall (p1631).

</details>

**4. In older adults, what is a key pitfall in recognizing infectious back pain (eg, discitis/osteomyelitis)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Fever can be absent in older patients with infection, so its absence does not rule it out (p1631).

</details>

**5. What is the most common spinal level for lumbar spinal stenosis, and which nerve root is most often affected?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

L4-L5 most common (followed by L3-L4 and L5-S1); lateral recess stenosis there most often causes an L5 radiculopathy (p1632).

</details>

**6. What position worsens lumbar spinal stenosis symptoms, and what position relieves them?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Extension (standing, walking, walking downhill) worsens symptoms; flexion (sitting, bending forward, leaning on a walker/cart) relieves them — the "shopping cart sign" (p1632).

</details>

**7. How do you distinguish neurogenic claudication from vascular claudication on exercise testing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Neurogenic claudication is worse on a treadmill but relieved on a bicycle (spinal flexion increases canal diameter); vascular claudication remains symptomatic on both (p1636).

</details>

**8. After what age does lumbar disc herniation become less common, and why?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Beyond age 55, because the nucleus pulposus loses water content with aging (p1633).

</details>

**9. What defines diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) radiographically?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Flowing calcification/ossification along the anterolateral aspect of at least 4 contiguous vertebral bodies, with relative preservation of disc height and no apophyseal joint ankylosis (p1633).

</details>

**10. List the passive hip range-of-motion benchmarks used to help distinguish hip from back pathology.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

40 degrees of abduction before the pelvis tilts, more than 100 degrees of flexion, 50–60 degrees of external rotation, and 20 degrees of internal rotation (p1634).

</details>

**11. According to Table 102-7, name at least 5 imaging "red flags" for back pain due to systemic disease.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Age > 70, significant (or milder if age > 50) trauma, unexplained weight loss/fever, immunosuppression (eg, diabetes), history of cancer, osteoporosis/prolonged glucocorticoid use, progressive/disabling focal neurologic deficits, symptom duration > 6 weeks, IV drug use (p1636).

</details>

**12. Why is lumbar spine imaging a poor stand-alone diagnostic tool in older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Abnormalities (disc bulge, disc degeneration, spinal stenosis, annular tears) are extremely common in asymptomatic older adults — eg, 93% degenerative disc disease and 36% herniated nucleus pulposus in asymptomatic adults over 60 — so imaging must be interpreted alongside history/exam (p1636).

</details>

**13. In the epidural steroid injection RCT of 400 older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis, what did adding glucocorticoid to lidocaine achieve?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Minimal or no additional short-term benefit in disability or leg pain compared with lidocaine alone (no placebo arm was included) (p1639).

</details>

**14. In the SPORT trial, what fraction of the nonoperative arm had crossed over to surgery by 8 years?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

52% (p1639).

</details>

**15. What is the reported complication rate range for lumbar decompressive surgery, and name 3 complication types?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1.8% to 18%; complications include wound infection, durotomy, nerve root injury, vascular complications, epidural hematoma, implant failure, adjacent-segment degeneration, and nonunion (p1639).

</details>
