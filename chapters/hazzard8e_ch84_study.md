# Chapter 84 Study: Aging of the Gastrointestinal System and Selected Lower GI Disorders

## Summary

**Aging & investigation**
- Visceral hyposensitivity can hide severe disease: perforation or ischemia may cause little tenderness. Abnormal liver tests should not be dismissed as normal aging (p1299, p1302).
- Age alone does not preclude endoscopy. Use careful preparation and small incremental sedation doses with cardiopulmonary monitoring (p1302).
- Polyethylene-glycol bowel preparation is well tolerated; avoid sodium-phosphate preparations in older adults because of fluid shifts, electrolyte disturbance and phosphate nephropathy (p1302).

**Diarrhea & drugs**
- C. difficile assays require clinical context: PCR can be positive in asymptomatic carriers. Withdraw the precipitating antibiotic if feasible (p1304–1305).
- Initial mild–moderate C. difficile disease: oral vancomycin **125 mg four times daily** or fidaxomicin **200 mg twice daily**, each for **10 days** (p1305).
- Intravenous vancomycin does not adequately reach the colonic lumen and is ineffective for C. difficile colitis (p1305).
- Microscopic colitis may have normal colonoscopy; exclude infection and obtain biopsies. Budesonide **9 mg daily** induces remission; prednisone carries more adverse effects in older adults (p1306).
- New IBD can occur late in life. Exclude infectious, ischemic, malignant and diverticular mimics before labeling ulcerative colitis (pp1306, 1308). In long-standing colonic ulcerative colitis, the book advises surveillance colonoscopy with biopsies **every 1–3 years**, beginning **8–10 years after disease onset** (p1309).

**Ischemia & obstruction**
- Sudden small-bowel ischemia can be catastrophic; chronic disease may cause postprandial pain, food avoidance and weight loss (p1309).
- Colonic ischemia more often produces mild crampy pain and limited rectal bleeding. Severe pain may indicate transmural necrosis (p1310).
- Suspected colonic ischemia warrants CT-based assessment and close monitoring; stabilize, optimize cardiac function and rest the bowel. Antibiotics are used for moderate/severe cases (p1310).
- Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction often follows severe illness, abdominal surgery, or orthopedic surgery of the hip, pelvis or knee; distinguish it from mechanical obstruction. Cecal diameter at least **12 cm** marks megacolon and increased concern for perforation (p1310). Correct reversible contributors, including electrolyte disturbances (p1311).


### Long-standing colitis and postoperative abdominal distention

- The surveillance question concerns chronic inflammation in the colon. Both the extent and persistence of inflammation matter. Crohn colitis can carry a risk comparable to ulcerative colitis. The chapter cites cumulative colorectal cancer risks in UC of **2% after 10 years** and **18% after 30 years**. A patient with long-standing colonic disease is therefore assessed for surveillance even if currently well. A retained rectal stump after subtotal colectomy still needs attention (p1309).
- Marked distention after hip or pelvic surgery, sometimes with surprisingly little pain, can reflect acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome). Exclude a mechanical cause. Radiography may show dilation of the entire colon or mainly the right colon. The cecum's large radius makes it particularly vulnerable to ischemia and perforation as pressure rises (p1310).
- Check electrolytes, including potassium, volume status and oxygenation, and review drugs that impair motility. Provide bowel rest and IV hydration. If dilation persists after supportive measures, the book describes monitored neostigmine or, in selected refractory cases, endoscopic decompression. Mechanical obstruction precludes neostigmine. Bradycardia and recent infarction are among the relative contraindications (p1310–1311).
- Brisk painless hematochezia suggests diverticular bleeding or vascular ectasia. Diverticula commonly occur on the left, yet diverticular bleeding often originates from the right colon. Stabilize hemodynamically significant bleeding first. In contrast, colonic ischemia often causes cramping with bleeding, and severe pain or peritoneal signs raise concern for more extensive injury (p1303, p1310–1311, p1313).

> Current practice (not the book): For an initial nonfulminant C. difficile infection, the focused guideline conditionally favors fidaxomicin when available. Oral vancomycin remains an acceptable alternative. - [SHEA/IDSA focused update, 2021](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/clostridioides-difficile-2021-focused-update/).

### Interpreting abdominal symptoms against aging physiology

An older adult can have a serious abdominal lesion with surprisingly little discomfort. Reduced perception of distention, altered visceral afferent signaling and reduced abdominal wall responses can weaken the clues a clinician normally relies on. Ask about a change in bowel habit, loss of appetite, bleeding, falls in hemoglobin or new dependence. Reconcile these changes with the examination; mild tenderness does not exclude inflammation or compromised bowel. The distinction is particularly important in suspected diverticulitis, ischemia and obstruction, where a late presentation can convert a reversible episode into an emergency (p1299–1303).

Figure 84-1 and Tables 84-1 through 84-3 separate structural changes from preserved function. Some brush-border responses decline, but many adults retain adequate macronutrient absorption. A reduction in absorption of particular vitamins or minerals does not mean that healthy aging inevitably produces malabsorption or iron-deficiency anemia. If iron deficiency develops, look for blood loss or a disease affecting intake or absorption. Changes in enteric neurons, smooth muscle and relaxation can predispose to altered colonic handling of stool or diverticula. Inactivity, illness, diet and drugs may account for more of an individual patient's change than chronological age alone (p1299–1302).

Gut immunity is also compartmentalized. An observed decline in gastrointestinal lymphocyte recruitment or function does not mean every component of mucosal immunity disappears. Similarly, a change in hepatic volume or drug clearance need not produce an abnormal routine liver panel. Attribute a new cholestatic or hepatocellular result to the patient's clinical circumstances and investigate it. For a patient who reports a sudden change in bowel habit or unexplained rectal bleeding, consider an obstructing lesion, vascular ectasia, diverticular disease or cancer. Hemorrhoids found during examination need not be the source of significant blood loss (p1300–1302).

### Choosing an investigation and making it tolerable

Table 84-4 shifts the differential for rectal bleeding toward diverticulosis, vascular ectasia and colon cancer in an older patient. With a new change in bowel habit, consider an obstructing lesion. An anal stricture can reflect neoplasm or previous pelvic radiation. These are changes in diagnostic likelihood, not exclusions based on age (p1302).

Colonoscopy permits inspection, tissue sampling, lesion removal and potentially hemostasis in a single procedure. Its value depends on the question and on whether the patient can undergo adequate preparation. Before the examination, consider renal and cardiac function, medications, ability to drink the preparation, mobility and help reaching a toilet. Polyethylene glycol is generally well tolerated in older people; sodium-phosphate products can cause substantial fluid and electrolyte shifts and phosphate nephropathy. Assess the risk of oversedation and use small incremental doses with monitoring rather than assuming tolerance from a previous procedure years earlier (p1302).

When a patient has unexplained blood loss after conventional evaluation, capsule endoscopy can examine mucosa in the small intestine inaccessible to routine upper and lower scopes. It provides images but cannot biopsy or stop bleeding. CT colonography can display the colonic lumen and larger polyps, but a finding that requires sampling or removal still needs optical colonoscopy. Contrast studies can demonstrate strictures, fistulas and distortion in appropriate settings. Decide which result would change care: a frail patient able to tolerate a less invasive diagnostic test may still need a discussion of what would follow a positive result (p1302).

### Diverticula with pain, bleeding or neither

Diverticula protrude through the muscle wall, particularly at points where vessels penetrate. Many people with diverticulosis remain asymptomatic. Finding diverticula on a scan obtained for nonspecific discomfort therefore does not establish that the pain originates in them. In diverticulitis, inflammation centered on an involved diverticulum can cause localized pain, fever and leukocytosis, sometimes progressing to an abscess or perforation. Older adults may have less rebound tenderness and no striking leukocytosis. Contrast the acute focal inflammatory episode with segmental colitis associated with diverticular disease, where inflammation can affect mucosa between diverticula and cause more chronic diarrhea, pain and intermittent bleeding (p1302–1303).

Abdominal and pelvic CT helps confirm diverticulitis and assess complications. Colonoscopy can be important later to evaluate the colon, but acute inflammation increases the risk of perforation. In a clinically stable immunocompetent patient with mild disease who can drink and return for review, the chapter allows observation and selective antibiotic use. Severe pain, persistent vomiting, intolerance of oral intake, dehydration, systemic illness or concern for a complication weighs toward admission. Most improve within **48–72 hours**. Failure to improve should prompt reassessment for abscess, persistent inflammation, obstruction or an alternative diagnosis. The decision about elective resection depends on course and circumstances rather than a fixed lifetime count of attacks (p1303).

A bleeding diverticulum presents differently. Injury to an adjacent artery can produce abrupt, painless hematochezia without the inflammatory features of diverticulitis. Although diverticula are common in the left colon, the source of a major diverticular hemorrhage is frequently on the right. Estimate the rate of blood loss, monitor circulation, and establish access for resuscitation before focusing on localization. A patient with repeated brisk hemorrhage may need angiographic intervention or surgery even if endoscopy initially fails to show an active point. Incidental diverticulosis by itself is not definitive proof of the bleeding site (p1303, p1311–1313).

### Diarrhea with and without visible inflammation

Antibiotic exposure, hospitalization, frailty and altered intestinal flora raise concern for *C. difficile*. The clinical spectrum runs from diarrhea to severe colitis, ileus, megacolon and systemic illness. A patient who stops passing stool while the abdomen enlarges may be deteriorating, not recovering. Interpret a molecular assay in someone with compatible symptoms; a positive result alone can identify carriage. Review laxatives and other causes of diarrhea before assigning the diagnosis. If endoscopy is necessary because the diagnosis remains uncertain, the pseudomembranes illustrated in Figure 84-3 supply a different kind of evidence from a positive test for a toxigenic organism (p1304–1305).

Table 84-5 places antimicrobial exposure alongside tube feeding, gastrointestinal procedures, acid suppression, ICU care and prolonged hospitalization as risk factors for *C. difficile*. Figure 84-2 spans asymptomatic carriage, mild or severe diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis and toxic megacolon. The figure describes a spectrum; a positive test alone does not locate someone on it or predict inevitable progression (p1304).

Route matters in treatment. Oral vancomycin reaches the intestinal lumen. Intravenous vancomycin cannot replace it in *C. difficile* colitis. For a very ill patient the book describes oral vancomycin **500 mg four times daily** with IV metronidazole **500 mg every 8 hours**. This differs from the lower dose for an initial nonfulminant episode given above. Persistent ileus or fulminant systemic illness calls for urgent specialist assessment, because failure of luminal treatment to reach the involved colon can change management. If the inciting antibiotic is still needed for another infection, reevaluate its indication and spectrum rather than assuming it can simply be stopped (p1305).

Symptoms returning after treatment can represent relapse or reinfection; failure from drug resistance is relatively uncommon in the chapter's discussion. Table 84-6 illustrates a taper after a second relapse using **125 mg oral vancomycin** four times daily, twice daily, daily, every other day and then every third day, with each phase lasting **7 days**. Table 84-7 discusses microbiota-based treatment for selected recurrent or refractory cases, including repeated episodes requiring hospitalization. The purpose is to address repeated symptomatic disease that has not responded durably to conventional therapy. Neither a persistently positive assay without diarrhea nor an initial mildly loose stool establishes that indication (p1304–1305).

Chronic watery, often nonbloody diarrhea with an unremarkable colonoscopy suggests microscopic colitis. The defining inflammation is histologic: collagenous colitis has a thickened subepithelial collagen layer, whereas lymphocytic colitis has increased intraepithelial lymphocytes. Exclude enteric infection and obtain colonic biopsies even if there is little visible abnormality. Ask about smoking and medicines such as NSAIDs, lansoprazole or sertraline. Stopping an implicated drug may help. A bulking agent or loperamide can improve symptoms without resolving histologic inflammation. Budesonide limits systemic steroid exposure compared with prednisone, which is especially relevant to an older adult with osteoporosis, diabetes or impaired muscle strength (p1305–1306).

### Establishing an inflammatory bowel diagnosis late in life

Table 84-8 puts withdrawal of plausible offending medicines and smoking cessation alongside treatment for microscopic colitis. Examples include loperamide or a bulking agent and, when anti-inflammatory therapy is indicated, budesonide with relatively low systemic exposure. The authors caution against diphenoxylate–atropine in this setting. Review drugs such as an NSAID or lansoprazole before attributing persistent watery diarrhea to a new indication for more treatment (p1306).

Age at presentation changes the differential but does not exclude new inflammatory bowel disease. Crohn disease may involve different segments anywhere along the gastrointestinal tract, with normal intervening mucosa, deep ulcers, strictures or penetrating complications. Colonic involvement can produce blood; small-bowel disease more often raises questions about watery diarrhea, malabsorption, weight loss or nutritional deficiency. Distal ileal disease or resection can impair vitamin B12 absorption. Chronic blood loss can produce iron deficiency even in a person whose symptoms have been attributed to longstanding inflammation (p1306–1307).

Table 84-9 contrasts Crohn disease with UC by distribution, depth and complications. Skip areas, transmural disease and fistula formation favor Crohn disease. Figure 84-4 illustrates the inflamed colonic mucosa of UC; the continuous distribution must be assessed across the examination, not inferred from a single photograph (p1306, p1308).

Ulcerative colitis usually begins in the rectum and extends continuously through a variable length of colon, primarily affecting the mucosa. Frequent bloody stools, urgency or tenesmus suggest distal inflammation, but clinical features alone do not distinguish UC from infection, ischemia, radiation injury, malignancy or segmental inflammation associated with diverticula. Determine distribution endoscopically and use biopsy for histology. CT or MR enterography can be especially helpful in suspected Crohn disease when strictures, fistulas, abscesses or small-bowel lesions lie beyond endoscopic reach. Avoid inferring that a skip pattern or rectal sparing has the same significance in every treated and untreated patient (p1306–1308).

Table 84-10 summarizes doses used for IBD rather than providing a single escalation sequence. Sulfasalazine **4–6 g/day** can cause nausea and folate depletion; hemolysis is a concern in G6PD deficiency. Mesalamine is listed at **up to 4.8 g/day** and may cause headache. Prednisone is listed at **up to 60 mg/day**; glucose intolerance, osteoporosis and proximal weakness are important when evaluating the net functional cost. The chapter distinguishes inducing control of a flare from maintaining remission. A symptomatic response to systemic steroids does not justify prolonged exposure without reassessing an older patient's fracture, infection and metabolic risks (p1307–1308).

The same table lists 6-mercaptopurine **1.5 mg/kg** and azathioprine **3 mg/kg**, with marrow suppression, pancreatitis or hepatitis as relevant toxicities. It gives metronidazole and ciprofloxacin at **up to 1 g/day** each. Peripheral neuropathy can limit prolonged metronidazole use; tendon rupture and antibiotic-associated diarrhea are important fluoroquinolone concerns. Infliximab is listed at **5–10 mg/kg IV**, with infection and other immune complications. The chapter cautions against anti-TNF therapy in advanced heart failure and discusses adalimumab, methotrexate and newer targeted agents without implying that all have equivalent evidence in frail older adults. Indication and comorbidity guide the choice; the table's values are not patient-specific prescriptions (p1307–1308).

May 2024 Q64 describes chronic UC in an independent older adult whose previous surveillance examination was normal. The relevant issue is long-duration colonic inflammation. The date of one reassuring colonoscopy does not reset the risk clock or convert surveillance into average-risk screening. Check the extent and duration of colitis, findings and adequacy of the previous examination, and whether the person can benefit from detection and treatment. The book's interval is given above; both the schedule and the rationale differ from the workup of a short episode of infectious colitis. A rectal stump after subtotal colectomy can remain at risk. Confirm what anatomy remains before concluding that colorectal surveillance is complete (p1308–1309).

Surgery raises different functional questions in Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis. Focal small-bowel Crohn disease complicated by obstruction, abscess or fistula can require resection; the extent and site determine what absorptive function is lost. Extensive colitis can require colectomy. In a malnourished or debilitated patient, the chapter discusses staged surgery with an initial subtotal colectomy and ileostomy so that physical condition can improve before the next decision. Rectal disease, remaining continence and ability to manage an ostomy help determine the eventual operation. Removing diseased colon also changes the anatomy that remains under surveillance. A previous operation should prompt a review of the operative details rather than the assumption that a retained rectum or pouch carries no ongoing needs (p1307–1309).

Look for effects outside the bowel as well. In Crohn disease, anemia may reflect iron loss, ileal vitamin B12 malabsorption, chronic inflammation or a combination. Thrombocytosis and a raised inflammatory marker support inflammation but do not specify the cause. In a patient on an immunomodulator, new cytopenia or hepatitis may instead be a treatment complication. Compare the timing of laboratory changes with bowel symptoms, surgery, nutritional status and drug exposure before attributing all of them to an IBD flare (p1307).

### Is the threatened bowel small intestine or colon?

Acute interruption of superior mesenteric arterial flow may jeopardize extensive small bowel. Severe or escalating pain with relatively modest examination findings is concerning; an older patient may describe even major injury imprecisely. Chronic progressive disease more often leads to cramping after meals as flow fails to meet digestive demand. Patients may reduce food intake and lose weight, which can be mistaken for frailty or malignancy. CT angiography can define arterial anatomy and direct intervention. Recovery from transient colon ischemia is a poor guide to prognosis when an acute major-vessel mesenteric event remains possible (p1309–1310).

Colonic ischemia often follows a reduction in perfusion without an identifiable large-vessel occlusion. The pattern can range from reversible segmental injury to fulminant pan-colonic gangrene. Cramping followed by passage of blood is common; tenderness or bleeding can be surprisingly limited. Isolated right-colon involvement, severe pain without rectal bleeding, sustained hemodynamic compromise or peritoneal signs should increase concern for more extensive injury. Review recent hypotension or surgery, heart disease, dehydration and drugs that affect perfusion. A working diagnosis of colitis does not remove the need to consider acute mesenteric ischemia when the distribution or intensity is atypical (p1309–1311).

Tables 84-11 and 84-12 distinguish underlying vascular or mechanical causes from medication-associated disease. Arrhythmia, shock, arterial embolus or thrombosis, vasculitis and strangulating obstruction suggest different mechanisms; ask also about vasopressin, pseudoephedrine, sumatriptan and NSAID exposure. A drug association must be interpreted alongside the timing, distribution and circulatory state (p1309).

Table 84-13 assigns mild disease to typical segmental nonisolated-right-colon ischemia without adverse features. Moderate disease includes up to **three** listed factors; systolic pressure **below 90 mm Hg**, pulse **over 100/min**, BUN **over 20 mg/dL**, hemoglobin **below 12 g/dL** and LDH **over 350 U/L** are among them. Male sex, pain without bleeding and mucosal ulceration also count. Severe disease includes more than three features, or any peritoneal signs, pneumatosis, portal venous gas, gangrene, pancolonic disease or isolated right-colon involvement. It warrants urgent surgical evaluation and intensive support. The printed sodium row reads **above 136 mEq/L** and the WBC row lacks a comparison sign. These entries differ from the guideline cited beneath the table (p1311).

> Current practice (not the book): In the ACG severity classification the adverse sodium finding is below 136 mEq/L and the white-cell criterion is above 15 × 10⁹/L. Apply those directions when using the guideline. - [American College of Gastroenterology, 2015, Table 6](https://acgcdn.gi.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/ACG-Colon-Ischemia-Guideline-Summary.pdf).

### A dilated cecum after hip surgery

June 2023 Q30 describes new distention, pain, constipation and reduced bowel sounds after a hip operation. The characteristic setting points toward acute colonic pseudo-obstruction. Check for **hypokalemia**, the electrolyte abnormality selected in the question; an electrolyte disturbance can contribute to impaired motility and is a reversible treatment target. Distention on the radiograph is not pathognomonic. Exclude a mechanical lesion such as volvulus, a tumor, stricture or impacted stool, and assess for peritoneal signs, fever or instability. The largest-radius segment, the cecum, experiences high wall tension with continued dilation and is at risk of ischemia and perforation (p1310–1311).

Begin with bowel rest, IV hydration, oxygenation, electrolyte correction and withdrawal of drugs worsening ileus where possible. Monitor the abdominal findings and degree of dilation. If these measures fail, the chapter discusses neostigmine **1–2 mg IV or subcutaneously** in an appropriately monitored setting, with possible repeat administration after an incomplete response. Mechanical intestinal or urinary obstruction and hypersensitivity are absolute contraindications in Table 84-14. Bradycardia, recent myocardial infarction, asthma, acidosis, peptic ulcer disease and beta-blocker therapy are relative concerns. Selected refractory patients may need endoscopic decompression; insufflation adds perforation risk in an already distended colon. Surgery remains an option for complications or failed less invasive treatment (p1310–1311).

### Localizing persistent or intermittent hemorrhage

The color of blood depends on transit and rate as well as anatomic site. Brisk upper GI bleeding can present as hematochezia, so a patient with shock and red rectal blood needs resuscitation and assessment of a possible proximal source. Review antiplatelets, anticoagulants and prior episodes, and evaluate circulation before undertaking bowel preparation. A stable patient able to tolerate preparation can undergo colonoscopy for localization and treatment; ongoing brisk bleeding or recurrent bleeding that remains difficult to localize may call for another strategy. The choice depends on whether the patient is actively bleeding at the time of the study (p1311–1313).

Tagged red-cell scintigraphy can detect a slower rate than conventional angiography; the chapter cites approximately **0.1 versus 0.5 mL/min**. Scintigraphy can help localize intermittent bleeding, whereas CT angiography can direct attention to a source and catheter angiography can permit embolization when there is active extravasation. A defined site can guide a limited operation if hemostasis fails. Do not assign causality solely because an older person's colonoscopy reveals vascular ectasias: they may occur in asymptomatic people. Multiple small lesions in the cecum or ascending colon can nevertheless cause substantial painless bleeding and can be treated endoscopically, for example with argon plasma coagulation (p1312–1313).

Table 84-15 broadens the bleeding differential to postpolypectomy bleeding, inflammatory or infectious colitis, radiation or NSAID injury, and selected small-bowel or vascular sources. Figure 84-5 proposes a mechanism for cecal ectasia: intermittent obstruction of a vein passing through the muscular wall is followed by dilated, tortuous submucosal veins and eventually a small arteriovenous communication. This explains a possible source of intermittent blood loss, without proving that any ectasia seen during an examination caused the current bleed (p1312).

## Exam traps

- Colonic ischemia often lacks demonstrable vascular occlusion; its prognosis differs from catastrophic small-bowel ischemia (p1309–1310).
- Glucocorticoids have no proven benefit in colonic ischemia and increase perforation risk; this differs from budesonide treatment for microscopic colitis (p1306, p1310).
