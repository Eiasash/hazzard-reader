# Chapter 84 Study: Aging of the Gastrointestinal System and Selected Lower GI Disorders

## Summary

**Aging & investigation**
- Visceral hyposensitivity can hide severe disease: perforation or ischemia may cause little tenderness. Abnormal liver tests should not be dismissed as normal aging (p1299, p1302).
- Age alone does not preclude endoscopy. Use careful preparation and small incremental sedation doses with cardiopulmonary monitoring (p1302).
- Polyethylene-glycol bowel preparation is well tolerated; avoid sodium-phosphate preparations in older adults because of fluid shifts, electrolyte disturbance and phosphate nephropathy (p1302).

**Diarrhea & drugs**
- C. difficile assays require clinical context: PCR can be positive in asymptomatic carriers. Withdraw the precipitating antibiotic if feasible (p1304–1305).
- Initial mild–moderate C. difficile disease: oral vancomycin **125 mg four times daily** or fidaxomicin **200 mg twice daily**, each for **10 days** (p1305).
- Intravenous vancomycin does not adequately reach the colonic lumen and is ineffective for C. difficile colitis (p1305).
- Microscopic colitis may have normal colonoscopy; exclude infection and obtain biopsies. Budesonide **9 mg daily** induces remission; prednisone carries more adverse effects in older adults (p1306).
- New IBD can occur late in life. Exclude infectious, ischemic, malignant and diverticular mimics before labeling ulcerative colitis (pp1306, 1308). In long-standing colonic ulcerative colitis, the book advises surveillance colonoscopy with biopsies **every 1–3 years**, beginning **8–10 years after disease onset** (p1309).

**Ischemia & obstruction**
- Sudden small-bowel ischemia can be catastrophic; chronic disease may cause postprandial pain, food avoidance and weight loss (p1309).
- Colonic ischemia more often produces mild crampy pain and limited rectal bleeding. Severe pain may indicate transmural necrosis (p1310).
- Suspected colonic ischemia warrants CT-based assessment and close monitoring; stabilize, optimize cardiac function and rest the bowel. Antibiotics are used for moderate/severe cases (p1310).
- Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction often follows severe illness, abdominal surgery, or orthopedic surgery of the hip, pelvis or knee; distinguish it from mechanical obstruction. Cecal diameter at least **12 cm** marks megacolon and increased concern for perforation (p1310). Correct reversible contributors, including electrolyte disturbances (p1311).


### Long-standing colitis and postoperative abdominal distention

- The surveillance question concerns chronic inflammation in the colon. Both the extent and persistence of inflammation matter. Crohn colitis can carry a risk comparable to ulcerative colitis. The chapter cites cumulative colorectal cancer risks in UC of **2% after 10 years** and **18% after 30 years**. A patient with long-standing colonic disease is therefore assessed for surveillance even if currently well. A retained rectal stump after subtotal colectomy still needs attention (p1309).
- Marked distention after hip or pelvic surgery, sometimes with surprisingly little pain, can reflect acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome). Exclude a mechanical cause. Radiography may show dilation of the entire colon or mainly the right colon. The cecum's large radius makes it particularly vulnerable to ischemia and perforation as pressure rises (p1310).
- Check electrolytes, including potassium, volume status and oxygenation, and review drugs that impair motility. Provide bowel rest and IV hydration. If dilation persists after supportive measures, the book describes monitored neostigmine or, in selected refractory cases, endoscopic decompression. Mechanical obstruction precludes neostigmine. Bradycardia and recent infarction are among the relative contraindications (p1310–1311).
- Brisk painless hematochezia suggests diverticular bleeding or vascular ectasia. Diverticula commonly occur on the left, yet diverticular bleeding often originates from the right colon. Stabilize hemodynamically significant bleeding first. In contrast, colonic ischemia often causes cramping with bleeding, and severe pain or peritoneal signs raise concern for more extensive injury (p1303, p1310–1311, p1313).

> Current practice (not the book): For an initial nonfulminant C. difficile infection, the focused guideline conditionally favors fidaxomicin when available. Oral vancomycin remains an acceptable alternative. - [SHEA/IDSA focused update, 2021](https://www.idsociety.org/practice-guideline/clostridioides-difficile-2021-focused-update/).

## Exam traps

- Colonic ischemia often lacks demonstrable vascular occlusion; its prognosis differs from catastrophic small-bowel ischemia (p1309–1310).
- Glucocorticoids have no proven benefit in colonic ischemia and increase perforation risk; this differs from budesonide treatment for microscopic colitis (p1306, p1310).

## Recall drill

**1. Why can a relatively benign abdominal examination be misleading?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Visceral sensation can decline with aging. Serious ischemia or perforation may produce little tenderness. (p1299, p1302)</details>

**2. Which bowel preparation risk is especially important in an older adult?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Sodium phosphate can cause electrolyte disturbance, fluid shifts and phosphate nephropathy. Polyethylene glycol is generally well tolerated. (p1302)</details>

**3. What is distinctive about diverticular hemorrhage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Often brisk and painless. Although diverticula predominate on the left, bleeding frequently originates in the right colon. (p1303)</details>

**4. How should a positive C. difficile PCR be interpreted?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It may identify an asymptomatic carrier. Interpret it in a patient with compatible diarrhea and review precipitating antibiotic exposure. (p1304–1305)</details>

**5. Why is IV vancomycin inappropriate treatment for C. difficile colitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It does not attain adequate colonic luminal concentrations. The chapter's vancomycin regimen for initial mild–moderate illness is oral. (p1305)</details>

**6. What suggests microscopic colitis despite a normal colonoscopy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chronic watery diarrhea, often with an unremarkable examination. Exclude infection and obtain biopsies to establish the histologic diagnosis. (p1305–1306)</details>

**7. Which steroid is used to induce remission in microscopic colitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Oral budesonide 9 mg daily. Its relatively low systemic absorption is valuable in an older adult. (p1306)</details>

**8. What influences colorectal cancer risk in long-standing IBD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Duration, extent and degree of colonic inflammation. Crohn colitis may carry a risk similar to UC. (p1309)</details>

**9. When does the book begin surveillance in long-standing colonic IBD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Eight to ten years after onset, with colonoscopy and biopsies every one to three years. Consider continued rectal surveillance after subtotal colectomy. (p1309)</details>

**10. How can chronic small-bowel ischemia present?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Meal-related abdominal pain may lead to food avoidance and weight loss. (p1309)</details>

**11. Which setting should raise suspicion for Ogilvie syndrome?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>New abdominal distention during severe illness or following abdominal, pelvic, hip or knee surgery, often with failure to pass flatus or stool. (p1310)</details>

**12. Why pay particular attention to the cecal diameter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The cecum usually has the largest radius and therefore high wall tension with dilation. A diameter of at least 12 cm meets the chapter's definition of megacolon. (p1310)</details>

**13. What reversible causes should be addressed in acute pseudo-obstruction?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Correct electrolyte disturbances, hypovolemia and hypoxemia. Review motility-impairing medications and provide hydration and bowel rest. (p1311)</details>

**14. What must be excluded before neostigmine is considered?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Mechanical obstruction. Review contraindications such as bradycardia and recent infarction and monitor closely if given. (p1311)</details>

**15. How does steroid use differ in colonic ischemia and microscopic colitis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Systemic glucocorticoids lack established benefit and increase perforation risk in ischemia. Budesonide can induce remission in microscopic colitis. (p1306, p1310)</details>
