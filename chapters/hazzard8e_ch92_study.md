# Chapter 92 Study: Gastrointestinal Malignancies

**Full chapter available: [Read Chapter 92](?chapter=92).**

## Summary

### Epidemiology & diagnosis
- Older adults are underrepresented in treatment trials; consider function, comorbidity, toxicity and social support, and refer early for oncology input. (p. 1435)
<a id="p1437"></a>
- Older adults appear to have more right-sided CRC, obstruction and perforation; stage distribution is similar to younger adults. Men treated with curative radiotherapy for localized prostate cancer have a reported **70% higher risk** of cancer in the previously irradiated bowel (pp1437–1438).
- Individualize CRC screening; stopping may be reasonable when life expectancy is shorter than the **5-10 years** for polyp-to-cancer progression. (p. 1440)

### Management
- Localized colon cancer: resection; adequate nodal evaluation examines at least **12 nodes**. Higher-volume hospitals had lower operative mortality in cited older cohorts. (p. 1440)
- Resected stage III colon cancer: adjuvant fluoropyrimidines benefit older adults; added oxaliplatin benefit is less certain in older subgroups and neuropathy may worsen falls/function. Consider fitness and comorbidities. (p. 1441)
<a id="p1445"></a>
- Pancreatic cancer: avoid assigning a poor prognosis without accurate pathological diagnosis; resection is potentially curative and can be safe in carefully selected older adults. (p. 1445)
<a id="p1446"></a>
- Gastric cancer: gastrectomy is the curative treatment described; most, but not all, studies suggest similar perioperative risk across age groups. (p. 1446)
<a id="p1448"></a>
- Liver-confined HCC: resection, transplantation, ablation or locoregional treatments may be options; geriatric assessment helps selection. In the study, **five-year survival and complication rates** after transarterial chemoembolization were similar in adults **75 or older** and younger adults (p1448).
<a id="p1449"></a>
- Esophageal surgery evidence is mixed: most, but not all, studies suggest age alone is not limiting; assess fitness and cardiopulmonary risk. For Barrett-associated adenocarcinoma, evidence on whether incidence increases with advanced age is conflicting (p1449).

### Drugs & supportive care
- Olaparib **300 mg twice daily**: POLO maintenance after at least **16 weeks** of continuous first-line platinum without progression in germline BRCA-mutated metastatic pancreatic cancer; improved progression-free survival, but interim overall survival did not differ. (p. 1446)
- Gemcitabine: liver-enzyme elevation, rarely hemolytic uremic syndrome; FOLFIRINOX: febrile neutropenia and sensory neuropathy are among its toxicities. (p. 1445)
- Pancreatic palliation may include drainage/stenting for biliary obstruction, lipase for pancreatic insufficiency, and celiac plexus neurolysis for pain. (p. 1446)


### Interpreting a colorectal cancer vignette

- A right-sided obstructing or perforated tumor can bring an older patient to surgery acutely. In the studies cited, the distribution of localized, regional and advanced disease at diagnosis was nevertheless similar across ages. Trial participants also had similar performance status and frequency of tumor-related symptoms, although older adults tended to lose more weight. More acute presentations contributed to greater perioperative mortality (p1437).
- Prior pelvic radiotherapy matters in its anatomical context. The reported excess after curative radiotherapy for localized prostate cancer concerned the irradiated bowel. Most older patients with colorectal cancer had no obvious risk factor beyond age, and hereditary syndromes accounted for less disease in this group because most hereditary cases presented earlier (p1438).
- Screening involves competing risks. Colonoscopy can see proximal lesions beyond the sigmoidoscope, but bleeding and perforation are more frequent with colonoscopy. CT colonography still requires preparation and insufflation. Consider whether a person could benefit from finding and treating an asymptomatic lesion within their remaining lifespan (p1439–1440).

### Pancreatic pain and liver-directed treatment

- Match the intervention to the symptom. A biliary stent or percutaneous drainage can relieve malignant obstruction. Pancreatic insufficiency may contribute to weight loss, for which pancreatic enzymes may help. Celiac plexus neurolysis is an option for pain. Cachexia, anorexia and weight loss occur in **up to 80%** of patients in the chapter's account (p1446).
- Maintenance therapy is a separate decision from treating newly metastatic disease. POLO compared olaparib with placebo only after a platinum response or stable disease in the specified germline BRCA population. Median progression-free survival was **7.4 versus 3.8 months**. At the interim analysis, overall survival was **18.9 versus 18.1 months**, without a significant difference (p1446).
- For hepatocellular carcinoma confined to the liver, assess tumor extent, hepatic reserve, procedural risk and life expectancy. A cited comparison found similar five-year survival and complication rates after chemoembolization in selected adults aged **75 or older** and younger patients. The surgery-versus-ablation SURF cohort excluded adults aged **80** and older, limiting extrapolation to that group (p1448).

### Reading the incidence and survival figures

Figure 92-1 plots new digestive-system cancers against age at diagnosis. For people aged **50–54**, the rate is **109.2 per 100,000**, rising to **513.6 per 100,000** at **85 or older**. Figure 92-2 instead plots deaths against age at death: the corresponding oldest group has **398.3 deaths per 100,000**. These are population rates. The patient asking “What happens now that my cancer has been diagnosed?” needs information conditional on site, stage and treatment, rather than the mortality rate of everyone in that age band (p1436–1437).

Figure 92-3 changes both the denominator and the outcome. Among people already diagnosed with a digestive-system cancer, five-year relative survival was **43% at ages 65–74** and **34.2% at 75 or older** in the SEER series displayed. Figure 92-4 narrows the site to colon and rectum and shows incidence rising across the plotted population groups, with different curves by race and ethnicity. Figure 92-5 then separates colorectal cancer survival by site-specific stage. Across age bands the difference between a localized and a distant cancer is striking. Unstaged disease is a separate category: the graph does not license assigning an unstaged tumor the prognosis of a proven localized cancer (p1437–1438).

When interpreting the survival gap, separate deaths due to cancer, competing illnesses and differences in care. In the SEER–Medicare cohort of older adults with localized colorectal cancer, heart failure accounted for **9.4%** of deaths, COPD **5.3%** and diabetes **3.9%**. Those are shares of deaths in a particular cohort. They are not the probabilities that a newly diagnosed patient with each comorbidity will die from it. Functional limitations and social isolation can affect both the opportunity to receive treatment and what the patient can tolerate once treatment begins (p1435, p1438).

### How representative is the evidence?

A patient at the median age of diagnosis may already be older than most people in a drug-registration trial. The chapter reports that people over **75** accounted for **9%** of trial participants compared with **31%** of people diagnosed with cancer in the United States. For colorectal cancer in particular, adults older than **65** accounted for **70–75%** of diagnoses but only **40–48%** of participants in the cooperative-group or NCI-sponsored trials. An efficacy estimate for the whole trial therefore often has much narrower uncertainty than the estimate specifically for frail older participants (p1435–1436).

Look beyond formal eligibility criteria. A burdensome schedule may require repeated transport and an available caregiver. The oncologist may be reluctant to propose enrollment, the patient may not know a trial exists, or the protocol may exclude comorbid illness or concurrent medication. A prospective trial designed specifically for older adults with previously untreated metastatic colon cancer closed early because too few entered. That early closure cannot tell us whether adding oxaliplatin helped or harmed the intended population. At consultation, ask which patient resembling this one actually received the regimen, what outcome mattered to them, and what support was needed to complete it (p1436).

### Colorectal cancer: screening, presentation and resection

The practical difference between a screening discussion and a diagnostic workup is important here. A person without symptoms can experience an immediate burden from preparation or investigation but benefit only much later if removing a polyp prevents cancer. The chapter's stopping argument depends on that lag and the individual's life expectancy. An older person already presenting with obstruction, perforation or weight loss has a different question: establish the diagnosis and its extent. The delay between a polyp and cancer is not a rationale for ignoring a symptomatic presentation (p1437, p1439–1440).

Fecal occult blood testing and flexible sigmoidoscopy do not impose the same demands or detect the same lesions as colonoscopy. In randomized studies discussed in the chapter, routine fecal occult blood testing reduced colon cancer mortality by **15–20%** during **8–18 years** of follow-up. A sigmoidoscopy trial in older adults showed benefit yet could not directly visualize the proximal colon. Colonoscopy offers that access, with a higher risk of bleeding and perforation. CT colonography avoids some aspects of endoscopy but still requires bowel preparation and distention. The choice turns on whether an abnormal result would lead to a diagnostic or therapeutic intervention the patient would accept (p1439–1440).

For resectable colon cancer, assess perioperative risk and the adequacy of the oncologic operation separately. Removing a visible tumor without an adequate lymph-node evaluation risks understaging the disease. The chapter describes substantially lower rates of adequate nodal evaluation in older adults. In a US claims analysis, operative mortality in those older than **80** was **4.6%** at high-volume hospitals and **7.3%** at smaller institutions. This was an association with center volume, not a guaranteed benefit for an individual transfer. Referral can change access to expertise, while travel, recovery support and urgency also matter to the patient (p1440).

A resected stage III colon cancer raises the question of treating occult residual disease. Evidence supports a fluoropyrimidine in appropriate older patients. Adding oxaliplatin is a separate decision: pooled or subgroup studies did not consistently show the same incremental benefit, and the person most vulnerable to neuropathy may pay a high functional price. Ask about gait, previous falls and dependence before treatment and again during treatment. Neuropathy sufficient to impair transfers can substantially alter the balance even when the patient remains able to attend chemotherapy (p1441).

Duration also matters. In the pooled IDEA comparison, the chapter reports five-year overall survival of **82.4% with 3 months** and **82.8% with 6 months** of oxaliplatin-based adjuvant treatment. Age-specific results were unavailable in the discussion. Many oncologists continued a longer infusional fluorouracil/oxaliplatin course for T4 or N2 disease. Thus overall similarity in the pooled population does not answer the narrower question of duration for every high-risk older adult. Distinguish treatment completion and toxicity, disease-free survival and overall survival when interpreting these results (p1441–1442).

Rectal cancer adds the problem of local pelvic recurrence. For deeply invasive or node-positive rectal disease, chemoradiation around surgery can improve local control and disease-free survival. The chapter describes increasing interest in delivering systemic treatment before surgery. PRODIGE23 reported improved disease-free and metastasis-free survival and more complete pathological responses with total neoadjuvant therapy in the population studied. Older adults were less often referred for perioperative treatment, and age-specific evidence was limited. Choose between these strategies with the location and local extent of the rectal tumor in mind; a recommendation about postoperative colon chemotherapy alone does not settle it (p1442).

### Metastatic colorectal cancer: resectability, fitness and molecular selection

Metastatic disease has different treatment pathways. A selected patient with resectable liver metastases may be considered for surgery, whereas a patient with diffuse unresectable disease may be offered systemic therapy for response, survival or symptom relief. Older patients who underwent liver resection in a specialist series had outcomes comparable with younger operative patients. This describes people selected for resection. It cannot predict the operative risk or benefit for an unselected patient with severe comorbidity or disease outside the proposed operative field (p1442).

Fluorouracil with leucovorin and oral capecitabine are fluoropyrimidine options. Adding irinotecan or oxaliplatin changes the toxicity to anticipate. In a comparison of irinotecan schedules, patients over **70** experienced more severe neutropenia and diarrhea despite no age difference in time to progression or survival. Oxaliplatin adds peripheral neuropathy. With capecitabine, painful hand–foot toxicity can interfere with walking or handling medications. For a patient whose priorities include getting out of bed independently, these adverse effects differ in ways a single numerical toxicity grade does not convey (p1441, p1443).

FOCUS2 addressed a more directly relevant population: older and frail patients began fluorouracil, FOLFOX, capecitabine or CAPOX at **80%** of standard doses, with subsequent adjustment at the clinician's discretion. The protocol tested an approach and combinations of agents under those conditions. It did not establish that every older adult should begin all chemotherapy at the same reduced percentage. Compare a fit trial participant able to maintain full dosing with a patient enrolled because usual initial treatment was judged unsuitable. Their results answer different questions about feasibility and toxicity (p1443).

Biologic and immune treatments also require selection. Cetuximab and panitumumab act against EGFR; the book explains why a tumor KRAS mutation can make an anti-EGFR antibody ineffective. Bevacizumab targets vascular endothelial growth factor. A comparison between different biologics given with combination chemotherapy cannot be read as a comparison of the underlying chemotherapy alone. For previously treated disease, agents such as regorafenib had modest survival effects in a defined trial population. Discuss the endpoint, prior treatment and the person's function before interpreting an active agent as a suitable next step (p1443–1445).

### Pancreas and stomach: separate curative therapy from palliation

A pancreatic mass with jaundice and weight loss can invite an immediate assumption of incurability. The chapter warns that older adults less often receive pathological confirmation and that some are consequently assigned an incorrect diagnosis and prognosis. Determine whether the tumor is localized and resectable. Surgery can offer the prospect of cure in selected patients. Operative series differ in case selection and complication rates. Even if operation is technically possible, the ability to recover, regain nutrition and proceed to postoperative treatment is relevant to the decision (p1445).

After pancreatic resection, ESPAC-4 compared gemcitabine plus capecitabine with gemcitabine alone and found a median overall survival of **28.0 versus 25.5 months**. A different trial compared modified FOLFIRINOX with gemcitabine after resection and reported **54.4 versus 35.0 months**. Those comparisons were randomized within each study. The chapter does not provide a head-to-head comparison of the two combination regimens in the same older population. Do not rank them simply by placing survival medians from different trials side by side (p1445).

In metastatic pancreatic cancer the choice may be between a more intensive combination, a less intensive drug or symptom-focused care. For a patient considered for FOLFIRINOX, consider febrile neutropenia, diarrhea and sensory neuropathy. Nab-paclitaxel added to gemcitabine improved response and median survival compared with gemcitabine alone in its trial, while adding peripheral neuropathy and cytopenias. A BRCA-associated tumor controlled on platinum opens the separate maintenance question described above. Meanwhile obstruction, malabsorption, pain and depression each have treatments that need not await a change in systemic therapy (p1445–1446).

For localized gastric adenocarcinoma, the operative question is whether disease can be removed completely and at an acceptable risk. In the CRITICS age analysis, preoperative chemotherapy produced severe toxicity in **77%** of older participants compared with **62%** of younger participants, and older patients less often completed planned therapy. Nevertheless two-year overall survival in the comparison was **59% versus 61%**. This was an unplanned age subgroup. Similar survival does not mean equal treatment tolerability, and a lower completion rate does not prove the treatment offered no benefit (p1446–1447).

In advanced gastric cancer, consider renal function when using a platinum drug. A fit patient who can tolerate combination therapy differs from one who cannot sustain adequate intake or manage adverse effects at home. Tumor testing can identify a different therapeutic target: in HER2-positive gastric cancer, ToGA compared chemotherapy with or without trastuzumab, with median overall survival of **13.8 versus 11.1 months**. Ramucirumab, alone or with paclitaxel, was studied in advanced disease in another setting. The chapter points out where detailed outcomes specifically for older participants were unavailable (p1447–1448).

### Liver, esophagus, biliary tract and anal tumors

For hepatocellular carcinoma, choice of therapy depends on the liver as well as the tumor. A small liver-confined tumor in a person with usable hepatic reserve may allow curative-intent resection, transplantation or ablation. Embolization can be considered when surgery is less suitable. By contrast the SHARP study enrolled patients with inoperable HCC and Child–Pugh A cirrhosis. Sorafenib prolonged median overall survival to **10.7 months** compared with **7.9 months** on placebo. This result in advanced disease does not establish superiority over local therapy in a resectable patient, or equivalent safety in more severe hepatic decompensation (p1448–1449).

For esophageal cancer, progressive difficulty swallowing can itself drive weight loss and diminish reserve. The chapter describes similar distributions of site, stage and histology at presentation across older and younger groups. In surgical series, some selected older cohorts had acceptable results and others experienced more complications. Look especially at respiratory and cardiovascular reserve. The CROSS study supported preoperative chemoradiation in its study population; older adults outside its age range were assessed separately in observational data. A better outcome after combined treatment in a registry may partly reflect selection of fitter patients for surgery (p1449–1450).

Older adults are also affected by disparities in referral. The US esophageal cancer comparison found fewer surgical consultations and less curative surgery among older Black patients than among older White patients. Thus “no surgical treatment” in a database can mean a decision after specialist evaluation, but it can also mean that the option was never evaluated. When substantial comorbidities limit treatment for metastatic esophageal cancer, single-agent fluoropyrimidine, taxane or irinotecan may provide palliation. The evidence for more intensive combinations specifically in older adults was limited (p1449–1450).

For gallbladder cancer and cholangiocarcinoma, increasing age brings higher incidence but the presentation is similar across ages. ERCP may contribute to diagnosis even at very advanced age. Resection is the potentially curative option described for localized disease. After resection the book lists adjuvant capecitabine for **6 months** among the options. In advanced disease, a gemcitabine-containing regimen may provide palliation; hematologic toxicity was greater in older patients in a small combination-therapy study. A favorable surgical series is not evidence to subject a frail patient with unresectable cancer to radical surgery (p1450–1451).

For localized anal carcinoma the treatment described is usually sphincter conserving: fluorouracil and mitomycin with radiation. The chapter reports that **60–73%** were free of both recurrence and colostomy at **4 years**. This combined endpoint matters to someone weighing the consequences for daily function. In women treated at older ages, pelvic radiation was associated with increased fracture risk. For metastatic disease the aim changes to systemic control. Carboplatin with paclitaxel is among the regimens discussed; the chapter cautions against assuming precise efficacy in the very old when the study population did not include them (p1451).

### What sequencing can change

A personal or family history of pancreatic cancer may justify a panel including BRCA genes even without a family history of breast or ovarian cancer. In colorectal cancer, germline testing can address inherited susceptibility. Profiling the established tumor can answer a different question: whether a molecular feature predicts benefit from a treatment or carries prognostic information. The same patient may need discussion of both, but a family-risk conclusion cannot be inferred simply from a tumor response (p1451–1452).

Use the molecular result alongside the clinical decision it is intended to inform. An apparent target may open a trial or suggest an option for later treatment. It does not by itself determine that a patient with limited reserve will benefit more from that option than from symptom control. The chapter leaves important practical questions open, including the optimal time to profile disease and whether to sample primary tumor, metastatic tissue or circulating tumor material. Those uncertainties belong in a treatment discussion, particularly where evidence from older patients is sparse (p1451–1452).

### Exam traps
- Poorer overall survival can reflect noncancer deaths; do not equate it automatically with worse cancer-specific survival. (p. 1438)
- Sigmoidoscopy can miss proximal tumors, which are more common in older adults. (p. 1439)

**1. Why are age-specific cancer treatment estimates uncertain?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Older adults are underrepresented in trials; function, comorbidities, treatment toxicity and social support must be considered when applying the evidence. (p1435)</details>

**2. What CRC presentation differences are described in older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>More right-sided tumors, obstruction and perforation, with a greater likelihood of acute presentation; stage distribution is similar to younger adults. (p. 1437)</details>

**3. Why can overall and cancer-specific survival tell different stories?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Comorbid diseases account for substantial mortality; some investigators found age-related overall-survival differences were no longer significant after excluding noncancer deaths. (p1438)</details>

**4. What limitation of sigmoidoscopy matters particularly in older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It misses proximal tumors beyond its reach, and older adults are more likely to have proximal neoplasia. (p1439)</details>

**5. What time horizon helps frame stopping CRC screening?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stopping may be reasonable when life expectancy is shorter than the <strong>5-10 years</strong> for a polyp to progress to cancer. (p1440)</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>At least <strong>12 lymph nodes</strong>; inadequate evaluation correlated with inferior survival. (p1440)</details>

**7. Is oxaliplatin's adjuvant survival benefit established equally in all older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Older-subgroup findings are mixed, with several showing no added survival advantage; it can be considered for fit adults with minimal comorbidity. (p1441)</details>

**8. Which oxaliplatin toxicity can undermine function and increase falls?**

**9. What pancreatic-cancer diagnostic shortcut can incorrectly assign a poor prognosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Omitting accurate tissue confirmation to spare diagnostic discomfort; older patients receive pathological confirmation less frequently and may be misdiagnosed. (p1445)</details>

**10. What is the potentially curative treatment for localized pancreatic cancer?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Surgical resection; it can be safe in carefully selected older adults. (p1445)</details>

**11. Which rare serious toxicity is described for gemcitabine?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Hemolytic uremic syndrome; liver-enzyme elevations are also described. (p1445)</details>

**12. Which patients received olaparib maintenance in POLO, and at what dose?**
