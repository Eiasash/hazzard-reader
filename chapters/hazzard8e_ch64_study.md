# Chapter 64 Study: Traumatic Brain Injury and Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy

## Summary

**TBI & later-life disease**
- TBI is heterogeneous: characterize severity, loss of consciousness, amnesia, frequency, injury mechanism and associated hemorrhage or other damage (p997).
- TBI of varying severity is associated with later dementia, but the underlying pathology is not established as simply Alzheimer disease (p997).
- A cited study found **44%** greater subsequent Parkinson diagnosis risk after TBI than after nonbrain trauma in middle-aged/older patients; severity and frequency influenced risk (p999).

**Chronic traumatic encephalopathy**
- CTE is a primary tauopathy associated with repetitive head impacts, including concussive and subconcussive exposure (p997, p999).
- Exposures include contact sports, military blast and repeated physical abuse; the amount/duration of exposure matters (p997, p999).
- The characteristic lesion is irregular perivascular phosphorylated tau in neurons and neurites at cortical sulcal depths (p997, p1004).
- Purely astrocytic perivascular tau, including age-related tau astrogliopathy, does not meet the pathological definition of CTE (p1004).
- Early CTE need not contain amyloid; amyloid deposition may appear with aging alongside the tau pathology (p1004).

**Assessment & progression**
- Behavioral, cognitive, dementia and motor presentations are nonspecific; no imaging, laboratory or cognitive test definitively diagnoses CTE during life (p997).
- Definitive diagnosis requires postmortem neuropathology; clinical symptoms or exposure history alone cannot establish it (p1005).
- Pathology generally becomes more extensive over time, involving medial temporal and deep brain structures; some individuals have an indolent course even at advanced age (p1004–1005).
- Coexisting disease can contribute to symptom progression; TBI may also cause axonal loss, white-matter degeneration and persistent inflammation (p998–999, p1005).

### What the dementia cohorts actually measured

May 2024 Q63 accepted both Alzheimer disease and normal pressure hydrocephalus. That discussion describes a broader association with subsequent dementia after injuries of varying severity. In a pooled cohort of **7130 participants**, a history of TBI with loss of consciousness did not correlate with a clinical Alzheimer diagnosis or Alzheimer neuropathological change at autopsy. An independent series of **more than 4000** autopsies also found no association with Alzheimer pathology. That discussion does not present normal pressure hydrocephalus as a result of those studies (p997).

The exposure and the outcome determine what a comparison means. A single severe injury can leave lasting axonal and white-matter damage. Repeated impacts, including ones without a recognized concussion, have a separate association with CTE. An epidemiologic diagnosis of dementia and a specific microscopic lesion are different outcomes (p997–999).

### Following the tau distribution

Look for phosphorylated tau in **neurons** around a small vessel deep in a cortical sulcus. The earliest lesions are focal. With greater pathological burden, lesions appear at multiple sulcal depths and extend into neighboring superficial cortex. More advanced stages involve the medial temporal lobe and other regions. Staging describes distribution at examination; it cannot establish when an individual patient's symptoms began or how rapidly they will progress (p1000–1005).

The map also assigns May 2024 Q66 to this chapter because the exam's chapter column says 64. Its cited page is in Chapter 60. In DICE, first describe the episode, then investigate causes such as pain, medication effects or constipation. Create a plan with the patient and caregiver, then evaluate whether it was tried and helped. Constipation belongs to **Investigate** (Chapter 60, p924).

### Describe the injury before asking what it predicts

The useful history begins with what actually happened. Record the age at injury, the mechanism, whether the injury was focal or diffuse, loss of consciousness, posttraumatic amnesia and the acute findings. A record of hemorrhage, contusion, infarction or skull fracture carries different information from a remembered blow without documented loss of consciousness. Separately ask about the number and duration of exposures, including contact sport, military service and physical abuse. Exposure to repeated head impacts includes impacts that produced no recognized concussion. Counting diagnosed concussions alone can therefore give a misleading impression of cumulative exposure (p997, p999–1000).

Then describe the interval after injury. Persistent difficulty beginning at the time of a severe injury raises a different question from a new progressive cognitive or motor syndrome after years of relatively stable function. Ask an informant which abilities changed, when they changed, and what the patient could still do between events. The long-term outcome may reflect the original injury, later neurodegeneration, comorbid disease and the person's reserve. A convincing history of head trauma makes this history more important; it does not eliminate the need to evaluate other causes of new dementia or parkinsonism (p997–1000, p1005).

### Dementia after TBI and what the autopsy studies measured

May 2024 Q63 captures an important tension between an exam key and its cited page. Both Alzheimer disease and normal-pressure hydrocephalus received credit. The opening of Chapter 64 describes a relationship between prior TBI and dementia but emphasizes uncertainty about the responsible pathology. In a pooled group of **7,130** participants from the Religious Orders Study, Memory and Aging Project and Adult Changes in Thought, TBI with loss of consciousness did not predict clinical Alzheimer dementia or Alzheimer neuropathological change at autopsy. This is a result about particular outcomes in particular cohorts. It neither reverses the broader dementia association nor demonstrates that hydrocephalus was the mechanism (p997).

A second study examined **more than 4,000** autopsies from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center. The investigators assessed neuritic plaques with CERAD measures and neurofibrillary pathology with Braak staging. They did not find a TBI association with the Alzheimer changes studied; several other examined lesions, including hippocampal sclerosis and amyloid angiopathy, also showed no association in that sample. When explaining the result, specify whether the outcome is a diagnosis made during life, the microscopic changes typical of Alzheimer disease, or all-cause dementia. Those three endpoints need not behave alike (p997).

Small studies after a single moderate or severe injury provide a different type of evidence. In one series of **39** survivors examined **1–47 years** after injury, amyloid plaques were denser than in controls. Among participants aged 60 or younger at death, tangles were more frequent than in age-matched controls and tended to appear in an unusual superficial and sulcal distribution. An imaging study in **nine** people after severe TBI also found increased amyloid tracer accumulation, in a pattern different from that of the Alzheimer comparison group. These results justify asking where deposits occur as well as whether they are present. Neither a positive tracer image nor the presence of tangles alone identifies CTE (p998).

The chapter's individual long-term survivors illustrate why tissue findings may resist a single disease label. Reported findings included severe atrophy, white-matter degeneration, diffuse amyloid plaques, amyloid angiopathy and unusual distributions of tau. One individual also had atypical alpha-synuclein and TDP-43 findings. Even with superficial tangles suggestive of CTE, the diagnostic perivascular lesion was absent in the described cases. A report can accurately identify multiple abnormalities while leaving uncertain which one drove the patient's symptoms. Assigning a familiar label to every stain would give an unjustified impression of diagnostic certainty (p998).

### Axons, inflammation and parkinsonism

Axonal injury occurs across injury severities. Long after a moderate or severe TBI, investigators have described reduced callosal thickness, persistent microglial changes and disruption of the blood–brain barrier. In one series, **47%** of individuals surviving at least a year after a single moderate or severe injury had evidence of chronic barrier disruption. This was a tissue finding in a selected group, not the probability that a person with a remote concussion will develop a clinical disorder. Vascular injury, white-matter damage and inflammation provide plausible contributions to disability in addition to disease-associated protein deposits (p998–999).

When interpreting the Parkinson data, pay attention to who acted as the comparator. The reported **44% higher risk** was for a later Parkinson diagnosis among middle-aged and older patients with TBI compared with people who had sustained trauma outside the brain. It is a relative difference between groups, not a 44% absolute chance of Parkinson disease. Severity and repeated injury strengthened the association. Recall bias remains possible, and early movement impairment may itself make an injury more likely. Those limitations matter particularly when a fall and a Parkinson diagnosis occur close together in later life (p999).

The findings also depended on which cohort and endpoint were examined. In the ROSMAP/ACT analysis, loss of consciousness for **less than an hour** was associated with a higher risk of cortical Lewy bodies, while loss for **more than an hour** was associated with cerebral microinfarcts. In the ACT cohort, the longer-duration exposure was also associated with a clinical Parkinson diagnosis. Do not turn these observations into a duration rule for diagnosing Parkinson disease after trauma. The national autopsy series described on p997 did not reproduce every association with Lewy pathology. Different case definitions and outcomes help explain why both results appear in the same chapter (p997, p999).

### Evaluating a living patient with a repetitive-impact history

The observed syndrome can include problems in behavior, mood, cognition and movement. Earlier symptoms described include headache, loss of concentration, short-term memory trouble and mood change. Later findings can include impaired judgment, paranoia, parkinsonism, gait difficulty or visuospatial problems. The authors describe behavioral and mood presentations more often in younger patients and cognitive and motor findings more prominently in older adults. Neither the age pattern nor any one symptom is sufficiently specific to identify CTE during life (p997, p1000).

At the visit, characterize these domains separately. Changes in planning or impulse control may be more evident from an informant's description than from a memory complaint. Examination for parkinsonism, language change, fasciculations or other motor-neuron findings can point toward alternative or coexisting disease. Neuropsychological testing can describe strengths and weaknesses in attention, learning, language and visuospatial function. Laboratory tests and brain imaging can investigate other explanations. A structural scan, an abnormal cognitive test, amyloid or tau PET, or a CSF result cannot by itself demonstrate the necessary microscopic arrangement of neuronal tau around a vessel in a cortical sulcus (p997, p1000, p1004–1005).

In the related May 2024 behavioral-management item, the practical task is to investigate a new behavior. Establish what the patient actually does, what preceded it, how the patient and caregiver are affected and whether there has been a change in the environment. During **Investigate**, consider pain, constipation, illness or a medication effect. The next task is to create a plan and then evaluate its implementation and effect. A caregiver who can describe the precipitating circumstances may help find a reversible cause even when the underlying dementia diagnosis remains uncertain. The DICE teaching is located in Chapter 60 on printed p924; it is a related exam item rather than a diagnostic criterion from Chapter 64 (p924, p1000).

### Read the histology in order: cell, vessel and cortical location

The diagnosis requires a particular spatial relationship. Find phosphorylated tau in neurons with abnormal neurites around a small vessel **at the depth of a cortical sulcus**, extending into the parenchyma rather than confined to the superficial margin. Associated thorn-shaped astrocytes may be present. Figure 64-1 uses a stage II example to show both the focal perivascular lesions and changes in neighboring cortex. Superficial cortical tangles provide support, but superficial tangles alone do not substitute for the lesion. The chapter contrasts their distribution with the tendency toward cortical layers III and V in Alzheimer disease (p997–998, p1004).

Figure 64-2 compares the three-repeat and four-repeat forms of tau in frontal and temporal tissue. It shows scattered neurons on the three-repeat images, more widespread neuronal and astrocytic staining in some four-repeat views, and labeling of both forms with a broader p-tau stain. The hippocampal panels also show tau immunoreactivity. The figure explains how different stains can show different components of the same process. The diagnosis depends on the cellular and regional pattern, not simply on choosing a positive three-repeat or four-repeat stain (p999).

Astrocytic tau deserves particular attention in older brains. Thorn-shaped astrocytes can be found beneath the pia, around vessels or in white matter in age-related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG). The consensus group specifically excluded **purely astrocytic** perivascular lesions from the definition of CTE. In a European series of **310** older people, ARTAG was found in **38%** and CTE in none. A report that describes perivascular tau without identifying the cell type and the position in the cortical fold does not give enough information to classify the finding (p1004).

### Stage and severity answer different questions

Table 64-1 and Figure 64-3 are easiest to read as a widening anatomical distribution. In McKee **stage I**, there are one or two cortical foci, commonly frontal, with tau tangles and dot- or thread-like neurites at a sulcal depth. Sparse tangles can also appear in the locus coeruleus. **Stage II** has three or more lesions in multiple cortical regions, with extension into nearby superficial cortex; tangles in the locus coeruleus and nucleus basalis become more evident. What changes is both the number of cortical epicenters and the spread away from them. The illustrated stage II medial temporal lobe is comparatively spared (p1000–1002).

In **stage III**, cortical lesions are more numerous and confluent. Figure 64-4 adds a distinguishing feature: substantial involvement of the medial temporal lobe, including hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and amygdala. Deep structures can also be involved. In **stage IV**, Figure 64-5 shows extensive cortical and medial temporal involvement, loss of neurons and prominent glial change. Other pathology can coexist, including widespread TDP-43 abnormalities and degeneration of myelin and axons. The paired figures let the learner distinguish progression across networks from merely finding another isolated patch in the frontal cortex (p1000–1003).

The later consensus approach deals with a practical problem: whole-hemisphere sections used in developing the four-stage scheme are not always available. The table gives a simplified distinction between **low** and **high** CTE once a diagnostic lesion is present. Fewer than **5** of the listed features favors low severity; **5 or more** favors high severity. Features include tangles on the side or crest of the adjacent gyrus, in superficial cortex, in hippocampal CA4 or CA2, and in the entorhinal cortex, amygdala, thalamus, mammillary body and cerebellar dentate nucleus. This is a count of specified distribution features. It is not a requirement to find five separate causal injuries or five diagnostic lesions before calling the disease (p1001, p1004).

A **single** qualifying cortical lesion was accepted as the minimum for pathological diagnosis. The binary severity classification broadly corresponds to stages I–II and III–IV. Diagnosis, extent and clinical severity therefore need to be reported separately. A person may have a diagnostic lesion of low extent, while another may have widespread pathology from CTE and additional disease. The summary paragraph's parenthetical “I–VI” conflicts with the four stages illustrated and tabulated in the chapter. The stage scheme actually described runs from **I to IV** (p1000–1005).

### What selected samples can establish

In one blinded exercise, expert neuropathologists reviewed **25 cases** representing several tauopathies without access to clinical or demographic information. A second blinded sample included **27 cases**, **17** of them CTE of varying severity. These exercises addressed agreement about an anatomic lesion and ways to classify its extent. They asked a different question from how often a person exposed to contact sport will develop disease. Similarly, in the Mayo brain-bank analysis, **21 of 66** selected contact-sport athletes met criteria, compared with **none of 198** controls without head-trauma or contact-sport histories. Reproducible classification in a bank is valuable; the method of donation and selection still limits any inference about population prevalence (p1001–1004).

Duration of a football career correlated with stage among the donated brains studied. Age at death and time since retirement were also associated with greater burden. Number of recalled concussions, playing position, years of education and lifetime steroid use did not show the same relationship to stage in the study described. A separate analysis of **177** former American football players with CTE found increasing regional tau burden across stages. The results support progressive pathology in many affected individuals, but a series collected at death does not follow one person's brain through each stage. Some individuals had relatively mild pathology at an advanced age (p1000, p1004–1005).

### Explaining the result to a family

A family may ask whether a remote head injury explains a new dementia and whether a scan will tell them “how much CTE” is present. Give separate answers. Prior TBI is relevant to risk and to the history of function over time. Some causes of cognitive and motor change can be characterized or treated during life. The defining pattern and formal staging of CTE, however, require neuropathological examination. The chapter provides no validated CTE-specific drug, target dose or imaging threshold for assigning a living patient to a stage. Clinical care can still address the observed cognitive, behavioral and mobility problems, coexisting illness, safety and caregiver concerns. That distinction is especially important when exposure is clear but the syndrome could also arise from Alzheimer disease, Lewy body disease, vascular injury or a mixture (p997, p999–1000, p1004–1005).
The history of diagnostic methods helps explain why older estimates are hard to compare. Some earlier brain-bank examinations used methods directed toward Alzheimer pathology. The revised criteria require looking for neuronal phosphorylated tau in the specified anatomic relationship. In the small set of re-examined banks cited in the chapter, estimates ranged from **1% to 30%**; some cases previously labeled Alzheimer disease alone had both pathologies on re-examination. The range reflects selected samples and differences in what was sought. It cannot be used to tell a former athlete their personal probability of developing CTE (p998).

When someone already has dementia, an additional microscopic diagnosis need not explain the entire syndrome. Progressive vascular disease, Lewy pathology and Alzheimer changes can affect how rapidly a given person loses function. This matters when relating stage to disability in a donor series: measured tau burden, the patient's premortem symptoms and the presence of other diseases are separate observations, all worth retaining in the explanation (p998–999, p1004–1005).

## Exam traps

- A cohort association with all-cause dementia does not identify Alzheimer neuropathology in an individual (p997–998).
- Keep the nonbrain-trauma comparator when quoting the reported increase in Parkinson diagnosis after TBI (p999).
- The number of recognized concussions can underestimate repetitive impact exposure and was not the measure associated with stage in the donation study (p999–1000).
- A single diagnostic lesion can establish CTE at autopsy. The low/high feature count describes its extent (p1001, p1004).
- Perivascular astrocytic tau alone and superficial cortical tangles alone each lack a required feature of the defining neuronal lesion (p997, p1004).
- In the exam's DICE example, constipation belongs to investigation of a contributor; the mnemonic comes from Chapter 60 (p924).


**Clinical implications & exam traps**
- Do not equate all post-TBI dementia with either Alzheimer pathology or CTE; distinguish single injury from repetitive impact exposure (p997–999).
- Selected brain-bank case frequencies are not population prevalence estimates; the chapter notes that prevalence in neurodegenerative brain banks remains uncertain (p998).
- The chapter emphasizes diagnostic uncertainty and biomarker development; it does not provide an established CTE-specific drug regimen (p1005).
