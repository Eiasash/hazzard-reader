# Chapter 64 Study: Traumatic Brain Injury and Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy

## Summary

**TBI & later-life disease**
- TBI is heterogeneous: characterize severity, loss of consciousness, amnesia, frequency, injury mechanism and associated hemorrhage or other damage (p997).
- TBI of varying severity is associated with later dementia, but the underlying pathology is not established as simply Alzheimer disease (p997).
- A cited study found **44%** greater subsequent Parkinson diagnosis risk after TBI than after nonbrain trauma in middle-aged/older patients; severity and frequency influenced risk (p999).

**Chronic traumatic encephalopathy**
- CTE is a primary tauopathy associated with repetitive head impacts, including concussive and subconcussive exposure (p997, p999).
- Exposures include contact sports, military blast and repeated physical abuse; the amount/duration of exposure matters (p997, p999).
- The characteristic lesion is irregular perivascular phosphorylated tau in neurons and neurites at cortical sulcal depths (p997, p1004).
- Purely astrocytic perivascular tau, including age-related tau astrogliopathy, does not meet the pathological definition of CTE (p1004).
- Early CTE need not contain amyloid; amyloid deposition may appear with aging alongside the tau pathology (p1004).

**Assessment & progression**
- Behavioral, cognitive, dementia and motor presentations are nonspecific; no imaging, laboratory or cognitive test definitively diagnoses CTE during life (p997).
- Definitive diagnosis requires postmortem neuropathology; clinical symptoms or exposure history alone cannot establish it (p1005).
- Pathology generally becomes more extensive over time, involving medial temporal and deep brain structures; some individuals have an indolent course even at advanced age (p1004–1005).
- Coexisting disease can contribute to symptom progression; TBI may also cause axonal loss, white-matter degeneration and persistent inflammation (p998–999, p1005).

### What the dementia cohorts actually measured

May 2024 Q63 accepted both Alzheimer disease and normal pressure hydrocephalus. The cited page describes a broader association with subsequent dementia after injuries of varying severity. In a pooled cohort of **7130 participants**, a history of TBI with loss of consciousness did not correlate with a clinical Alzheimer diagnosis or Alzheimer neuropathological change at autopsy. An independent series of **more than 4000** autopsies also found no association with Alzheimer pathology. The cited page does not present normal pressure hydrocephalus as a result of those studies (p997).

The exposure and the outcome determine what a comparison means. A single severe injury can leave lasting axonal and white-matter damage. Repeated impacts, including ones without a recognized concussion, have a separate association with CTE. An epidemiologic diagnosis of dementia and a specific microscopic lesion are different outcomes (p997–999).

### Following the tau distribution

Look for phosphorylated tau in **neurons** around a small vessel deep in a cortical sulcus. The earliest lesions are focal. With greater pathological burden, lesions appear at multiple sulcal depths and extend into neighboring superficial cortex. More advanced stages involve the medial temporal lobe and other regions. Staging describes distribution at examination; it cannot establish when an individual patient's symptoms began or how rapidly they will progress (p1000–1005).

The map also assigns May 2024 Q66 to this chapter because the exam's chapter column says 64. Its cited page is in Chapter 60. In DICE, first describe the episode, then investigate causes such as pain, medication effects or constipation. Create a plan with the patient and caregiver, then evaluate whether it was tried and helped. Constipation belongs to **Investigate** (Chapter 60, p924).

**Clinical implications & exam traps**
- Do not equate all post-TBI dementia with either Alzheimer pathology or CTE; distinguish single injury from repetitive impact exposure (p997–999).
- Selected brain-bank case frequencies are not population prevalence estimates; the chapter notes that prevalence in neurodegenerative brain banks remains uncertain (p998).
- The chapter emphasizes diagnostic uncertainty and biomarker development; it does not provide an established CTE-specific drug regimen (p1005).

## Recall drill

**1. Which features should be documented when characterizing a TBI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Severity, Glasgow Coma Scale, duration of loss of consciousness and amnesia, frequency, mechanism, focal or diffuse injury, and accompanying lesions (p997).</details>

**2. What conclusion about later cognition is supported across injuries of different severities?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>TBI is associated with increased risk of subsequent dementia. The pathology responsible is heterogeneous and incompletely defined (p997).</details>

**3. What did the pooled study of people with TBI and loss of consciousness find about Alzheimer disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Neither a clinical Alzheimer diagnosis nor Alzheimer pathology at autopsy was associated with this exposure in that cohort (p997).</details>

**4. How should the May exam's accepted Alzheimer answer be interpreted?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It received credit alongside normal pressure hydrocephalus. The cited book page reports a broader dementia association and negative Alzheimer-specific studies (p997).</details>

**5. Which injury history is associated with CTE?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Repetitive head impacts, including repeated subconcussive impacts that produce no recognized concussion (p997, p999).</details>

**6. What is the defining cortical lesion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Irregularly distributed neuronal phosphorylated tau around small vessels at the depths of cortical sulci (p997, p1004).</details>

**7. Can perivascular astrocytic tau alone establish CTE?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Age-related tau astrogliopathy can produce astrocytic deposits. Neuronal involvement is required (p1004).</details>

**8. Must amyloid plaques be present for CTE?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. They may appear with aging or coexisting pathology, and early CTE can lack them (p1004).</details>

**9. How does the pattern change from focal early disease to a more extensive stage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Additional sulci become involved, with local spread to superficial cortex and, in more advanced disease, wider cortical and medial temporal involvement (p1000–1003).</details>

**10. What other long-term changes have been described after significant TBI?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Axonal and white-matter degeneration, blood–brain barrier disruption and persistent inflammatory changes (p998–999).</details>

**11. Which comparator was used in the study reporting greater subsequent Parkinson risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Middle-aged and older people with nonbrain trauma. The TBI group had a <strong>44% higher risk</strong> of a Parkinson diagnosis in that study (p999).</details>

**12. Why might retrospective estimates of postinjury Parkinson risk be misleading?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Recall bias and reverse causation can affect the association; more severe or frequent injuries were associated with greater risk in the cited study (p999).</details>

**13. Can cognitive or behavioral symptoms diagnose CTE in a living former athlete?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No definitive clinical, imaging or laboratory test is presented. The chapter requires postmortem neuropathological assessment (p997, p1005).</details>

**14. Why avoid using brain-bank series to estimate an exposed person's odds of CTE?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Brains entering such series are selected. The observed frequency does not supply a population denominator (p998).</details>

**15. In the DICE item cross-listed to this chapter, which step includes checking for constipation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Investigate. Describe characterizes the behavior, Create builds a plan, and Evaluate checks implementation and response (Chapter 60, p924).</details>
