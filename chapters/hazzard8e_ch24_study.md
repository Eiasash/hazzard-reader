# Chapter 24 Study: Integrative Medicine and Health

## Summary

**Whole-person assessment**
- Integrative care combines conventional and complementary approaches; ask directly about use, stress, beliefs, support and supplements (p329-331).
- Weigh benefits against polypharmacy, product variability and drug–supplement interactions; disclosure is often incomplete (p331-332).

**Supplements: evidence and cautions**
- Vitamin D has bone benefit in institutionalized older adults; cognitive and other benefits remain uncertain. Magnesium evidence varies by indication (p332).
- Fish oil: **1000–2000 mg/day** EPA/DHA is discussed for potential cardiovascular benefit, but trials conflict; no convincing Alzheimer-treatment benefit (p332, p334).
- Coenzyme Q10: **100 mg three times daily** improved outcomes in one moderate–severe reduced-EF heart-failure trial as an adjunct to standard treatment; well tolerated (p334).
- Ginkgo: **240 mg/day** standardized extract may help selected cognitive symptoms; evidence conflicts. Use cautiously with bleeding disorders or anticoagulants (p334-335).
- Vitamin E does not prevent MCI progression; some high-dose meta-analyses suggest increased mortality (p335).
- Glucosamine/chondroitin: generally not recommended for osteoarthritis in the guidance; hand-OA chondroitin is a conditional exception, **800 mg/day** in one trial (p335).
- SAM-e: **200–1600 mg/day** in divided doses depending on indication/route; empty-stomach absorption and possible mania matter (p335-336).
- Melatonin: start **0.3–2 mg** immediate release **1 hour** before bed; long-term adverse-effect evidence is limited (p336).
- Valerian: **300–900 mg** standardized extract before bed; effects may take weeks, studies conflict, and severe liver disease warrants caution (p336-337).
- Black cohosh: studied Remifemin **20 mg once or twice daily**; heterogeneous evidence, uncertain long-term safety and caution when estrogen is contraindicated (p337).
- Saw palmetto: **160 mg twice daily**; formulation-dependent, conflicting results, usually mild adverse effects (p337).

**Movement and manual approaches**
- Tai chi reduces falls in meta-analyses; yoga and meditation may help selected physical/psychological outcomes, with variable evidence (p338-340).
- Acupuncture has its strongest evidence for chronic pain; chiropractic evidence in older adults is more limited, and cervical manipulation has rare serious risks (p340-341).
- Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese Medicine combine multiple approaches; align any chosen modality with the person's goals and conventional care (p342).

### Interpreting a supplement study

Check who was enrolled, what exact product they took, what it was compared with and which outcome was measured. Trials of EPA alone and mixed EPA/DHA can involve different patients, doses and comparators. Cognitive and cardiovascular outcomes are separate questions (p332, p334).

For difficulty sleeping, the book describes starting immediate-release melatonin about an hour before bed and notes uncertainty about prolonged use. The formulation, indication and timing matter when interpreting a patient's report of benefit (p336).

> Current practice (not the book): For chronic insomnia disorder, the guideline favors cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and suggests against melatonin or valerian. - [VA/DoD chronic insomnia guideline, recommendations 8 and 14, 2025](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Guideline_final_20250915.pdf).

When falls are the concern, the case integrates physical therapy, home safety and review of sedating medicines with movement-based options. Someone who asks about a supplement for balance may also have a modifiable drug exposure or a home hazard (p339, p343).

### Start with the outcome the older adult is trying to achieve

In Figure 24-1 the older adult is at the center of a comprehensive geriatric assessment that includes gait, frailty, cognition, sleep, mood, medicines, health behavior and social support. The surrounding domains of mind, body, community and spirit explain why an intervention can have value beyond the symptom for which it was first considered. A supervised movement class may provide balance practice and social contact. Conversely, an additional capsule for “healthy aging” can worsen pill burden without affecting the activity the patient wants to preserve. Define the intended outcome before deciding which evidence is relevant (p329–331).

Figure 24-2 separates four broad categories. Dietary supplements include botanicals, vitamins and other ingested products. Mind-body practices include yoga, tai chi, qigong and meditation. Manual approaches include chiropractic care, massage, osteopathic techniques and acupuncture as grouped by the authors. Ayurveda and traditional Chinese medicine are whole systems incorporating several approaches. A positive trial of a specific movement program does not validate an ingested product from the same tradition. Equally, an adverse effect of one herbal formulation does not establish the risk of a supervised exercise class. Identify the actual intervention before assigning benefit or harm (p329–331).

Table 24-1 broadens the history. Ask what the patient thinks is causing the problem, what stress or support affects it, what has already been tried and which conventional or complementary practitioners are involved. Include nutrition, activity, sleep, substance use, readiness for change and beliefs that help the patient cope. In the medication history, name teas, powders, vitamins, herbal preparations and products obtained without a prescription. Ask to see the package, including concentration and all ingredients, and establish how the patient actually uses it. A patient can reasonably answer “no” to taking additional medicines while taking several products regarded as food or as part of a daily routine (p331–332).

### What a label, a resource listing and a trial can actually establish

The chapter describes U.S. dietary supplements as a category distinct from approved medicines for specific diseases. Manufacturing requirements and adverse-event reporting do not establish clinical benefit before a product is sold. A brand name can encompass preparations with different constituent amounts or extracts. Record dose, formulation, indication, frequency and perceived effect alongside other medicines. This is particularly important for a person taking anticoagulants, psychoactive drugs or multiple products with overlapping ingredients. An interaction may affect risk even when the supplement itself has little evidence of efficacy for the advertised use (p331–333).

Table 24-2 lists resources for different questions. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements provides ingredient information, while NCCIH covers complementary approaches more broadly. A database of natural products can help with proposed interactions. Independent testing can help distinguish contents or quality but does not prove a treatment works. Other entries help locate movement programs, instructors, credentialed practitioners or integrative clinical centers. In practice, separate four questions: what is in the product, what clinical outcome has been tested, what adverse effects or combinations matter here, and whether an appropriate practitioner or feasible program is available (p332–333).

### Cardiovascular and nutritional products: specify the population and endpoint

Older adults may have less dietary vitamin D, less skin synthesis and less sun exposure. The chapter's support for skeletal benefit in institutionalized older adults is narrower than a claim about cognition, mood or every community-dwelling adult. Observational links between deficiency and cognitive performance do not show that supplements restore cognition. Similarly, magnesium intake is associated with blood pressure in observational work, but intervention studies have not shown consistent benefit for hypertension. Serum magnesium may poorly reflect intracellular and bone stores. The book discusses more favorable evidence for migraine prevention and uncertainty in osteoporosis; it directs readers elsewhere for a full treatment discussion of multivitamins. A mineral's physiological importance alone does not establish an indication for additional supplementation (p332).

For fish oil, distinguish EPA alone from products combining EPA and DHA. Consider whether the study enrolled people receiving statins, what their baseline cardiovascular risk was, whether the placebo was mineral or olive oil, and whether the outcome was infarction, all major cardiovascular events, cancer or cognition. In VITAL the **840-mg** omega-3 regimen did not reduce major cardiovascular events or cancer over the **5-year** follow-up summarized. The chapter discusses possible benefit for some coronary outcomes at higher doses and mentions **1000–2000 mg/day** of EPA/DHA as a dose considered by the authors. It does not establish that every product with “fish oil” on its label delivers this amount of active fatty acids or prevents cardiovascular events in an individual patient (p332–334).

> Current practice (not the book): The EMA identified a dose-dependent increase in atrial fibrillation with omega-3-acid ethyl esters in people with cardiovascular disease or risk factors. The risk was greatest at 4 g/day. [EMA pharmacovigilance committee, 2023](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/meeting-highlights-pharmacovigilance-risk-assessment-committee-prac-25-28-september-2023).

The depression and cognition literature asks still different questions. The chapter describes mixed depression results in older adults, differences in the EPA:DHA ratio, and a possible signal at doses above **1.5 g** in some studies. Evidence of benefit for treatment of Alzheimer disease was unconvincing. A patient who reports better mood on a product has described a meaningful experience, but that experience cannot answer whether the same product prevents cognitive decline. An added supplement can be unnecessary even when a potential benefit remains biologically plausible, as the patient and team decide in the case at the end of the chapter (p334, p342–343).

Coenzyme Q10 participates in mitochondrial energy production; statins can lower its synthesis. The chapter reviews reports of improved statin-associated muscle symptoms, including a meta-analysis using doses ranging from **100 to 600 mg/day** for approximately one to three months. That literature cannot supply the optimal dose or duration for every patient. The separate heart-failure trial used **100 mg three times daily** in moderate to severe heart failure with *reduced* ejection fraction as an adjunct to standard therapy. Major cardiovascular events were fewer at two years in that trial. Do not transfer that result to prevention in asymptomatic adults or assume it applies to preserved-EF heart failure simply because both are called heart failure (p334).

### Memory, osteoarthritis and mood: distinguish symptomatic relief from prevention

For ginkgo the studied material is often the standardized extract EGb761. In dementia trials, **240 mg/day** over about **22–24 weeks** was associated in some analyses with improvements in behavioral or psychological symptoms and less caregiver distress. Long-term administration and prevention of dementia are separate questions. Clinical trials did not establish excess bleeding, but case reports and plausible effects on platelet pathways warrant caution with bleeding disorders, aspirin, NSAIDs, warfarin or other anticoagulants. Asking about those exposures is more useful than simply writing “ginkgo” on a list without a formulation or indication (p334–335).

Vitamin E illustrates how an outcome and indication can become detached from the evidence. Trials have not established prevention of progression from mild cognitive impairment, and some meta-analyses associate high-dose use with increased all-cause mortality. The book separately mentions **800 IU/day** of alpha-tocopherol in earlier guidance for selected *nondiabetic patients with biopsy-proven nonalcoholic steatohepatitis*. This is a liver-specific example rather than a memory regimen. In a patient taking vitamin E for nonspecific cognitive protection alongside other medicines, ask whether continued use advances a stated goal and whether stopping a low-value product would simplify the regimen (p335, p342–343).

Glucosamine and chondroitin are commonly taken together for joints, but joint location and standardized preparation matter. The book cites guidance against glucosamine, chondroitin or their combination for hand, hip or knee osteoarthritis and identifies a conditional exception for chondroitin in hand osteoarthritis, based on a single trial using **800 mg daily**. It does not follow that a combination marketed for knee pain has the same effect. SAM-e is another product considered for osteoarthritis and mood. A history suggesting bipolar disorder changes the risk assessment because mania has been reported. Review whether apparent improvement in energy is accompanied by a change in sleep or behavior (p335–336).

### Sleep, vasomotor symptoms and urinary symptoms

Melatonin timing and formulation alter exposure. The chapter describes **0.3–2 mg** of immediate-release melatonin about **1 hour** before bedtime as a starting regimen and quotes a maximum of **9–10 mg**; that upper value is not a target for all older adults. Some studies in adults over 55 used **2 mg** one to two hours before bedtime, and evidence for prolonged use is limited. Establish whether the complaint is impaired sleep onset, nighttime waking, circadian timing or daytime sleepiness before interpreting a response. Reassess other sedating medicines, actual morning function and fall risk when discussing the book regimen and the chronic-insomnia guideline update above (p336).

For valerian, the chapter describes **300–900 mg** of extract standardized to **0.8% valerenic acid** or **2–3 g** of dried root as tea, taken **30 minutes to 2 hours** before bed. These formulations cannot be compared by capsule weight alone. Trials vary in extracts, outcome measures and duration. A patient expecting a reliable immediate hypnotic effect is asking a different question from a study evaluating symptoms over several weeks. The chapter calls for caution in severe liver disease. Neither valerian's botanical origin nor combining it with another sleep product removes the need to review next-day symptoms and other drugs (p336–337).

For menopausal symptoms the best-known studied black cohosh product in the chapter is Remifemin, a **20-mg** tablet taken **once or twice daily**. A Cochrane review of heterogeneous studies at a median **40 mg/day** did not find sufficient high-quality evidence for reliably reducing hot flashes or other menopausal symptoms. Distinguish an uncertain aggregate finding from a proven absence of effect in every individual, and do not assume results for one preparation apply to combinations. The authors advise caution when estrogen treatment is contraindicated. For lower urinary tract symptoms, saw palmetto is discussed at **160 mg twice daily**, with about **8 weeks** to assess response. One study escalating from 320 to 960 mg/day did not outperform placebo, while evidence for a particular hexanic extract differed. Dose escalation alone cannot resolve differences between extracts (p337).

### Movement and attention: choose a feasible practice

Yoga combines postures, breathing and attention, with differing emphasis across forms. In older populations some studies report better balance, strength, flexibility or quality of life. The comparison group matters: in one falls study the yoga group improved from its own baseline, but it did not differ significantly from an exercise control. Ask whether the person can safely assume and leave a position and whether a trained instructor can adapt it. Someone unable to get down to a mat may still participate in a modified or chair-based practice. The intended function and whether improvement exceeds a comparable activity are more informative than the word “yoga” alone (p337–339).

Tai chi links weight transfer, controlled movement, balance, attention and breathing. Meta-analyses cited in the chapter reported reductions in falls of roughly **20%–45%**. In a study of people at high risk, a twice-weekly program over **24 weeks** reduced falls by **58%** compared with stretching. Those estimates belong to specific programs and ambulatory populations. Training may address reduced strength, impaired proprioception, coordination and fear of falling at the same time. A person with orthostatic symptoms, sedating medication or a dangerous home environment still needs evaluation of those contributors alongside practice. Consider the support needed to attend and continue the class (p338–339, p343).

Meditation includes several attentional practices; mindfulness-based programs often combine more than silent meditation. Some studies report improvements in anxiety, depressive symptoms, fatigue or pain-related outcomes, but populations, program intensity and comparators differ. The chapter describes reduced anxiety in some cancer populations and stresses the limited evidence for several other indications. Specify whether the goal is reduced distress, less fatigue or relief of pain. Patient preference, cultural fit, willingness to practice and the ability to follow the instruction influence whether even a well-studied program can help this individual (p339–340).

### Manual therapies and whole medical systems

Chiropractic care often includes advice and exercise as well as spinal manipulation. Studies of chronic low back pain and mechanical neck pain therefore do not all test the same component. Among older adults in the studies described, adding manipulation to home exercise produced some improvements in pain. Extending a combined program from **12 to 36 weeks** did not produce an additional important disability reduction in the comparison, although some secondary outcomes improved. Nonserious musculoskeletal symptoms can follow treatment, and rare serious events are a specific concern with cervical manipulation. Separate the anatomical region, technique, treatment duration and functional outcome when counseling someone considering care (p340–341).

Acupuncture has stronger evidence for chronic pain than for many of its other proposed applications. An individual-participant meta-analysis summarized results from **39 trials** involving **20,827** people with conditions including musculoskeletal pain, headache and osteoarthritis. Benefits persisted beyond treatment in the studies discussed, but the comparison with sham procedures is a different contrast from adding treatment to usual care. Ask which pain limits the patient, whether relief would improve participation in a valued activity and whether attending treatment is practical. Combine assessment of benefit with a review of concurrent therapy and the practitioner delivering the treatment (p341–342).

Ayurveda groups individualized dietary, exercise, lifestyle and other treatments within a medical system originating in the Indian subcontinent. Research on a single herb does not test the entire approach. The chapter warns that some preparations contain metals or minerals with potential for toxicity. Traditional Chinese medicine likewise includes diet, herbs, movement, manual methods and acupuncture. Studies of a whole treatment package cannot identify which component caused a change. Ask for the full preparation and the other treatments given; respect the person's beliefs while assessing dose, interactions and plausible benefit separately for each exposure (p342).

### Apply the framework to the chapter's independence goal

In the clinical case, an older woman living after a stroke wants to remain independent in the community. A Timed Up and Go of **15 seconds**, a MoCA of **24/30**, mild depressive symptoms and limited social support raise different questions. Ask whether a proposed intervention addresses a limiting function, whether she can carry it out, and whether solving one problem will worsen another. Figure 24-3 connects physical therapy and a home-safety assessment to fall risk, tai chi to movement and social participation, and review of sedating medicines to gait and frailty. Nutritional support, assessment of orthostasis, psychotherapy and community services each target other identified barriers (p331, p342–343).

The figure also illustrates a decision to remove rather than add treatment. Nonessential vitamin E and the sedating burden of gabapentin deserve review; fish oil was discussed but the patient elected not to add it in the context of polypharmacy. The team can follow change in gait, confidence, independence and willingness to continue the plan over time. A patient who needs fewer burdensome medicines and can again participate in a meaningful activity has reached an outcome more relevant to her original request than simply increasing the number of “integrative” treatments prescribed (p342–343).

## Exam traps

- A within-group improvement in yoga participants is not evidence of superiority to an active exercise control (p338).
- CoQ10 heart-failure data cited here are adjunctive in reduced-EF disease; formulation and population matter for interpretation (p334).
- Compare dose of active EPA/DHA, not total fish-oil capsule mass; prevention of cardiovascular events and treatment of depression or dementia are separate endpoints (p332–334).
- The upper melatonin dose quoted in the book is not its suggested starting dose; the current chronic-insomnia recommendation is separately labeled (p336).
- A result with a standardized botanical extract applies most directly to that preparation, dose and indication (p334–335, p337).
- Improvement in symptoms of established disease and prevention of new disease require different trial outcomes (p334–335).
- A positive finding for hand osteoarthritis cannot be transferred automatically to hip or knee osteoarthritis (p335).
