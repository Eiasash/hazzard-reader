# Chapter 24 Study: Integrative Medicine and Health

## Summary

**Whole-person assessment**
- Integrative care combines conventional and complementary approaches; ask directly about use, stress, beliefs, support and supplements (p329-331).
- Weigh benefits against polypharmacy, product variability and drug–supplement interactions; disclosure is often incomplete (p331-332).

**Supplements: evidence and cautions**
- Vitamin D has bone benefit in institutionalized older adults; cognitive and other benefits remain uncertain. Magnesium evidence varies by indication (p332).
- Fish oil: **1000–2000 mg/day** EPA/DHA is discussed for potential cardiovascular benefit, but trials conflict; no convincing Alzheimer-treatment benefit (p332, p334).
- Coenzyme Q10: **100 mg three times daily** improved outcomes in one moderate–severe reduced-EF heart-failure trial as an adjunct to standard treatment; well tolerated (p334).
- Ginkgo: **240 mg/day** standardized extract may help selected cognitive symptoms; evidence conflicts. Use cautiously with bleeding disorders or anticoagulants (p334-335).
- Vitamin E does not prevent MCI progression; some high-dose meta-analyses suggest increased mortality (p335).
- Glucosamine/chondroitin: generally not recommended for osteoarthritis in the cited guidance; hand-OA chondroitin is a conditional exception, **800 mg/day** in one trial (p335).
- SAM-e: **200–1600 mg/day** in divided doses depending on indication/route; empty-stomach absorption and possible mania matter (p335-336).
- Melatonin: start **0.3–2 mg** immediate release **1 hour** before bed; long-term adverse-effect evidence is limited (p336).
- Valerian: **300–900 mg** standardized extract before bed; effects may take weeks, studies conflict, and severe liver disease warrants caution (p336-337).
- Black cohosh: studied Remifemin **20 mg once or twice daily**; heterogeneous evidence, uncertain long-term safety and caution when estrogen is contraindicated (p337).
- Saw palmetto: **160 mg twice daily**; formulation-dependent, conflicting results, usually mild adverse effects (p337).

**Movement and manual approaches**
- Tai chi reduces falls in meta-analyses; yoga and meditation may help selected physical/psychological outcomes, with variable evidence (p338-340).
- Acupuncture has its strongest evidence for chronic pain; chiropractic evidence in older adults is more limited, and cervical manipulation has rare serious risks (p340-341).
- Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese Medicine combine multiple approaches; align any chosen modality with the person's goals and conventional care (p342).

### Interpreting a supplement study

Check who was enrolled, what exact product they took, what it was compared with and which outcome was measured. In VITAL, **840 mg** of omega-3 fatty acids did not lower major cardiovascular events or cancer incidence relative to placebo over **5 years**. Trials of EPA alone and mixed EPA/DHA can involve different patients, doses and comparators. Cognitive and cardiovascular outcomes are separate questions (p332, p334).

For difficulty sleeping, the book describes starting immediate-release melatonin about an hour before bed and notes uncertainty about prolonged use. The formulation, indication and timing matter when interpreting a patient's report of benefit (p336).

> Current practice (not the book): For chronic insomnia disorder, the guideline favors cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and suggests against melatonin or valerian. - [VA/DoD chronic insomnia guideline, recommendations 8 and 14, 2025](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/HEALTHQUALITY/guidelines/CD/insomnia/I-OSA-CPG_2025-Guideline_final_20250915.pdf).

When falls are the concern, the case integrates physical therapy, home safety and review of sedating medicines with movement-based options. Tai chi combines attention, balance and movement practice. Someone who asks about a supplement for balance may also have a modifiable drug exposure or a home hazard (p339, p343).

## Exam traps

- A result with a standardized botanical extract applies most directly to that preparation, dose and indication (p334–335, p337).
- Improvement in symptoms of established disease and prevention of new disease require different trial outcomes (p334–335).
- A positive finding for hand osteoarthritis cannot be transferred automatically to hip or knee osteoarthritis (p335).

## Recall drill

**1. What information belongs in a supplement history in addition to the name?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask what the patient hopes to treat, which preparation and amount they take, what benefit they perceive and what other medicines or supplements they use. (p331–332)</details>

**2. Which details can explain disagreement between two fish-oil trials?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The population, baseline treatment, EPA versus EPA/DHA, dose, duration, placebo and outcome measured all affect interpretation. (p332, p334)</details>

**3. What did VITAL find for major cardiovascular events and cancer?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Older participants receiving <strong>840 mg</strong> of omega-3 fatty acids did not have a lower incidence than placebo recipients over <strong>5 years</strong>. (p334)</details>

**4. In which patients was coenzyme Q10 studied as an adjunct?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A trial in moderate to severe heart failure with reduced ejection fraction used <strong>100 mg three times daily</strong> alongside standard therapy. (p334)</details>

**5. Why review anticoagulants when discussing ginkgo?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ginkgo may increase bleeding risk. The mixed cognitive evidence involves particular extracts and doses. (p334–335)</details>

**6. Does the book describe vitamin E as preventing progression from mild cognitive impairment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. This endpoint was not improved, and some meta-analyses raised a mortality concern with high doses. (p335)</details>

**7. Where does the book describe an exception to generally negative advice on glucosamine or chondroitin?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chondroitin was conditionally supported for hand osteoarthritis. A trial used <strong>800 mg/day</strong>. (p335)</details>

**8. Which psychiatric history matters when considering SAM-e?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask about mania or bipolar illness because SAM-e may precipitate mania. The chapter describes divided dosing and taking it on an empty stomach. (p335–336)</details>

**9. Which melatonin formulation and timing does the book describe for older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Immediate-release <strong>0.3–2 mg</strong> about <strong>1 hour</strong> before bedtime, with limited evidence on long-term adverse effects. (p336)</details>

**10. What cautions accompany valerian in the chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Studies disagree about efficacy, any effect may take weeks, and severe liver disease warrants caution. (p336–337)</details>

**11. Why ask which black cohosh preparation a patient intends to buy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The studied extract and dose matter. Evidence and long-term safety are uncertain, particularly when estrogen is contraindicated. (p337)</details>

**12. What makes a claim about saw palmetto difficult to generalize?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Preparations differ and studies give conflicting results. The book describes <strong>160 mg twice daily</strong> for urinary symptoms. (p337)</details>

**13. What distinguishes tai chi from taking a supplement for fall prevention?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It practices coordinated movement and balance, with attention to the movement itself. The chapter discusses trial evidence for reducing falls. (p338–340)</details>

**14. Which indication has the strongest evidence for acupuncture in this chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chronic pain. Check the condition studied before extending a result to a different indication. (p341)</details>

**15. What should be reviewed alongside mind-body therapies in the chapter's fall case?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Review gait and home safety, orthostasis and sedating or nonessential medicines, and consider physical therapy aligned with the patient's goals. (p343)</details>
