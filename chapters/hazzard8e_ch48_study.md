## When the patient cannot speak freely

A woman with a third fracture may have osteoporosis, falls, inflicted injury or more than one of these. A question about **psychological** mistreatment asks for a different observation. Notice when a patient goes quiet, who answers for her, and whether she can tell her story in private. Fear of retaliation, shame, dependence for care and fear of losing her home can all affect what she is willing to say. Start with what happened and who was present. Ask about safety and the patient’s concerns in her own words. In June 2022 Q60, the change in her willingness to speak when her daughter is present is the reason to pursue this line of inquiry. [Private interview, pp. 722–723](?chapter=48#ch48-interview) · [Israeli signs of psychological harm, Annex 1B](?chapter=48#ch48-il-interview).

Take the caregiver’s account separately. Ask who buys the medicine, how doses are given, whether the patient can transfer or prepare food, and how injuries occurred. Nursing staff, home-care workers or relatives may have seen the interaction outside the examination room. An accusation in front of the patient may prompt a caregiver to cut off access to care. Use an experienced interviewer when possible. [History and behavioral observation, Table 48-3, p. 724](?chapter=48#ch48-assessment).

## Missed medicines and a discharge request

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-e8ba38432f0a33923c977eb3" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

The patient in June 2023 Q82 wants to go home. She has dementia and has been hospitalized for heart failure after missed medication. Her son is the designated caregiver and has never visited the ward. Ask the patient what she understands and wants, establish how she managed before admission and find out who actually supplied treatment. Missed doses may reflect cost, adverse effects, confusion, caregiver difficulty or deliberate withholding. The discharge question turns on whether essential care will be available and whether the circumstances raise a reasonable concern about neglect by the person responsible. [Subtle presentations, p. 722](?chapter=48#ch48-missed-care) · [Israeli procedure, §§8–10](?chapter=48#ch48-il-discharge).

Treating the heart failure addresses the immediate harm. A discharge plan must also identify who can obtain the medication, help the patient take it and alert the clinical team if this arrangement fails. Involve the hospital social worker now, use the statutory reporting route when its conditions are met, and agree the disposition with the treating team and statutory social worker. Assess the specific decision and available support before considering a guardianship application. [Safe discharge in Israel](?chapter=48#ch48-il-discharge) · [Capacity and community resources, pp. 724–725](?chapter=48#ch48-capacity).

## Protection while treating acute illness

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-632ba530eb4611e16d60adfe" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

In June 2026 Q28, the patient is more alert after fluids. Hypernatremia, renal impairment and marked hyperglycemia still need care. A prolonged lapse in follow-up, poor general condition and dependence on the accompanying guardian raise concern about how food, fluids and treatment have been provided. Reassess the patient directly as alertness improves. Identify unmet needs, document observed findings and record who supplied each part of the history. A guardian’s signature cannot resolve the clinical need for admission or the safety concern. The site of care, protection measures and reporting each require a decision. [Israeli hospital protection procedure, §9](?chapter=48#ch48-il-protection) · [Cited ED team coordination, p. 213](?chapter=15#exam-p213-mcq-632ba530eb4611e16d60adfe).

## Recognizing the different forms

Mistreatment can involve a breach of trust or targeting because of age or disability. A single household may combine threats, theft, inadequate food and physical injury. Ask who was responsible for the unmet need, what help was available and whether the person could obtain it. Deliberate withholding and a caregiver who cannot meet demands may leave similar findings but require different support. A history of intimate partner violence may long predate either partner’s disability. [Definitions and examples, pp. 719–720](?chapter=48#p719).

Community studies summarized in the chapter found that as many as one in ten older adults experienced abuse, neglect or exploitation in a year. Smaller dementia studies found higher estimates, and methods and populations differed. Isolation and fear can conceal harm long before services become involved. A caregiver may depend on the patient for housing or money. Other contributors include earlier family violence, overwhelming care demands, psychiatric illness or substance use. Assess each relationship on its own evidence. [Epidemiology and potential mechanisms, pp. 720–722](?chapter=48#p721).

## Examining the patient and using EASI

Ask about pain, mobility and injury mechanism, and examine skin, scalp, mouth, ears, neck and extremities. Assess hydration, nutritional intake, medication access, cognition, mood and functional dependence. Bruises on the face, posterior trunk or neck, an injury inconsistent with the history or evidence of restraint warrant careful investigation. Acute changes in kidney function or nutrition can accompany neglect or medical illness. Target tests and imaging to the presentation. Ask permission before touch, explain why documentation is needed and avoid requiring repeated accounts. [Physical findings, p. 722](?chapter=48#p722) · [Systematic assessment, Table 48-3](?chapter=48#ch48-assessment).

The Elder Abuse Suspicion Index (EASI) comprises five patient questions and one clinician observation. In cognitively intact primary-care patients, sensitivity was 47% and specificity 75%. The first question establishes dependence for activities. The prose says one or more positive responses raises suspicion (p722). Figure 48-1 specifies **a “yes” to any of questions 2 through 6** (p723). A patient can need help with shopping and medications while receiving excellent care. Low sensitivity also means a negative screen leaves room for clinical concern. [Figure 48-1, p. 723](?chapter=48#ch48-easi).

> Current practice (not the book): The USPSTF found insufficient evidence to determine the benefits and harms of universal caregiver-abuse screening in older or vulnerable adults. Signs or disclosure of abuse still require assessment and action under local law. [USPSTF, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40553450/).

## Safety, autonomy and financial exploitation

Identify immediate danger and whether the patient can realistically carry out a safety plan. For someone choosing to remain at home, agree whom to contact, where to go, how to access essential documents and medicines, and how to signal that help is needed. Revisit the plan as circumstances change, even if the patient initially declines help. If cognitive or functional impairment prevents self-protection, coordinate practical assistance and assess capacity for the decision at hand. The chapter links mistreatment with hospitalization, depression, loss of independence and excess mortality. It found insufficient randomized evidence for a single intervention applicable to every family. [Safety plan, pp. 723–725](?chapter=48#ch48-safety-plan).

**US practice vignette.** A patient unable to safeguard his money still has preferences about his relationship with his grandson and where he lives. Adult Protective Services (APS) can investigate and coordinate financial safeguards and living support. Check for continuing danger before assuming that co-residence is safe. Respite, day services, help managing finances or a safe alternative placement may address different needs. [US response, pp. 724–725](?chapter=48#ch48-aps)

> Current practice (not the book): A representative payee manages designated Social Security benefits; the appointment alone does not cover other accounts. [SSA, representative payee duties and limits](https://www.ssa.gov/payee/faqrep.htm).

## Mistreatment in long-term care

Consider failures to provide or follow an appropriate care plan, as well as acts by staff or other residents. A resident with dementia and behavioral symptoms may be harmed by another resident in a shared space. Ask who witnessed the event, whether supervision met the care plan, and what can prevent repetition. The chapter describes US state ombudsman services for institutional complaints. Assisted-living and residential-care settings may have less supervision despite caring for people with substantial needs, and the chapter reports limited prevalence data for these facilities. [Institutional care, pp. 725–726](?chapter=48#p726).

## Summary

**Recognize the spectrum**
- Mistreatment includes physical, sexual or psychological abuse, neglect, abandonment and financial exploitation; definitions vary (p719).
- It may occur at home, in the community or in institutions, often within a relationship of trust (p719).
- Studies suggest **as many as 10%** of community-dwelling older adults are affected annually; smaller studies suggest nearly **50%** among people with dementia (p720).
- Research suggests authorities identify **as few as 1 in 24** cases. Fear, dependence, isolation or cognitive impairment may prevent disclosure (p720).

**Clinical assessment**
- Risk factors are neither a diagnosis nor a reliable way to exclude mistreatment; family circumstances and perpetrator–victim relationships matter (p720–721).
- Chronic disease may mimic abuse, and abuse may be misattributed to chronic disease (p722).
- Maintain clinical suspicion; examine injuries, hygiene, nutrition, medications, function, cognition and social circumstances (p722–724).
- The suspected perpetrator's presence can complicate assessment; obtain appropriate expertise and collateral information sensitively (p722–723).

**Safety & response**
- Establish whether there is immediate danger, then make a practical safety plan even when danger is not immediate (p723–724).
- In most U.S. states, reporting to adult protective services is required; available services and statutory arrangements vary (p724).
- If the patient lacks capacity, document objective evidence; guardianship may be needed with protective/community services (p724–725).
- A capable person may choose to remain in an abusive environment: explain escalation risk, review safety options and remain available (p725).
- Use individualized, trauma-informed management. The chapter reports no good-quality randomized trials establishing an intervention strategy (p723, p725).

**Institutional settings**
- Consider neglect and failures of care planning, as well as staff abuse (p725).
- Resident-to-resident verbal, physical and sexual mistreatment also occurs; institutional safeguards do not eliminate the need for vigilance (p726).
