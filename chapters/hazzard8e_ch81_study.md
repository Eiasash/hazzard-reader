## Home oxygen and acute ventilatory failure

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-26e8f15baa3a145e21c8d533 mcq-f94957c688e051f74a5dd7d3" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Measure oxygenation at rest before deciding how the equipment will be used. Table 81-11 uses **PaO₂ ≤55 mm Hg or SpO₂ ≤88%** for daily oxygen. A patient with PaO₂ of 58 and cor pulmonale also qualifies. The upper thresholds are **PaO₂ ≤59 or SpO₂ ≤89%** when pulmonary hypertension, heart failure/cor pulmonale or erythrocytosis with hematocrit **above 55%** is present. The table specifies at least 16 hours daily. Ask when the measurements were obtained and whether the patient is stable enough for them to represent chronic hypoxemia (p1256–1257).

A saturation of 86% while walking with a resting value of 92% raises a different question. Compare walking distance, breathlessness and oxygenation during a trial of ambulatory oxygen. The survival evidence for severe resting hypoxemia does not extend to isolated exercise desaturation. For a prescription at home, assess the weight of the portable system, tubing along walking routes, smoking and open flames (p1256–1257).

Pulse oximetry cannot detect rising CO₂. During an acute exacerbation with prior CO₂ retention, treat hypoxemia and check ventilation, including a blood gas when indicated. New acidosis with hypercapnia can indicate a need for bilevel mask ventilation. Look for cooperation, a protected airway and stable circulation. Pressure support takes work off the respiratory muscles while bronchodilators and other indicated treatment take effect (p1257, 1261).

## Matching the inhaler to the person

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-18741c7ea62219890efeda8a" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Ask the patient to show how to prepare and take a dose. An MMSE of 23 raises concern about learning the sequence. It cannot determine whether an individual can use a particular device. Observe manual dexterity, grip, vision, coordination and inspiratory effort, then establish who can help at home. A patient who cannot follow commands and has a caregiver can receive a nebulized drug even with poor peak inspiratory flow (Figure 81-2, p1248 and p1255).

A dry-powder device depends on a sufficiently forceful inspiration to disperse the dose. A spacer reduces press-and-breathe coordination demands but still requires correct assembly and use. With cognitive impairment and no helper, the figure asks the clinician to weigh the benefit against the risk of incorrect use. Before escalating medication for persistent symptoms, ask about cost and refills, inspect the devices and repeat the demonstration (p1248, 1255).

## Which drugs prevent exacerbations and which cause harm?

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0a8fce186272773039f15df2 mcq-8a61d27740b818d564cb66db" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) can reduce exacerbations in selected patients. Locally deposited drug may cause dysphonia and oral candidiasis. Observe technique and ask whether the person rinses and spits after use. Pneumonia is more frequent in people receiving ICS, especially with high doses, frailty or low BMI. Reconsider the balance of benefit and harm after pneumonia (p1253–1254).

Systemic exposure can accumulate. Above 1000 micrograms/day of fluticasone or equivalent, studies described faster bone loss and more fractures. The chapter advises considering **osteoporosis prevention** for patients receiving long-term ICS. Cataracts and visual impairment can contribute to falls, although the causal evidence for inhaled steroids is less clear than for systemic steroids. In May 2024 Q100 both upper gastrointestinal bleeding and falls were accepted as exceptions. The chapter explicitly describes a possible indirect pathway to injurious falls (p1253).

In the chapter’s GOLD framework, LAMA is generally the first long-acting bronchodilator. ## Confirming COPD in an older adult

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e53fe32921107e7f9d4490f5" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Ask about chronic symptoms and exposure, then check spirometry after bronchodilation. Aging reduces elastic recoil and the diameter of small airways. FEV₁ can therefore fall more than FVC, lowering the ratio even in healthy older adults. A fixed threshold of 0.70 can label a result abnormal when it is above the age-adjusted lower limit of normal. The chapter uses the fifth percentile, a z-score near −1.64, to illustrate the distinction. Review the clinical context and quality of the maneuver before attaching a COPD diagnosis (p1236–1237, 1242–1244).

## Preventing readmission and loss of independence

After an exacerbation, ask what the patient could do before admission and what now provokes breathlessness. Pulmonary rehabilitation combines supervised endurance and strength training with education, pacing and a plan for activity at home. It improves exercise tolerance and quality of life and reduces hospital use. Persistent symptoms despite optimized medication justify referral even with severe obstruction, advanced age or comorbidity. Arrange smoking-cessation support and reconcile the inhalers before discharge (p1256–1258, 1261).

Check gait, falls, frailty, weight trajectory and bone health. Vertebral deformity and a rigid chest wall compound the loss of respiratory reserve. An abnormal Timed Up and Go can guide balance training and selection of a walking aid. A high GDS prompts assessment of mood, while a low MMSE prompts observation of technique. Each finding changes a different part of care (p1237–1238, 1241, 1247–1248, 1255, 1258).

## Summary

### Epidemiology & definitions
- Hazzard reports COPD as the **fourth** leading cause of death in the US and projects fifth for disability and third for death worldwide by 2030 (p1235).
  > Current practice (not the book): In CDC's final **2024** US statistics, the broader category **chronic lower respiratory diseases**, which includes COPD and asthma, ranked **fifth**. [CDC/NCHS, final 2024 mortality](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db548.htm).
- Global prevalence **10%–12%** (up to **384 million cases**, 2010 estimate); spirometry-confirmed COPD prevalence **15.4%** in US adults ≥65 (p1235).
- The chapter projected that people aged **75 or older** would account for about **half of adults with COPD by 2030** (p1235).
- Dyspnea in **one-third** of older persons; chronic bronchitis in **15%**; wheezing in **12%** (p1235).
- Chronic bronchitis = chronic cough + sputum production for **≥3 months/year for 2 consecutive years** (p1239).
- **Two-thirds** of people with chronic-bronchitis symptoms and **half** with physician-diagnosed emphysema/COPD have *normal* spirometry — symptoms ≠ airflow obstruction (p1235).
- Bronchiectasis present in up to **30%** of COPD patients (p1239).

### Diagnosis & spirometry
- Diagnosis needs compatible history **plus** spirometry-confirmed airflow obstruction (p1242).
- In a compatible clinical context, GOLD uses **post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70**. Because the fixed ratio does not adjust for age, it can classify more older adults as obstructed than an age-adjusted lower limit of normal. In the older cohort described here, estimates were **37.7% versus 15.4%** (p1244, Table 81-5).
- Normal aging lung alone (no COPD) already shows ↑chest-wall rigidity, ↓elastic recoil, "senile emphysema," air trapping, and ↑closing volume approaching FRC — the same physiologic changes seen in COPD, just less severe (single vs double arrows) — part of why fixed spirometric cutoffs overdiagnose older adults (Table 81-1, p1238).
- Age-adjusted approach (GLI): airflow obstruction = FEV1/FVC **Z-score < –1.64** (5th percentile LLN); **–2.55** = 0.5th percentile, a severity marker (p1243–1244).
- Table 81-5 reproduces older ATS/ERS categories using FEV1 percent predicted: **≥70%**, **50–69%** and **<50%** (p1244).
  > Current practice (not the book): The 2022 ERS/ATS interpretive standard grades impairment by **z-score**. GOLD's airflow-obstruction grades are a separate framework. [ERS/ATS interpretive standard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1183/13993003.01499-2021).
- Hazzard describes normal FEV1/FVC with low FEV1 **and evidence of inflammatory airways disease** as a group at risk of progression and calls this PRISm (p1244).
  > Current practice (not the book): GOLD defines the post-bronchodilator pattern by **FEV1/FVC ≥0.70 and FEV1 <80% predicted**. Evidence of airway inflammation is not required. [GOLD 2027, initial pharmacological treatment, groups B and E](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/GOLD-Report-2027-FINAL-v1.1-20Nov2026_WMV.pdf#page=79).

### Comorbidity & functional burden
- COPD patients average **3.7 chronic conditions** and **35 filled prescriptions/year** (p1247).
- Skeletal muscle dysfunction in **~30%**; sarcopenia in an estimated **22%** (up to **63%** in nursing-home residents) (p1241). **Osteoporosis** is a common COPD comorbidity and can further impair mobility (p1241 and Table 81-3, p1242).
- Older adults with COPD have a **twofold increased risk of frailty** (p1241).
- Postmortem study of COPD-exacerbation deaths: heart failure **37%**, pulmonary thromboembolism **21%**, pneumonia **28%**, respiratory failure **14%** (p1247).

### Pharmacologic management (stable COPD)
- **COPD inhaled treatment:** offer a short-acting rescue bronchodilator. Hazzard generally uses a LAMA first for long-acting bronchodilation. In Group D, high symptom burden, frequent exacerbations and blood eosinophils **≥300 cells/mL** as printed support adding ICS to LABA (pp1253–1254). *[sic — Table 81-7, p1246, uses cells/µL.]*
  > Current practice (not the book): GOLD 2026 generally favors **LABA/LAMA** for symptomatic group B or exacerbation-prone group E patients and considers **LABA/LAMA/ICS** in group E at eosinophils **≥300 cells/μL**. Following exacerbations on LABA/LAMA, ICS benefit is more likely as the eosinophil count rises. Concomitant asthma requires an ICS-containing regimen. [GOLD treatment algorithms](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/GOLD-Pocket-Guide-2026-v1.1-20Nov2025_WMV.pdf#page=29).
- ICS risks in older adults: **1.2–1.6-fold** increased pneumonia risk (highest with older age, frailty, low BMI, high dose, eosinophils **<100 cells/mL** as printed *[sic — Table 81-7 uses cells/µL]*); consider osteoporosis prevention during long-term treatment, especially given accelerated bone loss above **1000 mcg/day** fluticasone-equivalent; subcapsular cataracts are established with systemic steroids, while causation with ICS is less certain; atypical mycobacterial infection (p1253–1254).
- Roflumilast: oral PDE4-inhibitor for severe obstruction + chronic bronchitis + history of exacerbations (p1254).
- Clinical phenotypes steer add-on therapy beyond bronchodilators: bronchiectasis → airway-clearance techniques (not ICS-focused); emphysema → consider LVRS/transplant or α1-antiprotease (if AAT-deficient); COPD-asthma overlap/eosinophilic COPD → consider biologics; COPD-OSA overlap → positive airway pressure (Table 81-7, p1246).
- Azithromycin **250 mg daily or 3×/week** reduces exacerbations in former smokers; watch for QTc, hearing loss, resistance; maintenance may be considered for frequent exacerbators despite maximal triple inhalers when worsening airflow obstruction and airway inflammation drive symptoms, without hearing impairment, resting tachycardia or other arrhythmia, apparent QTc-prolongation risk, or clinical/radiographic NTM evidence, with NTM assessment before regular use (p1254).
- Theophylline: goal level **8–12 mcg/mL**; narrow therapeutic window, rarely used now (p1255).

### Devices & adherence
- DPIs need PIF **>45 L/min**; SMIs need only a gentle breath — an advantage over DPIs/pMDIs for low-PIF patients (p1255).
- Cognitive-impairment cutoffs predicting poor inhaler technique: **MMSE <24** or **EXIT-25 ≥15** (p1255).
- Nebulizers/compressors fall under Medicare **Part B**; handheld inhalers under Part D — cost-relevant for donut-hole patients (p1255).

### Acute exacerbations
- Average older COPD patient has **2–3 exacerbations/year**, each lasting up to **2 weeks**; exacerbations cause up to **20% of hospitalizations** in those ≥75 (p1259).
- Respiratory infection causes **~70%** of exacerbations; bacteria **40–60%** (*H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, S. pneumoniae* most common) (Table 81-12, p1259).
- Systemic corticosteroids: delirium risk rises above **60 mg/day** prednisone-equivalent; a **5-day course of 40 mg/day** prednisone was noninferior to a 7–14-day taper (p1236, p1261).
- Antibiotic duration typically **3–7 days** (longer, 10–14 days, if bronchiectasis) (p1236, p1261).
- NPPV/BPAP for acute COPD exacerbation with acute hypercarbia (PaCO2 **>45–50 mm Hg**) **and respiratory acidosis** (no contraindications) reduces intubation, LOS, and mortality (p1257).

### Surgical & LTOT
- LTOT indicated for resting PaO2 **≤55 mm Hg** or SpO2 **≤88%** (daily use ≥16 h/day improves survival) (Table 81-11, p1256).
- LVRS candidates: FEV1 **<50%** predicted, RV **>150–200%** predicted, upper-lobe-predominant emphysema. Hazzard says surgical LVRS should be avoided when **FEV1 or DLCO is <20% predicted and emphysema is homogeneous** because postoperative mortality is higher (p1258).
  > Current practice (not the book): NETT defined high risk as **FEV1 ≤20% predicted** plus **either homogeneous emphysema or DLCO ≤20% predicted**. [NETT high-risk analysis](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa11798).
- Lung transplant referral thresholds: both FEV1 and DLCO **<25%** predicted, PaCO2 **>50 mmHg**, BODE **>5**; median post-transplant survival **5–6 years**; age >70 has higher 30-day/1-year mortality (p1258–1259).

### Palliative care
- Average life expectancy from diagnosis: **14 years** for COPD vs **21 years** for heart disease (p1262).
- Symptom burden in advanced COPD (Table 81-14): dyspnea **94%**, pain **54%** (p1263); respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough, sputum) up to **98%** of patients (p1262).
- Psychological/sleep symptom burden in advanced COPD: depression **>90%** (antidepressants, supportive counseling); anxiety **>90%** (benzodiazepines, behavioral interventions); delirium (antipsychotics + behavioral interventions); sleep disturbance **40%** (trazodone/Z-meds, nocturnal O2, CPAP, CBT) (Table 81-14, p1263).
- Very severe obstruction (**FEV1 <30%** predicted or FEV1 Z-score **<–2.55**) helps identify patients at risk for uncontrolled symptoms from advanced COPD — but FEV1 alone is not sufficient to identify who would benefit from palliative care (p1262).
- SPIKES framework for breaking bad news: **S**etting, **P**erception, **I**nvitation, **K**nowledge, **E**mpathy, **S**trategy/summary (p1264).

## Recall

**1. What are the two fundamental age-related flaws in the GOLD spirometric criteria for COPD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) A fixed FEV1/FVC < 0.70 does not distinguish normal age-related airflow limitation from true COPD-related obstruction (often <0.70 in healthy older never-smokers). (2) FEV1 expressed as %Pred assumes spirometric variability is constant across the adult lifespan, which it is not (p1236, p1243).

</details>

**2. At what GLI Z-score threshold is airflow obstruction (LLN) defined, and what does a Z-score of –2.55 signify?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Z-score < –1.64 defines the lower limit of normal (5th percentile). –2.55 corresponds to the 0.5th percentile and is used as a severity threshold (severe FEV1 Z-score < –2.55), and appears as a marker of resistant-organism risk in exacerbations and of risk for uncontrolled symptoms from advanced COPD (p1236, p1243–1244, p1262).

</details>

**3. What proportion of people with symptoms of chronic bronchitis, and what proportion with physician-diagnosed emphysema/COPD, have normal spirometry?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Two-thirds of those with chronic-bronchitis symptoms and half of those with physician-diagnosed emphysema/COPD have normal spirometry (no airflow obstruction) — yet remain at risk for poor outcomes (p1235).

</details>

**4. What average number of chronic conditions and filled prescriptions per year characterizes a COPD patient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

3.7 chronic conditions and 35 filled prescriptions per year (p1247).

</details>

**5. What eosinophil threshold favors adding inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) to bronchodilator therapy, and what eosinophil level increases ICS-associated pneumonia risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hazzard reports benefit from ICS/LABA with eosinophils **≥300 cells/mL** and high symptom burden plus frequent exacerbations (Group D). ICS-associated pneumonia risk rises with **<100 cells/mL**, older age, frailty, low BMI or high dose. The chapter discusses ICS withdrawal with **<300 cells/mL**, particularly **<100 cells/mL** (pp1253–1254). *[sic — all three units are printed per mL in the prose; Table 81-7 on p1246 uses cells/µL.]*
> Current practice (not the book): GOLD expresses these eosinophil counts in cells/µL. [GOLD Figure 3.10](https://goldcopd.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/GOLD-Pocket-Guide-2026-v1.1-20Nov2025_WMV.pdf#page=29).

</details>

**7. What peak inspiratory flow (PIF) does a dry powder inhaler (DPI) require, and which device is preferred when PIF is inadequate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

DPIs require PIF > 45 L/min. A soft-mist inhaler (SMI) needs only a gentle breath, offering an advantage over DPIs and pMDIs for patients with low PIF or poor "press and breathe" coordination (p1255).

</details>

**8. What MMSE and EXIT-25 cutoffs predict impaired ability to learn inhaler technique?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

MMSE < 24, or EXIT-25 ≥ 15 (also inability to draw intersecting pentagons) (p1255).

</details>

**9. At what prednisone-equivalent dose does delirium risk rise significantly during COPD exacerbation treatment, and what shorter regimen was shown noninferior to a 7–14 day taper?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Delirium risk rises above 60 mg/day. A 5-day course of oral prednisone 40 mg/day was noninferior to a conventional 7–14 day taper, with a shorter length of stay (p1236, p1261).

</details>

**10. What are the three most common bacterial pathogens in acute COPD exacerbations?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae (p1259, Table 81-12).

</details>

**11. What PaO2/SpO2 thresholds indicate daily long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT), and how many hours per day are needed for a survival benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Resting PaO2 ≤ 55 mm Hg or SpO2 ≤ 88% (or PaO2 ≤ 59/SpO2 ≤ 89% with cor pulmonale, pulmonary hypertension, or erythrocytosis). LTOT worn more than 16 hours per day improves survival (p1256).

</details>

**12. What FEV1/DLCO and homogeneity findings make a patient a poor candidate for lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS)?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hazzard says surgical LVRS should be avoided when **FEV1 or DLCO is <20% predicted and emphysema is homogeneous** because postoperative mortality is higher (p1258).
> Current practice (not the book): In NETT, FEV₁ at most 20% predicted plus either homogeneous emphysema or DLCO at most 20% predicted identified patients at high surgical risk. The 30-day mortality was 16% after surgery versus 0% with medical treatment. [Original NETT analysis](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa11798).

</details>

**13. What BODE Index score threshold, and what FEV1/DLCO percentage, triggers lung transplant referral consideration?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

BODE Index score > 5, and FEV1 and DLCO both < 25% predicted, among other criteria (PaCO2 > 50 mmHg, PaO2 < 60 mmHg, pulmonary hypertension/cor pulmonale, ≥1 hospitalization with acute hypercapnia or ≥3 severe exacerbations in the prior year) (p1258–1259).

</details>

**14. How does average life expectancy from diagnosis compare between COPD and heart disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

14 years for COPD vs 21 years for heart disease (p1262).

</details>
