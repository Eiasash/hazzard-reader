# Chapter 91 Study: Lung Cancer

## Summary

**Risk & evaluation**
- Smoking is the major risk factor; lung cancer also occurs in never-smokers (p1421).
- In the United States NLST, annual low-dose CT screening reduced lung-cancer mortality by **20%** versus chest radiographs; false positives and procedural harms matter (p1422).
- A nodule's size, appearance, change over time and patient risk determine evaluation; low/intermediate suspicion may justify serial CT (p1424).
- Obtain tissue by the least invasive approach likely to yield a diagnosis; select bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound or transthoracic biopsy by location (p1424).
- Stage before treatment. The printed discussion calls for brain imaging even with suspected early-stage disease, preferably MRI, especially in confirmed SCLC (p1425).

**Treatment by disease extent**
- Early NSCLC: assess for resection; stereotactic body radiotherapy offers an option for medically inoperable patients (p1425).
- Stage II patients are more likely than stage I patients to benefit from adjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy (p1426).
- Stage III requires multidisciplinary planning; mediastinal involvement often precludes upfront surgery. Concurrent chemoradiation suits more functionally fit patients with unresectable disease (p1426).
- Metastatic NSCLC treatment is palliative. Test molecular alterations; targeted treatment may improve disease control and quality of life (p1427).
- Without a targetable alteration, immunotherapy selection depends partly on tumor PD-L1 expression; immune toxicity can affect multiple organs (p1427–1429).

**SCLC & geriatric priorities**
- SCLC grows rapidly, often presents symptomatically and commonly recurs after an initial response (p1431). Its paraneoplastic presentations include **SIADH, Cushing syndrome and Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome** (p1424).
- Platinum plus etoposide underpins treatment; concurrent radiation treats limited-stage disease, with greater esophagitis/toxicity than sequential treatment (p1431).
- Carboplatin may substitute when cisplatin is poorly tolerated; immunotherapy plus chemotherapy improves survival in extensive-stage disease (p1431).
- Introduce palliative care early and clarify treatment goals (pp1421, 1432). In the cited randomized study of metastatic NSCLC, early palliative care was associated with median overall survival of **11.6 months versus 8.9 months** with standard care alone.
- Assess function, cognition, nutrition, comorbidity, medications and psychosocial support; chronological age and performance status alone incompletely describe fitness (p1433).

### Separating screening evidence from staging

In the US NLST, participants were **55 to 74 years** old, had smoked at least **30 pack-years**, and were current smokers or had stopped within **15 years**. The original relative reduction in lung cancer deaths was **20%** with annual low-dose CT versus chest radiographs; a later analysis estimated **16%**. Many positive screens proved benign. A frail patient with major competing illness may face an invasive workup without living long enough to benefit (p1422).

The book cites an older US screening recommendation. Eligibility for screening is distinct from the workup of a suspicious nodule or new symptoms (p1422–1424).

> Current practice (not the book): The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends annual low-dose CT at ages 50–80 with at least 20 pack-years for current smokers or those who quit within 15 years. Stop when illness substantially limits life expectancy or the ability or willingness to undergo curative lung surgery. - [USPSTF, 2021](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening).

> Current practice (not the book): For curative-intent NSCLC, NICE omits dedicated brain imaging in asymptomatic clinical stage I disease, advises contrast CT in stage II with MRI for suspected metastases, and contrast MRI in stage III. Neurologic symptoms warrant separate evaluation. - [NICE NG122, 2019, recommendations 1.2.24–27](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng122/chapter/diagnosis-and-staging).

### Matching treatment to what the patient can tolerate

A solitary nodule in an operable patient may be resectable; a suspicious mediastinal node can instead provide tissue and change the stage. In metastatic NSCLC, histology, driver alterations and immune biomarkers influence drug choice. Ask about pre-existing functional limitations, how much toxicity the patient would accept and what support is available at home. A trial that enrolled fit older adults may tell little about someone dependent in daily activities (p1424–1427, p1433).

## Exam traps

- A 20% relative reduction in lung cancer deaths in NLST is neither a positive scan's predictive value nor a survival gain for an individual (p1422).
- A never-smoker can develop lung cancer and may have an actionable driver alteration (p1421).
- The early palliative care survival result arose in a metastatic NSCLC trial, alongside oncology care (p1432).

## Recall drill

**1. What was compared in the US NLST?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Annual low-dose CT and chest radiographs in a higher-risk smoking population. The original report found a <strong>20% relative reduction</strong> in lung cancer mortality (p1422).</details>

**2. Why can screening have less benefit in a frail older patient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Competing illness can limit the time available to benefit, while false positives still expose the patient to workup and procedural harms (p1422).</details>

**3. What information should guide assessment of an incidental nodule?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Its size, morphology and interval change, the person's risk profile, and whether observation, biopsy or resection could change management (p1424).</details>

**4. How is a tissue sampling route selected?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use the least invasive route likely to establish a diagnosis, guided by lesion and node location. Options include bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound and transthoracic biopsy (p1424).</details>

**5. Why establish mediastinal and distant extent before definitive surgery?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Nodal or distant disease may change the aim and sequence of treatment and the value of resection (p1424–1426).</details>

**6. Which option offers local treatment for medically inoperable early NSCLC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stereotactic body radiotherapy delivers focused ablative radiation over a small number of fractions (p1425).</details>

**7. Which resected group has a clearer rationale for postoperative chemotherapy in the chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Stage II disease carries a higher recurrence risk than stage I and may benefit from a cisplatin-based doublet (p1426).</details>

**8. What makes concurrent chemoradiation challenging in unresectable stage III disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Combined treatment imposes greater toxicity and requires adequate physiological and functional reserve (p1426).</details>

**9. Why obtain molecular results in advanced NSCLC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Targetable alterations such as EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements can direct treatment and change expected benefit (p1425, p1427).</details>

**10. What additional tumor marker influences immunotherapy selection?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>PD-L1 expression, together with driver status and histology; immune-mediated adverse effects can involve multiple organs (p1427–1429).</details>

**11. Which paraneoplastic syndromes suggest SCLC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>SIADH, ectopic ACTH production causing Cushing syndrome, and Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (p1424).</details>

**12. What treatment backbone is described for limited-stage SCLC?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A platinum agent plus etoposide with thoracic radiotherapy; concurrent therapy adds toxicity, including esophagitis (p1431).</details>

**13. What option is discussed in SCLC when cisplatin toxicity is a concern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Carboplatin can substitute in selected patients. Treatment still requires assessment of fitness and expected toxicity (p1431).</details>

**14. What did early specialist palliative care achieve in the cited metastatic NSCLC trial?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In addition to usual oncology care it improved quality of life, with median survival of <strong>11.6 versus 8.9 months</strong> in the comparison (p1432).</details>

**15. Which factors can a performance score alone miss?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Cognitive impairment, medication burden, nutrition, comorbidities, functional limitations and the availability of help (p1433).</details>
