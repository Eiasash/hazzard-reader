# Chapter 91 Study: Lung Cancer

## Summary

**Risk & evaluation**
- Smoking is the major risk factor; lung cancer also occurs in never-smokers (p1421).
- In the United States NLST, annual low-dose CT screening reduced lung-cancer mortality by **20%** versus chest radiographs; false positives and procedural harms matter (p1422).
- A nodule's size, appearance, change over time and patient risk determine evaluation; low/intermediate suspicion may justify serial CT (p1424).
- Obtain tissue by the least invasive approach likely to yield a diagnosis; select bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound or transthoracic biopsy by location (p1424).
- Stage before treatment. The printed discussion calls for brain imaging even with suspected early-stage disease, preferably MRI, especially in confirmed SCLC (p1425).

**Treatment by disease extent**
- Early NSCLC: assess for resection; stereotactic body radiotherapy offers an option for medically inoperable patients (p1425).
- Stage II patients are more likely than stage I patients to benefit from adjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy (p1426).
- Stage III requires multidisciplinary planning; mediastinal involvement often precludes upfront surgery. Concurrent chemoradiation suits more functionally fit patients with unresectable disease (p1426).
- Metastatic NSCLC treatment is palliative. Test molecular alterations; targeted treatment may improve disease control and quality of life (p1427).
- Without a targetable alteration, immunotherapy selection depends partly on tumor PD-L1 expression; immune toxicity can affect multiple organs (p1427–1429).

**SCLC & geriatric priorities**
- SCLC grows rapidly, often presents symptomatically and commonly recurs after an initial response (p1431). Its paraneoplastic presentations include **SIADH, Cushing syndrome and Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome** (p1424).
- Platinum plus etoposide underpins treatment; concurrent radiation treats limited-stage disease, with greater esophagitis/toxicity than sequential treatment (p1431).
- Carboplatin may substitute when cisplatin is poorly tolerated; immunotherapy plus chemotherapy improves survival in extensive-stage disease (p1431).
- Introduce palliative care early and clarify treatment goals (pp1421, 1432). In the randomized study of metastatic NSCLC, early palliative care was associated with median overall survival of **11.6 months versus 8.9 months** with standard care alone.
- Assess function, cognition, nutrition, comorbidity, medications and psychosocial support; chronological age and performance status alone incompletely describe fitness (p1433).

### Separating screening evidence from staging

In the US NLST, participants were **55 to 74 years** old, had smoked at least **30 pack-years**, and were current smokers or had stopped within **15 years**. The original relative reduction in lung cancer deaths was **20%** with annual low-dose CT versus chest radiographs; a later analysis estimated **16%**. Many positive screens proved benign. A frail patient with major competing illness may face an invasive workup without living long enough to benefit (p1422).

The book cites an older US screening recommendation. Eligibility for screening is distinct from the workup of a suspicious nodule or new symptoms (p1422–1424).

> Current practice (not the book): The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends annual low-dose CT at ages 50–80 with at least 20 pack-years for current smokers or those who quit within 15 years. Stop when illness substantially limits life expectancy or the ability or willingness to undergo curative lung surgery. - [USPSTF, 2021](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening).

> Current practice (not the book): For curative-intent NSCLC, NICE omits dedicated brain imaging in asymptomatic clinical stage I disease, advises contrast CT in stage II with MRI for suspected metastases, and contrast MRI in stage III. Neurologic symptoms warrant separate evaluation. - [NICE NG122, 2019, recommendations 1.2.24–27](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng122/chapter/diagnosis-and-staging).

### Matching treatment to what the patient can tolerate

A solitary nodule in an operable patient may be resectable; a suspicious mediastinal node can instead provide tissue and change the stage. In metastatic NSCLC, histology, driver alterations and immune biomarkers influence drug choice. Ask about pre-existing functional limitations, how much toxicity the patient would accept and what support is available at home. A trial that enrolled fit older adults may tell little about someone dependent in daily activities (p1424–1427, p1433).

### Estimate the benefit of screening before investigating the finding

Lung cancer is common at ages when chronic lung disease, cardiovascular disease and functional limitations affect the choice of treatment. The book gives a median age at diagnosis of **71 years**. Tobacco is the major modifiable risk, but the absence of a smoking history does not exclude a primary lung tumor. In the United States about **13%** of patients with lung cancer in the estimates cited were never-smokers, disproportionately women. Some such tumors carry actionable alterations. Smoking history is valuable for risk assessment and cessation counseling; it cannot substitute for histology or molecular testing once cancer is diagnosed (p1421–1422).

Compare the trials using the actual comparator. NLST compared annual low-dose CT with chest radiographs, whereas NELSON compared four CT screening rounds with no screening. NELSON reported a **24% relative reduction** in lung cancer mortality among the men studied. The mean false-positive rate per round was **23.3%** in NLST and **10.4%** in NELSON. These rates describe screening rounds. The observation that more than **96%** of positive NLST screens proved false uses *positive screens* as its denominator. The two statistics can both be true. Over three NLST rounds, **1.8%** of participants without cancer underwent an invasive procedure after a positive result (p1422).

The trial population had enough fitness to enter a screening study, and very old adults were underrepresented. Discuss what would happen after a positive scan: repeated imaging, a possible biopsy, and the feasibility of potentially curative treatment. Someone unable to tolerate either resection or focal radiotherapy can incur the burden of workup without receiving the benefit for which screening was offered. Screening an asymptomatic person is distinct from evaluating persistent hemoptysis, postobstructive pneumonia or unexplained weight loss. A screening eligibility rule cannot rule out a diagnostic evaluation when there are symptoms (p1421–1424).

### Symptoms suggest a location or mechanism, not a histologic diagnosis

A central tumor can obstruct a bronchus, producing cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea or recurrent pneumonia beyond the obstruction. Hoarseness or stridor can indicate effects on nearby structures. A superior sulcus tumor at the lung apex can cause shoulder or arm pain, brachial plexus dysfunction or Horner syndrome; a patient with prominent shoulder symptoms may not emphasize the chest. Distant spread may first become apparent through symptoms from brain, bone, liver, adrenal or contralateral lung. The clinical task is to distinguish effects of the primary lesion, regional invasion, distant metastasis and systemic tumor effects, since these lead to different tests and interventions (p1423–1424).

Paraneoplastic syndromes can precede or dominate the pulmonary complaint. Hazzard specifically associates small-cell histology with inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, ectopic Cushing syndrome and Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome. In that item the combination of proximal weakness, difficulty rising and reduced reflexes in a patient with SCLC makes a neuromuscular junction disorder plausible. The additional keyed findings are orthostatic symptoms and dry mouth, indicating autonomic involvement. Antibodies to acetylcholine receptors point to a different junctional disorder; a thyroid clue or remote travel history fits the clinical pattern less well. The autonomic details come from the linked exam, while the tumor association is explicit in the chapter (p1424).

### Use diagnostic tissue efficiently

A solitary pulmonary nodule is a radiologic finding. Its size, margin, growth and appearance on earlier imaging change the estimate of malignancy alongside smoking exposure and history of cancer. If the likelihood is low enough, serial imaging may give useful information about growth without exposing the person to an immediate invasive test. For an operable patient with a highly suspicious resectable nodule, thoracoscopic resection can furnish a diagnosis and definitive local treatment. A central lesion is accessible differently from a peripheral nodule. Bronchoscopy or endobronchial ultrasound can sample the airway or mediastinal nodes, while imaging-guided transthoracic sampling can reach a peripheral target (p1424).

Choose a target whose result will change the plan. A sample from a suspicious mediastinal node can establish cancer and document nodal spread in one procedure. Similarly, a safely accessible distant lesion can establish metastatic extent. Request enough material to distinguish SCLC from NSCLC, determine NSCLC subtype and preserve the opportunity for biomarker testing if systemic therapy is contemplated. Lung adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma differ in their treatment options. A small diagnostic specimen should not be exhausted by tests that add little while clinically decisive molecular results remain unavailable (p1422–1425).

Stage then guides treatment intent. In the book's eighth-edition Table 91-1, stage I NSCLC is localized, node negative and potentially curable. Stage II includes selected node-negative tumors and disease involving regional N1 nodes. Stage III encompasses more extensive locoregional disease, particularly mediastinal nodal involvement; not all such tumors are resectable. Distant spread puts disease into stage IV, even when the thoracic primary appears small. The table reports five-year survival of **53%–60%** for stage II, **13%–36%** for stage III and **under 10%** for stage IV in the cohorts it summarizes. These are historical group estimates; a targetable alteration, response to treatment and competing illness change the course for a particular individual (p1423–1427).

### Local treatment and consolidation in NSCLC

For potentially resectable early NSCLC, first ask whether the patient can tolerate the required extent of resection. Baseline lung function, comorbidity and ability to recover determine the clinical significance of an anatomically successful operation. The chapter describes lobectomy as a usual approach for fit patients and discusses more limited resections when reserve is insufficient. Stereotactic body radiation focuses a high dose on a defined primary over relatively few fractions, offering local control without a major operation for selected medically inoperable patients. A high local-control rate should not be equated with the same overall survival in populations with different surgical fitness (p1425).

> Current practice (not the book): In CALGB 140503, sublobar resection was noninferior to lobectomy for disease-free survival in selected patients with peripheral, node-negative NSCLC ≤2 cm after intraoperative nodal confirmation. In resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC, ADAURA showed an overall-survival benefit from adjuvant osimertinib. [CALGB 140503, 2023 and ADAURA, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036605/).

The rationale for adjuvant therapy after apparently complete resection is occult microscopic disease. The book describes a larger potential benefit from cisplatin-based chemotherapy for stage II than for low-risk stage I. Think about the absolute recurrence risk, kidney function, postoperative functional decline and whether the patient resembles the trial population. Cisplatin-related renal toxicity may make carboplatin attractive, but their adjuvant evidence bases in the chapter are not identical. A resected tumor with a sensitizing EGFR alteration also raises the separate question of adjuvant targeted treatment. Results in the resected setting should not be inferred solely from response rates in metastatic disease (p1425–1426).

In unresectable stage III NSCLC the radiation and systemic components address different risks. Concurrent chemoradiation gives effective combined treatment in patients with sufficient reserve, while sequential treatment may be more tolerable for others. Mediastinal staging and multidisciplinary discussion distinguish selected stage IIIA cases in which surgery might follow initial treatment from those treated without planned surgery. The PACIFIC population had disease control following concurrent platinum-based chemoradiation. The book gives durvalumab **10 mg/kg every 2 weeks for 12 months** as the trial regimen and reports an overall-survival advantage. The printed sentence describing progression-free survival places **16.8 months** and **5.6 months** in reverse order relative to the described improvement (p1426).

> Current practice (not the book): In the original PACIFIC report, median progression-free survival was 16.8 months with durvalumab and 5.6 months with placebo. For unresectable stage III NSCLC with an EGFR exon 19 deletion or exon 21 L858R mutation and no progression during or after platinum chemoradiation, the FDA subsequently approved osimertinib. - [Antonia et al., PACIFIC, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1709937), [FDA osimertinib decision, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cancer-approvals/fda-approves-osimertinib-locally-advanced-unresectable-stage-iii-non-small-cell-lung-cancer).

### Table 91-2: match a driver alteration to a treatment and a toxicity

For metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC, the book separates a targetable molecular alteration from PD-L1 expression. The first can predict sensitivity to a drug directed at the driver. The second can help select immunotherapy when there is no suitable actionable driver. Table 91-2 reports specific regimens, not interchangeable members of a single oral-drug class. Some rows compare treatments in randomized trials; others provide response rates in single-arm cohorts. A high response rate from a small cohort cannot rank that agent above another drug tested in a different tumor genotype (p1427–1429).

- **Sensitizing EGFR mutation.** In FLAURA, osimertinib produced median progression-free survival of **18.9 versus 10.2 months** compared with earlier-generation EGFR inhibitors. The table reports median overall survival of **38.6 versus 31.8 months**. Consider diarrhea, skin toxicity, pneumonitis, cardiomyopathy and QT prolongation in a patient with marginal cardiac or pulmonary reserve (p1428).
- **ALK rearrangement.** Alectinib and brigatinib are compared separately with crizotinib. The ALEX data for alectinib show median progression-free survival of **34.8 versus 10.9 months**. Monitor hepatic and gastrointestinal effects and distinguish treatment-emergent dyspnea from cancer progression. Brigatinib can also cause bradycardia, visual disturbances and creatine kinase elevations. The age subgroup is much smaller than the full treatment population (p1428).
- **ROS1 rearrangement.** Crizotinib and entrectinib have activity. The table gives overall response rates of **72%** and **77%** from separate early-phase studies. Edema and altered vision can matter with crizotinib, and dizziness, constipation and edema can limit daily activities with entrectinib. The table does not present a head-to-head comparison (p1428).
- **BRAF V600E and RET.** A BRAF V600E mutation can be treated with dabrafenib plus trametinib; fever, rash and cardiomyopathy are among the concerns. A RET rearrangement points instead to selpercatinib, with dry mouth, hypertension and elevated transaminases among reported adverse effects. These choices require the named alteration. A smoking history or adenocarcinoma morphology alone does not identify it (p1428).
- **MET exon 14 skipping and NTRK fusion.** Capmatinib achieved responses in **68%** of previously untreated and **41%** of pretreated patients in the cohorts shown. Edema, nausea, reduced appetite, dyspnea and interstitial lung disease are relevant cautions. NTRK fusion can make larotrectinib or entrectinib options; check for dizziness, fatigue, hepatic abnormalities and other agent-specific effects. Distinguish an alteration shown in the tumor from an incidental symptom or an adverse event that develops on treatment (p1429).

### Table 91-3: immunotherapy, histology and practical treatment burden

In KEYNOTE-024, patients without EGFR mutations or ALK rearrangements and with PD-L1 tumor proportion score **at least 50%** were randomized to pembrolizumab or platinum chemotherapy. The table reports overall response **45%**, median progression-free survival **10.3 versus 6 months** and median overall survival **30 versus 14.2 months**. These trial data apply to the selected population. Glancing at a high PD-L1 score before the driver results are known can miss a more specific therapeutic option. The chapter correctly uses tumor ligand expression for selection but calls pembrolizumab a PD-L1 inhibitor in its narrative (p1427–1430).

> Current practice (not the book): Pembrolizumab blocks the **PD-1 receptor**. PD-L1 expression on the tumor is the biomarker used in the trial; it is not the drug's binding target. - [FDA pembrolizumab prescribing information, 2026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/125514s194lbl.pdf).

For nonsquamous tumors without an actionable driver, Table 91-3 illustrates a platinum–pemetrexed–pembrolizumab combination and subsequent maintenance therapy. For squamous histology the example uses carboplatin with paclitaxel or nab-paclitaxel and pembrolizumab. KEYNOTE-407 reports median overall survival of **15.9 versus 11.3 months** for the studied squamous population. Pemetrexed can cause myelosuppression and fatigue, while taxane neuropathy may be particularly costly to a person already using a walking aid. Carboplatin-associated thrombocytopenia may affect a patient taking anticoagulants. Do not transfer a nonsquamous regimen to a squamous case solely because the PD-L1 percentage is similar (p1429–1431).

The atezolizumab, bevacizumab, carboplatin and paclitaxel row illustrates a different combination in nonsquamous disease. Bevacizumab adds bleeding, hypertension, proteinuria, thrombosis and wound-healing concerns to the burden of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. The immune component can produce pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathy, nephritis or, less commonly, myocarditis and myositis. Dyspnea, diarrhea, weakness or confusion during therapy therefore merit assessment of treatment toxicity alongside infection, other illness and tumor progression. The absence of routine cytotoxic chemotherapy does not make an immune or targeted regimen a low-burden default (p1427–1431).

After initial control, maintenance therapy aims to prolong control at a tolerable cost. In the PARAMOUNT older subgroup, maintenance pemetrexed reduced risk of progression without demonstrating an overall-survival advantage in that subgroup. At relapse, options depend on prior treatment, reserve and goals; the book discusses docetaxel and the addition of ramucirumab, a VEGF-receptor antibody. Maintenance treatment, second-line therapy and treatment beyond objective progression are different decisions. Before each one, ask what symptomatic or time benefit is expected and which adverse effects could cost the patient independence (p1430–1431).

### Small-cell disease: limited field, extensive spread and relapse

Table 91-1 defines limited-stage SCLC by the ability to encompass the primary and involved lymph nodes in a safe radiation field. Extensive-stage disease usually reflects metastasis. The limited/extensive distinction answers a practical radiation question and should not be inferred from the diameter of the main mass alone. The table gives historical five-year survival of **10%–15%** for limited and **2% or less** for extensive disease. SCLC often produces an impressive initial response but recurs. Consequently a patient with apparent early shrinkage still needs discussion of the likelihood of relapse and the purpose of follow-up (p1423, p1431).

Platinum plus etoposide is the systemic backbone in both groups. For limited-stage disease, concurrent thoracic radiation can improve disease control at the cost of additional toxicity, particularly esophagitis. Consider preexisting swallowing difficulty, nutritional reserve, kidney function and ability to attend a prolonged treatment course. Carboplatin can be used when cisplatin toxicity would be prohibitive. For extensive-stage disease the IMpower133 comparison added atezolizumab to platinum–etoposide, followed by maintenance atezolizumab. Median survival was **12.3 versus 10.3 months**. The reported benefit was not restricted to a high PD-L1 score, unlike the selection for single-agent pembrolizumab in the NSCLC trial (p1431–1432).

> Current practice (not the book): The FDA also approved durvalumab for adults with limited-stage SCLC whose disease has not progressed after concurrent platinum chemotherapy and radiation. - [FDA durvalumab decision, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cancer-approvals/fda-approves-durvalumab-limited-stage-small-cell-lung-cancer).

Prophylactic cranial irradiation aims to reduce subsequent brain metastases after a response. Weigh the evidence separately for limited and extensive disease, possible late neurocognitive effects and the patient's baseline cognition. On relapse the chapter distinguishes a treatment-free interval longer than **3 months** from relapse within that interval when assessing the prospect of a renewed platinum response. Topotecan yielded response rates of **15%–30%** in the data summarized, with substantial marrow and nonhematologic toxicity. Lurbinectedin is another later-line option; response in a single-arm study is not equivalent to demonstrating longer survival versus supportive care in a frail individual (p1432).

### Geriatric assessment and early palliative care

Performance status alone can conceal a patient who can walk into clinic but cannot prepare meals, remember medicines or attend treatment without help. Assess activities, falls, cognition, comorbid disease, polypharmacy, nutrition and social support. Link the findings to specific decisions: neuropathy may threaten mobility, renal impairment may limit platinum choices, and living alone can complicate early recognition of immune toxicity. A treatment trial that admitted selected fit older adults provides limited information about an individual with major functional or cognitive impairment (p1425–1427, p1433).

 In addition to the reported survival difference, depressive symptoms were less common and aggressive end-of-life care was less frequent (33% versus 54%). The trial defined this outcome as chemotherapy within 14 days before death, no hospice care, or hospice admission three days or less before death in the early-care group. These results came from a defined population and treatment era. Ask the patient to explain what the proposed regimen is meant to achieve. Tumor shrinkage or a chance of living longer can be worth pursuing without implying cure. Clarifying that distinction permits a discussion of how much clinic time, toxicity or functional loss a person is willing to accept in exchange for the expected benefit (p1432–1433).

## Exam traps

- A positive screening CT is not the same as a high probability of cancer after diagnostic evaluation; check the denominator of each false-positive statistic (p1422).
- Hazzard associates Lambert–Eaton syndrome with SCLC; the 2026 related item cites Harrison and tests the accompanying autonomic features (p1424).
- In PACIFIC, do not reverse the durvalumab and placebo progression-free survival medians as the printed sentence does (p1426).
- High PD-L1 expression and a targetable driver are distinct results. Interpret them with histology and the population studied (p1427–1430).

- A 20% relative reduction in lung cancer deaths in NLST is neither a positive scan's predictive value nor a survival gain for an individual (p1422).
- A never-smoker can develop lung cancer and may have an actionable driver alteration (p1421).
- The early palliative care survival result arose in a metastatic NSCLC trial, alongside oncology care (p1432).
