# Chapter 19 Study: Value-Based Care

## Summary

**Value and risk**
- Value means patient health outcomes relative to costs. Quality targets alone may miss patient preferences or sustainability (p263).
- Choosing Wisely identifies services that offer little benefit, may harm patients, incur unnecessary costs or waste resources. The AGS lists 10 treatments to avoid. These lists are one component of a broader effort to reduce low-value care (p264, Table 19-1).
- Common elements: financial risk sharing, quality measurement, care coordination and population health management (p264-266).
- Upside risk rewards performance; downside risk permits losses. Bundles cover an episode; capitation pays per person. Quality thresholds help prevent withholding beneficial care (p265).

**Models in the United States**
- Medicare Advantage receives risk-adjusted capitation. Institutional special-needs plans serve institutional-level needs; equivalent plans extend this approach into the community (p267).
- PACE: **55 or older**, state-certified nursing-home level of care, living in its service area and able to live safely in the community at enrollment; provides integrated medical and long-term support (p270).
- Medicare hospice requires terminal certification of **6 months or less**, a comfort-care choice and signed election. Payment varies by care setting/intensity (p270).
- Medical homes coordinate comprehensive, accessible primary care; ACOs span wider provider networks and share savings or risk (p271).
- Hospital value-based purchasing/readmission programs, MIPS and SNF value-based purchasing tie payment to selected performance measures. Bundled programs extend accountability across an episode and postacute care (p271-272).

**Measure what matters**
- Donabedian: structure, process and outcomes. Separate intermediate markers from outcomes patients experience, including pain, function and continence (p272-273).
- Outcome comparisons need appropriate risk adjustment. Multimorbidity, drug interactions and competing recommendations complicate disease-specific measures in older adults (p273-274).
- Improvement depends on local implementation, leadership and context; there is no universally successful recipe (p274).
- Measurement itself consumes resources. Hospital programs may disproportionately penalize safety-net hospitals and those treating complex patients. Evaluate risk adjustment, quality outcomes and spending together (Table 19-3, p269; p274–275).

### Reading a value-based contract

Three questions distinguish the arrangements in the chapter. Who is accountable, what period does the payment cover, and what happens when spending differs from the benchmark? A surgical bundle can link a hospital to postacute costs from one episode. Capitation instead places a population's covered needs within a per-person payment. An accountable care organization can still bill for individual services while sharing savings if it meets spending and quality requirements (p265, p271–272).

Patient attribution changes what the accountable team can know in advance. Retrospective attribution uses the care received over the year to assign a patient afterward. Prospective attribution uses earlier utilization to assign a patient for the coming year. Risk adjustment addresses another issue: a benchmark that ignores complexity can financially disadvantage a practice caring for sicker people (p271, p274).

A direct outcome is something a patient experiences, such as pain, continence or walking ability. Blood pressure is an intermediate marker. Lower admission rates may reflect better community care, but that utilization count by itself says little about a particular patient's symptoms or function (p272–274).

## Exam traps

- A cheap service can still be low value when it offers little benefit or causes harm (p263–264).
- Shared savings can coexist with fee-for-service billing (p271).
- Risk adjustment and quality thresholds solve different problems: case mix versus incentives to withhold useful care (p265, p274).

## Recall drill

**1. What two elements determine value in the chapter's framework?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Health outcomes that matter to patients and the cost of achieving those outcomes. (p263)</details>

**2. Why does providing fewer services fail as a measure of value by itself?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It can avoid low-benefit care or withhold an effective service. Examine patient outcomes and cost together. (p263–265)</details>

**3. What distinguishes one-sided from two-sided risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>One-sided risk offers a reward for good performance. With two-sided risk the organization may also lose money if it misses its targets. (p265)</details>

**4. What is the unit of payment in a bundle and in capitation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A bundle covers a defined episode of care. Capitation pays a set amount per enrolled person for covered care over time. (p265)</details>

**5. Why include quality thresholds in a savings contract?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Spending less could be achieved by withholding valuable services. Requiring adequate quality helps counter that incentive. (p265, p271)</details>

**6. How does risk adjustment affect a benchmark?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It takes underlying illness and expected spending into account, reducing the disincentive to care for patients with complex needs. (p274)</details>

**7. What distinguishes an institutional special-needs plan from its community counterpart?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>One serves people in institutions. The equivalent plan applies a similar approach to people in the community with institutional-level needs. (p267)</details>

**8. Who qualifies for PACE in the chapter's US example?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>People <strong>55 or older</strong> who live in the service area, meet state criteria for nursing-home-level care and can live safely in the community with program support. (p270)</details>

**9. Which elements establish the Medicare hospice election described in the book?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Certification of a terminal prognosis of <strong>6 months or less</strong>, choice of comfort-focused care and a signed election of the benefit. (p270)</details>

**10. What is the organizational difference between a medical home and an ACO?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The medical home organizes primary care. An ACO brings providers together with responsibility for the quality and cost of care for an attributed population. (p271)</details>

**11. How can an ACO know which patients it will be accountable for before the year begins?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Prospective attribution uses past care to assign patients in advance. Retrospective attribution identifies them after the performance year. (p271)</details>

**12. Can an ACO that bills individual visits still share savings?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. The chapter describes fee-for-service billing with additional accountability for quality and spending on the attributed population. (p271)</details>

**13. What does Donabedian's framework ask about a service?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>How care is organized and resourced, what clinicians actually do and what outcomes follow: structure, process and outcomes. (p272–273)</details>

**14. Why is continence a different kind of endpoint from blood pressure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The patient can experience continence directly. Blood pressure is an intermediate clinical measure related to the risk of future health outcomes. (p273)</details>

**15. Why might a uniformly applied hospital penalty fall disproportionately on safety-net facilities?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Patients' health, complexity and social context influence measured outcomes. Incomplete adjustment can make comparisons and financial penalties inequitable. (p269, p274–275)</details>
