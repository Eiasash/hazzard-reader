# Chapter 19 Study: Value-Based Care

## Summary

**Value and risk**
- Value means patient health outcomes relative to costs. Quality targets alone may miss patient preferences or sustainability (p263).
- Choosing Wisely identifies services that offer little benefit, may harm patients, incur unnecessary costs or waste resources. The AGS lists 10 treatments to avoid. These lists are one component of a broader effort to reduce low-value care (p264, Table 19-1).
- Common elements: financial risk sharing, quality measurement, care coordination and population health management (p264-266).
- Upside risk rewards performance; downside risk permits losses. Bundles cover an episode; capitation pays per person. Quality thresholds help prevent withholding beneficial care (p265).

**Models in the United States**
- Medicare Advantage receives risk-adjusted capitation. Institutional special-needs plans serve institutional-level needs; equivalent plans extend this approach into the community (p267).
- PACE: **55 or older**, state-certified nursing-home level of care, living in its service area and able to live safely in the community at enrollment; provides integrated medical and long-term support (p270).
- Medicare hospice requires certification of a life expectancy of six months or less and election of the hospice benefit; its daily payment varies with the level of care (p270).
- Medical homes coordinate comprehensive, accessible primary care; ACOs span wider provider networks and share savings or risk (p271).
- Hospital value-based purchasing/readmission programs, MIPS and SNF value-based purchasing tie payment to selected performance measures. Bundled programs extend accountability across an episode and postacute care (p271-272).

**Measure what matters**
- Donabedian: structure, process and outcomes. Separate intermediate markers from outcomes patients experience, including pain, function and continence (p272-273).
- Outcome comparisons need appropriate risk adjustment. Multimorbidity, drug interactions and competing recommendations complicate disease-specific measures in older adults (p273-274).
- Improvement depends on local implementation, leadership and context; there is no universally successful recipe (p274).
- Measurement itself consumes resources. Hospital programs may disproportionately penalize safety-net hospitals and those treating complex patients. Evaluate risk adjustment, quality outcomes and spending together (Table 19-3, p269; p274–275).

### Reading a value-based contract

Three questions distinguish the arrangements in the chapter. Who is accountable, what period does the payment cover, and what happens when spending differs from the benchmark? A surgical bundle can link a hospital to postacute costs from one episode. Capitation instead places a population's covered needs within a per-person payment. An accountable care organization can still bill for individual services while sharing savings if it meets spending and quality requirements (p265, p271–272).

Patient attribution changes what the accountable team can know in advance. Retrospective attribution uses the care received over the year to assign a patient afterward. Prospective attribution uses earlier utilization to assign a patient for the coming year. Risk adjustment addresses another issue: a benchmark that ignores complexity can financially disadvantage a practice caring for sicker people (p271, p274).

A direct outcome is something a patient experiences, such as pain, continence or walking ability. Blood pressure is an intermediate marker. Lower admission rates may reflect better community care, but that utilization count by itself says little about a particular patient's symptoms or function (p272–274).

### Choose the patient outcome before labeling care as valuable

Figure 19-1 places cost and effect on outcomes on separate axes. The clearest high-value example improves what matters to the patient while using fewer resources. The clearest low-value example uses more resources without a corresponding benefit or exposes the patient to avoidable harm. The difficult comparisons lie between these extremes. A more expensive treatment can still be worthwhile for a substantial outcome gain; a cheaper alternative that leaves a person substantially worse off may not represent an improvement. Ask what is gained or lost, by whom and over what period (p263–264).

The unit of analysis changes the answer. A hospital department may save money by sending a patient home sooner, while the patient's family pays for urgent personal care or the patient returns through the emergency department. For an older adult whose goal is walking independently, a higher-cost intervention that restores mobility can matter more than a small improvement in a laboratory marker. A claim of “better value” that names only the immediate payer or only the first visit can miss the actual patient outcome and the total resources required (p263–265).

In geriatric care the individual's preferences also determine which outcomes count. More survival, less pain, preserved cognition, continence and the ability to live at home are not interchangeable. Ask a person choosing between treatments which effects they value and which burdens they would accept. Quality measures that reward a disease target can create conflict if the extra treatment produces adverse effects or treatment burden now while its expected benefit lies beyond the patient's likely time horizon. This is why the chapter treats value as a broader construct than compliance with a single guideline (p263–264, p273–274).

### Low-value care and the printed Choosing Wisely table

Table 19-1 illustrates decisions in which doing more can provide less value. For advanced dementia it describes assisted oral feeding in place of routine percutaneous feeding tubes. For anorexia or cachexia it calls attention to medication effects, social support, appealing food, feeding assistance and goals before prescribing an appetite stimulant or supplement by reflex. The common reasoning is to identify the limiting problem and the outcome sought. An intervention that changes intake on paper but adds burden without improving the patient's meaningful outcomes deserves reconsideration (p264).

A medication review may reveal that the proposed treatment addresses an adverse effect of another drug. The table warns against routine use of sedative-hypnotics as the initial response to insomnia, agitation or delirium in older adults; against physical restraint for delirious behavior; and against continuing a cholinesterase inhibitor without revisiting perceived benefit and gastrointestinal effects. These examples distinguish reassessment from withholding care. A clinician still needs to assess why the patient is distressed, what nonpharmacologic or other care is effective and whether an existing treatment is helping (p264).

The table's **10 numbered entries** include a printing duplication: the assisted-feeding recommendation appears as both the first and second item. Count the numbered lines and distinct text separately if reviewing it. Its other examples include inappropriate tight glycemic targeting, treating bacteriuria without compatible symptoms and ordering cancer screening without considering life expectancy, overdiagnosis and downstream treatment. The value question is the expected net benefit under the specified conditions, not whether a procedure, test or medicine is intrinsically wasteful in every indication (p264).

### Identify who carries financial risk and for which event

With fee-for-service payment, delivering another separately billable service can generate additional revenue. A performance incentive can be layered onto that base without changing who bears most costs. Under one-sided shared savings, an organization can receive part of the difference between a spending benchmark and lower actual spending when quality conditions are met. Under two-sided risk it can also owe money when spending exceeds the agreed threshold. Figure 19-2 arranges models by degree of risk transfer. A quality bonus, shared savings and capitation are related but not synonyms (p264–265).

In a bundled episode, a defined set of related services is considered together over an agreed interval. The book gives joint replacement with postdischarge accountability extending to **90 days** as an example. A hospital considering discharge home versus postacute institutional rehabilitation then has a financial reason to pay attention to the next setting. The appropriate disposition still depends on the patient's mobility, support and safety. Define what services are in the bundle and what happens to unrelated illness before inferring that the organization is responsible for all future costs (p265, p272).

Capitation is a fixed amount per enrolled person for covered care over a defined period. It rewards preventing avoidable illness and coordinating care, but also creates an incentive to avoid expensive patients or omit beneficial services. Case-mix adjustment and quality requirements address different parts of this problem. A higher payment for a more complex patient makes enrollment less financially unattractive; a quality requirement asks whether necessary care was actually delivered or an important outcome maintained. Neither device alone eliminates the need to assess patient experience (p265, p273–275).

For example, compare a hospital-attributed episode that ends after rehabilitation with a capitated plan responsible for longitudinal care. Investing in medication reconciliation might reduce costs within either interval. Investing now in a far less immediate preventive benefit might fall outside the bundle while remaining relevant to the longitudinal plan. This difference does not determine which intervention is medically appropriate. It explains why payment design can strengthen one care process and leave another without a funding mechanism even when both improve health (p264–266).

### Coordination and population health: what the organization does with the incentive

Coordination is more than referring to another professional. Table 19-2 includes teamwork, care management, communication, linking people to resources and supporting transitions. Consider a patient discharged with a new medication, a changed dose of a longstanding drug and a pending test. The hospital, community prescriber, pharmacist and patient each hold different pieces of the plan. Coordination requires reconciling the instructions, identifying who reviews the test and ensuring that the patient can obtain and take the intended medicines. Counting transmitted discharge summaries does not show these actions occurred (p265–266).

Population health management first identifies the people for whom the organization is accountable, then uses information about risk and care gaps to target services. A patient with past falls, repeated admissions or multiple chronic illnesses may warrant proactive outreach rather than waiting for another crisis. Risk prediction can help prioritize finite resources but does not establish what action will help a particular person. Review the modifiable cause: transportation, home support or medication harm may require a very different intervention from progression of a disease (p266).

Attribution defines the population. Under a retrospective arrangement a beneficiary's actual care pattern can assign responsibility after the measurement period. Under prospective attribution, previous use identifies the population before the period begins. The latter allows more advance planning but may not accurately predict where a person ultimately seeks care. In either case the patient may interact with clinicians outside the usual network. Ask what information the accountable team can see and what authority it has to coordinate care before attributing all utilization changes to its actions (p271).

### Distinguish the US programs by enrollment and responsibility

Under Medicare Advantage, the chapter describes private plans receiving risk-adjusted capitated payment for covered Medicare services. The plan can use network design and coordinated care to manage that responsibility. An institutional special-needs plan targets a group residing in or expected to need an institution over the period specified by the program. The institutional-equivalent variant applies an institutional level of need to people living in the community. The distinction is about residence and eligibility as well as medical complexity; a person does not become a nursing-home resident merely by meeting a level-of-care criterion (p267–269).

PACE integrates medical and long-term support for selected community residents aged **55 or older** who are certified as requiring nursing-home-level care and can safely remain in the program's service area with its support. The model's breadth matters. A change in transportation, adult day services, personal care or access to a clinician can prevent a hospital or facility admission. The chapter reports that only about **7%** of participants nationally resided in a nursing home despite the initial level-of-care requirement. Enrollment eligibility and subsequent place of residence are different outcomes (p267–270).

Hospice also coordinates care but around a different population and goal. The chapter's Medicare description requires certification of terminal illness with expected survival of **6 months or less**, election of comfort-oriented hospice services and a signed election. Payment is based on the day and varies with the level of care. It is not simply a bill for each individual home visit. A person with substantial palliative needs who is not electing hospice can still benefit from symptom management or advance care planning in other models; high need for support and hospice eligibility are not identical (p270).

A patient-centered medical home emphasizes accessible, comprehensive, coordinated primary care. An accountable care organization extends accountability to a broader network and to spending and quality for an attributed population. A practice can deliver medical-home functions while participating in an ACO, and an ACO may operate over underlying fee-for-service claims. In the latter arrangement, meeting savings and quality conditions can lead to a shared payment. Do not infer from the word “accountable” that every constituent practitioner is individually paid a single capitated sum (p271).

Table 19-3 also separates hospital-level programs, clinician performance payment, skilled nursing facility incentives and episode-based arrangements. A hospital readmission measure examines events after discharge; a value-based purchasing program combines selected performance domains; a clinician measure may concern professional services; and a surgical episode can incorporate postacute cost. Compare each program's accountable party, population, measurement period, financial consequence and intended quality outcome. The percentages and implementation dates in the book refer to the program periods described, not a universal penalty for every provider or year (p268–272).

### Measure structure, process, outcomes and patient experience

A structural measure asks whether a capability exists: for example, whether an organization has a team or system capable of identifying high-risk patients. A process measure asks what happened, such as review of a medication list or delivery of an intervention to eligible patients. An outcome assesses the result. Structure can facilitate a process without guaranteeing its delivery, and a reliably completed process can still have limited relevance if it is weakly connected to the outcome the patient values. Donabedian's framework helps make these distinct claims explicit (p272–273).

Intermediate clinical outcomes matter but need interpretation. Blood pressure or glycemic control may predict later illness, yet an aggressively pursued target can create immediate adverse effects. Direct outcomes such as pain, ability to perform a valued task or urinary continence describe something the patient experiences. Utilization outcomes such as hospital readmission are important to the patient and payer but still have multiple meanings: a needed admission can indicate appropriate rescue, whereas an avoidable readmission can indicate failure of follow-up. Count the event and assess its context (p272–274).

Define a measure before interpreting a percentage. Who belongs in the denominator? Which exclusions are appropriate, what is the time window and what data source supplies the numerator? An outcome obtained from claims may be readily available but miss a change in daily function. A detailed chart abstraction or patient report may answer the clinical question better but require staff time. If a service improves measured performance by changing documentation rather than care, the measure and the patient outcome have diverged. Verify that the measure is both feasible and faithful to the purpose (p272–275).

Multimorbidity changes the benefit–burden balance. An older adult taking multiple medicines may face conflicting recommendations for separate diseases, interactions, adverse effects and several appointments. A disease-specific target that benefits many middle-aged patients over years can have a different net effect in someone with limited life expectancy or marked vulnerability to treatment harm. Ask which important outcome the target predicts, how long it takes for benefit to appear and what the patient experiences in the meantime. An agreed individualized plan can be high value even when it differs from a generic process target (p273–274).

Consider how a team would evaluate an intervention to prevent hospital readmission. It might first count eligible patients who received coordinated follow-up, then ask whether unplanned readmissions changed over the specified interval. It should also ask whether patients could reach help when they needed it, whether functional status or symptom control worsened, and whether more care was simply shifted to families or another institution. An improvement in a single count can otherwise conceal a change in destination or a new barrier to appropriate care (p265–266, p272–274).

Similarly, a plan enrolling people with nursing-home-level needs should expect substantial use of long-term support. In PACE, the program continues to coordinate care and pays for nursing-home care if an enrolled person comes to need it. Therefore a move to a facility does not by itself mean the program abandoned coordination or failed to improve value. The more informative questions are whether the person's needs changed, whether safe community support remained possible, what the patient preferred and whether care in either setting achieved the intended goals. The same utilization event may signify appropriate care for one participant and an avoidable gap for another (p270).

### Risk adjustment, inequity and implementation

Raw outcome comparisons can penalize organizations treating sicker people. Table 19-5 illustrates how hierarchical condition categories weight documented illness for payment adjustment. In the illustrated version, diabetes with acute or chronic complications carries a weight of **0.37**, while diabetes without complications carries **0.12**. These are model weights, not absolute probabilities of admission or a dollar payment to the patient. The same table gives **0.30** for vascular disease and **0.41** for vascular disease with complications. The distinction illustrates how clinical specificity and coding can affect a benchmark (p275).

No coding system captures all dimensions of frailty, function, social support or poverty. A safety-net hospital can have worse measured outcomes influenced by what happens after discharge, including access to primary care or medication. Risk adjustment can make comparisons fairer, but adjusting away a difference without investigating it can also obscure a remediable inequity. Compare clinical need, resources and outcomes across populations and ask whether the proposed payment rule supports improvement or shifts money away from organizations that most need capacity to intervene (p269, p273–275).

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research in Table 19-4 shifts attention from “does the published intervention work?” to “can this organization deliver it here?” Consider the intervention's evidence, complexity and cost, the outer setting of patient needs and policy, the inner setting of leadership and communication, the staff members expected to act, and the process of planning, implementation and evaluation. A care-coordination model requiring a pharmacist may fail where no time is funded for medication review even if the electronic referral itself functions perfectly (p274).

Lastly, measurement itself has costs. Staff who enter several overlapping metrics have less time for direct care, and a measure optimized for public comparison may not tell a local team how to improve. Select an outcome important enough to justify that burden, a process that can actually influence it, and a way to detect unintended effects such as avoidance of complex patients or loss of access. A lower cost per attributed beneficiary becomes persuasive only when the population is comparable and outcomes meaningful to those beneficiaries are preserved or improved (p273–275).

## Exam traps

- A cheap service can still be low value when it offers little benefit or causes harm (p263–264).
- Shared savings can coexist with fee-for-service billing (p271).
- Risk adjustment and quality thresholds solve different problems: case mix versus incentives to withhold useful care (p265, p274).
