# Chapter 19 Study: Value-Based Care

## Summary

**Value and risk**
- Value means patient health outcomes relative to costs. Cost cutting alone can harm outcomes; quality targets alone may miss patient preferences or sustainability (p263).
- Low-value care provides little benefit, may harm patients, or wastes resources. Choosing Wisely lists identify targets, but lists alone have had limited effects (p264).
- Common elements: financial risk sharing, quality measurement, care coordination and population health management (p264-266).
- Upside risk rewards performance; downside risk permits losses. Bundles cover an episode; capitation pays per person. Quality thresholds help prevent withholding beneficial care (p265).

**Models in the United States**
- Medicare Advantage receives risk-adjusted capitation. Institutional special-needs plans serve institutional-level needs; equivalent plans extend this approach into the community (p267).
- PACE: **55 or older**, state-certified nursing-home level of care, living in its service area and able to live safely in the community at enrollment; provides integrated medical and long-term support (p270).
- Medicare hospice requires terminal certification of **6 months or less**, a comfort-care choice and signed election. Payment varies by care setting/intensity (p270).
- Medical homes coordinate comprehensive, accessible primary care; ACOs span wider provider networks and share savings or risk (p271).
- Hospital value-based purchasing/readmission programs, MIPS and SNF value-based purchasing tie payment to selected performance measures. Bundled programs extend accountability across an episode and postacute care (p271-272).

**Measure what matters**
- Donabedian: structure, process and outcomes. Separate intermediate markers from outcomes patients experience, including pain, function and continence (p272-273).
- Outcome comparisons need appropriate risk adjustment. Multimorbidity, drug interactions and competing recommendations complicate disease-specific measures in older adults (p273-274).
- Improvement depends on local implementation, leadership and context; there is no universally successful recipe (p274).
- Measurement burden can divert resources. Imperfect risk adjustment may disadvantage complex patients or safety-net services; monitor outcomes alongside spending (p274-275).
