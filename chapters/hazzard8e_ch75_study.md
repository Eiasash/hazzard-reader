## The pressure-loaded ventricle

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d2c5ea7ebee8dc1b114cfb92" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Aortic sclerosis thickens the leaflets but permits forward flow. Stenosis makes the left ventricle generate a higher systolic pressure to maintain output. The resulting hypertrophy can appear on ECG or echo while contraction is still preserved. The characteristic murmur rises and falls during systole and radiates to the carotids. The carotid upstroke can be small and delayed, although arterial stiffening makes this less obvious in older people. A bounding pulse with a wide pulse pressure points toward aortic regurgitation (pp1149–1150, 1154).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-451247c9890caf3f339aeb4d" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Why does the chest hurt?** Two-thirds of patients in the chapter's series presented with angina, and about 40% of those with angina had no significant coronary disease. Consider coexisting coronary disease first in the older patient. When there is no significant epicardial obstruction, a thick ventricle needs more oxygen while coronary flow reserve is reduced. The subendocardium is particularly vulnerable. Inadequate output during exertion helps explain syncope. Atrial fibrillation removes the atrial contribution to filling and can precipitate dyspnea (pp1150–1151).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-3e9b544b729728bac5b28c1c" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Why does a valve lesion cause bleeding?** A severely narrowed valve exposes circulating blood to high shear and impairs von Willebrand factor–dependent platelet adhesion. Epistaxis and easy bruising can precede a major bleed. Gastrointestinal bleeding from colonic angiodysplasia is the association called Heyde syndrome. The mechanism concerns primary hemostasis. Anemia due to vitamin B12 deficiency and intestinal villous atrophy would require a separate explanation (p1151).

## Surveillance and the decision to replace the aortic valve

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-76fb4ce0edbbab9f1ac529f2" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Table 75-1 grades stenosis using valve area, peak jet velocity and mean pressure gradient. It prints an area below 1 cm², velocity above 4 m/s and mean gradient above 40 mm Hg for the severe category. A high velocity and small area coupled with an unexpectedly low mean gradient, as in 2020 Q47, require checking flow and the Doppler measurements. Exertional angina in that setting warrants prompt evaluation. Syncope in someone whose valve area and velocity suggest only mild stenosis warrants a search for another cause (pp1150–1152, Tables 75-1 and 75-2).

**Ask what “asymptomatic” means in daily life.** Someone who stopped climbing stairs may no longer reach the workload that brought on dyspnea. Closely supervised exercise testing can uncover symptoms in a person with severe disease who appears asymptomatic. Symptoms, a flat blood-pressure response or ischemic ST changes identify a group much less likely to remain symptom-free. Exercise testing in symptomatic severe disease carries a risk of hemodynamic collapse (p1151).

**What changes management?** Severe symptomatic stenosis, severe stenosis with EF below 50%, or severe stenosis when another cardiac operation is planned are indications in Table 75-2. Thus a woman with severe stenosis who already needs CABG can have the valve replaced even with an EF of 55% and no symptoms. For an asymptomatic patient with preserved EF and no other trigger, examine the trajectory. Very high jet velocity, abnormal exercise response, markedly elevated BNP or rapid progression can favor earlier intervention (pp1151–1152, Table 75-2).

> Current practice (not the book): under 2025 European guidance, early replacement should also be considered alongside close surveillance when severe high-gradient stenosis is genuinely asymptomatic (normal exercise test if feasible), EF is at least 50%, and procedural risk is low. [ESC/EACTS 2025, recommendations for aortic stenosis](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf194)

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-a48ff09dba8d7490a501c290" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**When is the next echo due?** The chapter gives six to twelve months for severe, one to two years for moderate, and three to five years for mild asymptomatic stenosis. New exertional angina, syncope or heart failure changes this plan immediately. Asking the patient to report symptoms gives a way to act between scans (p1151).

## Surgery, catheter replacement or palliation

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-65b34859cab672fc287d6c20" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

With symptomatic severe stenosis, ask first whether relief of obstruction would meaningfully improve survival, symptoms or function in the time the patient is likely to live. Severe dementia, severe frailty and multiorgan impairment can favor active symptom management. In June 2025 Q22 an area of 0.6 cm², gradient of 50 mm Hg and velocity of 5 m/s already establish severe obstruction. The unresolved question is what outcome the patient could gain. A patient with mild cognitive impairment who wants to return home may have very different priorities and prospects (pp1151–1153, Table 75-3).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-4f72edde12c83ecaea816428 mcq-6773688c3c44df05d7ecb0b5" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-8b52ce59675a85ee8879d616" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Which procedure serves those goals?** A heart team can combine SAVR with coronary bypass or another valve operation. Prior chest irradiation, significant comorbidity or impaired mobility may favor TAVR when access and anatomy permit. The chapter's trial comparisons describe quicker recovery, less major bleeding and less postoperative atrial fibrillation with TAVR. They also describe more permanent pacemakers, paravalvular leakage and vascular complications. The transcatheter frame lies close to the conduction system, and its seal against a calcified annulus can be incomplete. Compare outcomes against the actual alternative under consideration, especially when surgery is prohibitive (pp1152–1153, Table 75-3).

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-4b12b2b09f6a219dc0a66d76" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**What can a balloon alone accomplish?** Inflation can widen a calcific valve enough for temporary relief or help stabilize a patient awaiting definitive treatment. The opening often remains small, and restenosis usually occurs within six to twelve months. A patient considering the procedure for palliation should hear both the potential for short-term relief and the procedural risk (p1152).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-92f1bc01da4974c018371e81" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Which patient is likely to tolerate surgery?** Page 1150 describes greater perioperative morbidity and mortality among older women with marked LV hypertrophy. Page 1152 lists right heart failure, renal impairment, advanced symptoms, emergency surgery and concomitant mitral surgery among predictors of operative mortality. Male sex is relatively favorable among the alternatives in June 2026 Q94. For an individual, combine operative risk estimates with cognition, nutritional status, strength, mobility, functional trajectory and the goals of the patient. STS-PROM is derived from operated patients and cannot by itself determine the outcome with conservative care (pp1150, 1152, 1160).

## Matching murmurs, pulses and voice changes to the lesion

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-8b25bc06ab666f9e2b1a5628" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

In chronic aortic regurgitation, blood returns from the aorta to the left ventricle during diastole. The widened pulse pressure and rapidly rising, collapsing Corrigan pulse follow from a large systolic stroke volume and diastolic runoff. Hill sign refers to a higher systolic pressure in the leg than in the arm. The usual auscultatory clue is a diastolic murmur. By contrast, stenosis impedes systolic ejection and produces a weak, late carotid upstroke. December 2021 Q70 asked for the pulse in regurgitation. May 2024 Basic Q104 asked for the lesion–finding pair (pp1150, 1153–1154, Table 75-4).

Mitral stenosis impedes left atrial emptying. Raised atrial pressure promotes pulmonary congestion and an enlarged atrium predisposes to atrial fibrillation and embolism. The enlarged left atrium can compress the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and cause hoarseness (Ortner syndrome). An opening snap and a diastolic rumble provide other bedside clues. Echo assesses valve morphology, valve area and pulmonary pressures (pp1155–1157, Table 75-7).

## Anticoagulation and endocarditis prophylaxis

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ca4ef9243406c07cf8276806" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Mechanical valves are durable but thrombogenic. A mechanical mitral prosthesis requires lifelong warfarin, usually targeting INR 3.0 within a range of 2.5–3.5. Aspirin and clopidogrel inhibit platelets and cannot substitute for this anticoagulation. Apixaban is unsuitable for a mechanical valve. Bioprosthetic valves present a different trade-off because they can structurally deteriorate and generally do not themselves require lifelong anticoagulation. Another indication, such as atrial fibrillation, may still require ongoing therapy (pp1161–1162, Table 75-15).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-de92a386e0ddf092e78f5d24" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

For prophylaxis, establish the cardiac risk and the procedure before choosing a drug. December 2021 Q51 describes routine diagnostic bronchoscopy in a patient with native rheumatic mitral stenosis. This scenario requires no endocarditis prophylaxis. Qualifying dental procedures in a patient with a prosthetic valve or previous endocarditis are a different setting. June 2025 Basic Q134 accepted two oral regimens for a penicillin-allergic patient. Table 75-14 lists **clindamycin 600 mg** or **cephalexin 2 g orally 1 hour before the procedure** for a penicillin-allergic adult (p1161).

> Current practice (not the book): AHA dental guidance instead includes azithromycin 500 mg 30–60 minutes before the procedure and removes clindamycin. [AHA prevention card, adult dental regimens](https://www.heart.org/-/Media/Files/Health-Topics/Infective-Endocarditis/Infective-Endocarditis-Wallet-Card.Pdf)

## Other valve decisions

**Aortic regurgitation.** Abrupt severe regurgitation can cause shock before the left ventricle adapts. Endocarditis and acute aortic dissection are important causes. In chronic regurgitation, a dilating ventricle initially accommodates the extra volume. Serial EF and ventricular dimensions can reveal deterioration before overt heart failure. Symptoms attributable to severe regurgitation warrant surgical assessment, even when resting EF appears preserved (pp1153–1155, Tables 75-5 and 75-6).

**Mitral stenosis.** Slowing a rapid heart rate allows more time for filling across a narrowed valve. Diuresis can ease pulmonary congestion. For symptomatic rheumatic stenosis, mobile leaflets, little calcification and limited subvalvular thickening favor balloon commissurotomy. Left atrial thrombus and moderate or severe regurgitation weigh against it. A heavily calcified annulus in an older adult makes operative reconstruction more hazardous (pp1156–1158, Tables 75-7 to 75-9).

**Mitral regurgitation.** Primary disease affects the leaflets or supporting structures. Secondary regurgitation results from ventricular remodeling and annular dilation. Acute papillary muscle or chordal rupture can produce pulmonary edema or shock. A chronic apical holosystolic murmur often radiates toward the axilla and may soften when ventricular and atrial pressures equalize. For suitable primary disease, durable repair preserves ventricular geometry and the supporting apparatus. EF can appear preserved despite evolving dysfunction because some ejection is into the low-pressure atrium. Assess ventricular dimensions, symptoms, pulmonary pressure and new atrial fibrillation when planning intervention (pp1158–1160, Tables 75-10 and 75-11).

**Choosing the prosthesis.** Mechanical valves trade lifelong anticoagulation and bleeding risk for durability. Bioprostheses deteriorate more slowly in older recipients and their aortic position generally outlasts their mitral position. A homograft may be useful when endocarditis has destroyed surrounding tissue. A Ross operation substitutes the patient's pulmonary valve for the aortic valve and requires another valve in the pulmonary position. Its complexity and small incremental durability benefit limit its role in older adults. Surgical sutureless valves still require an open approach and bypass (pp1161–1163).

## Summary

**Aortic stenosis (AS)**
- Prevalence **2–9%** of older patients; leading clinically significant valvular disorder in older adults (p1149). **90%** of AS in patients >65y is calcific degeneration of a tricuspid valve (p1149).
- Valve sclerosis (thickening, no obstruction): **25%** of >65y, **48%** of >75y; progresses to frank stenosis in **~10%** within 5 years (p1150).
- Average progression: jet velocity **+0.3 m/s/yr**, valve area **−0.1 cm²/yr** (p1150).
- **Severe AS (Table 75-1, p1150):** aortic valve area **<1.0 cm²**, jet velocity **>4 m/s**, mean gradient **>40 mm Hg**.
  > Current practice (not the book): ACC/AHA describes severe high-gradient AS as peak velocity **≥4.0 m/s** or mean gradient **≥40 mm Hg**; valve area is typically **≤1.0 cm²** — [ACC/AHA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000923).
- Classic triad once symptomatic: angina (**50%** 5-yr survival untreated), syncope (**50%** 3-yr mortality), heart failure (**50%** mortality at 2 yr — worst prognosis) (p1151).
- Sudden death <1% while truly asymptomatic (p1150); acquired platelet/von Willebrand dysfunction from severe AS causes epistaxis or ecchymoses in **20%** of patients, and patients can also develop Heyde syndrome (GI bleeding from colonic angiodysplasias); these abnormalities resolve with valve replacement (p1151).
- Exercise testing in "asymptomatic" severe AS unmasks symptoms in up to a third; positive test (symptoms, flat BP response, ST changes) → **19%** 2-yr symptom-free survival vs **85%** if negative (p1151).
- 5-yr symptom-free probability even in severe AS averages only **50%** (p1151).
- BAV: enlargement rarely **> 1.0 cm²**, **10%** complication rate, restenosis by 6mo–1yr, 1-yr mortality **35–50%** — bridge/palliation only, not a replacement substitute (p1152).
- SAVR bioprosthetic structural deterioration **~10%** at 15–20 yr; Medicare operative mortality **8.8%** overall, **6.0%** at high-volume centers (p1152).
- Referral bias: **41%** of >70y (European study) and **33%** of >75y (second study: 1200 patients, 92 centers, 25 countries) with severe symptomatic AS were not offered surgery/valve replacement (p1152).
- Hazzard defines **prohibitive SAVR risk** as an STS predicted risk of morbidity and mortality **over 50%** due to comorbidity or serious irreversible conditions. It describes TAVR durability **beyond five years as unknown** and notes the possibility of valve-in-valve reintervention (p1153).
  > Current practice (not the book): randomized NOTION follow-up reports valve outcomes through **10 years** in lower-risk patients — [NOTION follow-up, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae043).
- Table 75-3 (SAVR vs TAVR vs palliative selection): SAVR favored for longer life expectancy, few frailty markers, TAVR-unsuitable anatomy, or concomitant coronary artery disease or valve disease (lower reintervention/PPM/vascular-complication risk, longer stay); TAVR favored for shorter life expectancy, severe comorbidities/mobility issues, or prior chest irradiation (shorter stay, but accepts possible risk of repeat intervention and higher PPM/vascular-complication risk) (p1153).
- Palliative care (same table): symptom relief without life prolongation, avoiding periprocedural stroke/PPM/vascular-complication risk — for limited life expectancy, severe dementia/frailty/multiorgan impairment, valve anatomy/access unsuitable for TAVR, or goals of care against futile procedures (p1153).

**Aortic insufficiency (AI)**
- Chronic AI in **20–30%** of >65y (p1153).
- Table 75-4 eponymous AI signs (owner-highlighted): Austin Flint murmur = low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble; Duroziez sign = to-and-fro femoral artery murmur with compression; Quincke sign = pulsatile nail beds; Watson's water-hammer pulse = bounding peripheral pulse; Corrigan pulse = rapidly rising/falling pulse on palpation (p1154).
- Asymptomatic, normal LV function: **0.2%** incidence of sudden death, **3.5%**/yr progress to symptoms, **6%**/yr develop LV dysfunction or symptoms; once LV dysfunction develops, **>25%**/yr progress to symptomatic disease (p1153).
- Symptomatic mortality **>10%**/yr for angina, **>20%**/yr for heart failure (medical management only) (p1154).
- NYHA III–IV: **25%**/yr mortality; NYHA II: **6%**/yr (p1153).
- LVESD **> 50 mm**: **19%**/yr develop dysfunction/symptoms/death; LVESD 40–50mm: **6%**/yr (p1153).
- Table 75-5 severe cutoffs: jet-width/LVOT ratio **≥ 65%**, vena contracta **≥ 0.6 cm**, regurgitant volume **≥ 60 mL/beat**, EROA **≥ 30 mm²** (p1154).
- **Severe chronic AR (Table 75-6, p1155):** valve replacement is indicated for symptomatic severe AR regardless of LV function, asymptomatic severe AR with **EF <50%**, or severe AR during other cardiac surgery. Consider surgery with normal LV function and **LVESD >50 mm**, or for moderate AR during other cardiac surgery. Progressive LV dilatation **>65 mm** with low surgical risk is a possible indication.
  > Current practice (not the book): <span id="current-practice-1" data-reader-metadata="navigation"></span> ACC/AHA uses **LVEF ≤55%** for stage C2 severe AR and distinguishes **LVESD >50 mm** or **indexed LVESD >25 mm/m²** from progressive severe LV end-diastolic enlargement; the 65-mm AR threshold is LV end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD >65 mm, progressive, low surgical risk: may be considered); LVESD >50 mm is the end-systolic trigger — ACC/AHA valvular guideline, 2020.
- Severe LV dysfunction: operative mortality **at least 10%**, with lower postsurgical survival (p1155).

**Mitral stenosis (MS)**
- Overwhelming majority rheumatic in etiology; normal MVA **4.0–5.0 cm²**, shrinks **0.09–0.32 cm²/yr**; symptoms usually appear once area falls to **2.5–1.5 cm²** (p1156).
- Symptom onset lags rheumatic fever by **20–40 years** (p1156).
- Once symptomatic, 10-yr survival only **50–60%**; asymptomatic 10-yr survival **80%**; unoperated despite meeting surgical criteria: 10-yr survival **< 30%** (p1156).
- **Severe mitral stenosis in Table 75-7 (p1156): mitral valve area <1.0 cm²**, pulmonary artery pressure **>50 mm Hg** and mean gradient **>10 mm Hg**. Gradient varies with flow and heart rate.
  > Current practice (not the book): ACC/AHA staging defines severe rheumatic MS by mitral valve area **≤1.5 cm²** and very severe MS by **≤1.0 cm²** — [ACC/AHA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000923).
- **30–40%** of older MS patients present with AF; with AF, 10-yr survival **25%** vs **46%** if they remain in sinus rhythm (p1156).
- Systemic emboli in **20%** of patients (p1157).
- Percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty: **90%** get symptomatic relief; freedom from complications/death **50–65%** at 7 yr (up to **80–90%** with favorable pre-procedure score) (p1157).
- Table 75-8 procedural risks: LV perforation **0.5–4.0%**, systemic embolization **0.5–3.0%**, MI **0.3–0.5%**, death **1.0–2.0%** (p1157).
- Surgical operative mortality up to **20%** in older patients with comorbidities and pulmonary hypertension (p1158).

**Mitral regurgitation (MR)**
- **~2%** of the population; mitral valve prolapse (**1–2.5%** of population) is the most common cause (p1158).
- Severe chronic MR (especially with a flail leaflet): **7%**/yr mortality (p1158).
- 10-yr survival by EF: **32%** if EF < 50%, **53%** if borderline EF 50–60%, **70%** if EF > 60% — all untreated (p1158).
- Table 75-10 severe cutoffs: vena contracta **≥ 0.7 cm**, regurgitant volume **≥ 60 mL/beat**, RF **≥ 50%**; EROA row prints "**≥ 40**" cm² — almost certainly a misprint for ≥0.40 (see exam-trap note) (p1158).
- **Mitral repair durability:** p1159 describes good durability but prints 7–10% as “freedom from reoperation,” an inconsistent endpoint label.
  > Current practice (not the book): in a surgical cohort of 1,604 patients, actual freedom from reoperation after repair was **93.6% at 10 years**. Durability depends on anatomy, repair technique and population — [Glauber et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-015-0390-y).
- Operative mortality: **2%** for repair vs **6%** for replacement overall; a 292-patient >70y cohort found **0.7%** in-hospital mortality for repair vs **14%** for replacement (p1160).
- Elsewhere in the same chapter, repair-vs-replacement mortality is instead quoted as **1–2%** vs **5.4–6.4%** (p1163) — a second, different pair of numbers for the same comparison; both as printed.

**Perioperative risk, anticoagulation, prosthetic valves**
- Table 75-12 risk criteria (combine per column, not PROM alone; organ counts = major organ dysfunction that will not improve postoperatively): low = STS-PROM **< 4%** *and* no frailty *and* no organ dysfunction *and* no procedure impediment; intermediate = **4–8%** *or* 1 frailty index *or* 1 organ system *or* possible impediment; high = **> 8%**, ≥2 frailty indices, ≤2 organ systems, possible impediment; prohibitive = death/morbidity risk **> 50%** *or* ≥3 organ systems *or* severe impediment (p1160).
- **Endocarditis prophylaxis for eligible adults (Table 75-14, p1161):** give **amoxicillin 2 g PO 1 hour before**. For penicillin allergy the table lists **clindamycin 600 mg** or **cephalexin 2 g PO 1 hour before**. If oral dosing is impossible, the table lists ampicillin 2 g IV/IM 30 minutes before, or clindamycin 600 mg IV or cefazolin 1 g IV/IM 30 minutes before for penicillin allergy.
  > Current practice (not the book): clindamycin is no longer recommended for dental prophylaxis; penicillin-allergic options are cephalexin, azithromycin/clarithromycin or doxycycline PO, or cefazolin/ceftriaxone IM/IV — but no cephalosporin after anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria with penicillin/ampicillin — AHA endocarditis prophylaxis wallet card.
- **Prosthetic valves (Table 75-15, p1161):** a mechanical aortic valve uses **INR 2–3**, or **2.5–3.5** with additional risk factors; a mechanical mitral valve uses **INR 2.5–3.5**. The table adds **aspirin 75–325 mg/day** for mechanical valves. For a bioprosthetic aortic or mitral valve without additional risk factors, it gives **INR 2–3 for 3 months plus aspirin 75–100 mg/day**. With risk factors the printed bioprosthetic ranges are **2–3** for aortic and **2.5–3.5** for mitral, with aspirin **75–100 mg/day**.
  > Current practice (not the book): US guidance targets INR 2.5 for a current-generation mechanical aortic valve without additional thromboembolic risk and 3.0 for a mechanical mitral valve; VKA to INR 2.5 for 3–6 months is reasonable after selected surgical bioprosthetic replacements. With a mechanical valve on VKA, adding low-dose aspirin 75–100 mg/day for antiplatelet therapy requires a separate indication (ESC/EACTS: concomitant symptomatic atherosclerotic disease) and low bleeding risk; ESC/EACTS 2025 class IIa, level B — ESC/EACTS 2025, Eur Heart J 2025;46:4635; ACC/AHA 2020 class 2b, level B-R — ACC/AHA 2020, Circulation 2021;143:e142.
- Thromboembolism risk **1–2%/yr** mechanical vs **0.7%/yr** bioprosthetic, lower still for aortic- vs mitral-position prostheses (p1161).
- **Supratherapeutic INR (p1161):** Hazzard states that **INR 5–10** can be treated by holding warfarin and giving **oral vitamin K 1–2.5 mg**. Monitor INR daily until it falls below 5.
  > Current practice (not the book): INR 4.5–10 with no bleeding: suggest against routine vitamin K; INR >10 without bleeding: oral vitamin K; major bleeding: 4-factor PCC plus vitamin K 5–10 mg slow IV — ACCP/CHEST 9th ed, 2012.
- **Mechanical aortic valve without thromboembolic risk factors:** the book gives a **48–72-hour** preoperative warfarin hold, restart **within 24 hours after surgery**, and **no heparin bridge**. It describes bridging for a mechanical mitral valve or a mechanical aortic valve with high-risk features (p1162).
  > Current practice (not the book): <span id="current-practice-2" data-reader-metadata="navigation"></span>CHEST suggests a warfarin hold of **at least five days** when interruption is necessary and resumption **within 24 hours** when hemostasis permits. CHEST suggests against routine bridging. ACC/AHA allows individualized bridging for higher-risk mechanical valves and advises interruption without bridging for a bileaflet mechanical aortic valve without additional thromboembolic risk. [CHEST 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chest.2022.07.025) · [ACC/AHA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000923).
- Bioprosthetic durability by age: **90%** freedom from structural deterioration at 15–20 yr at age 70; **90–95%** freedom from reoperation if age >75 (p1162).
- Aortic valve repair for calcific disease is rarely durable, but for normal leaflets it achieves **85%** freedom from reoperation at 10 years (p1163).

**Exam traps / book self-contradictions**
- Table 75-10's severe regurgitant-orifice-area cutoff for MR prints "**≥ 40**" cm²; the row's other two columns (<0.20, 0.20–0.39) make **≥ 0.40** the obviously intended value — as printed, not corrected in this build (p1158).
- Mitral repair-vs-replacement operative mortality is given twice with different numbers: **2% vs 6%** overall / **0.7% vs 14%** in a >70y cohort (p1160), and later **1–2% vs 5.4–6.4%** (p1163) — both as printed.
- MS "usually develop symptoms" once MVA falls to **2.5 to 1.5 cm²** (p1156), which overlaps Table 75-7's own **mild** cutoff (>1.5 cm²) — read literally, symptom onset and "mild" grade can coincide.
- With asymptomatic severe AR and preserved LV function, Table 75-6 lists **LVESD >50 mm** for consideration of surgery. For low-risk surgery the table describes progressive LV dilatation **>65 mm** without specifying the dimension, while the narrative specifically calls it **LVESD** (p1155). See the [current-practice comparison](#current-practice-1).

## Recall

**1. In patients older than 65, what fraction of aortic stenosis is caused by calcific degeneration of a trileaflet valve, and at what age does a bicuspid valve's stenosis typically present?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

90% of AS in patients over 65 is calcific degeneration of a tricuspid valve; bicuspid-valve stenosis usually presents earlier, in the fourth to sixth decade (p1149).

</details>

**2. Name the classic triad of symptoms once aortic stenosis becomes symptomatic, and rank them by average survival without valve replacement.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Angina (~50% 5-yr survival), syncope (~50% 3-yr mortality), and heart failure (~50% mortality at 2 years — the worst prognosis of the three) (p1151).

</details>

**3. On exercise testing, what result predicts a poor 2-year symptom-free survival in "asymptomatic" severe aortic stenosis, and what are the two survival rates?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Development of symptoms, failure to generate a 20 mmHg increase in blood pressure, or ST-segment abnormalities predicts a 19% 2-year symptom-free survival, versus 85% if none of these occur (p1151).

</details>

**4. What STS risk cutoff defines "prohibitive" surgical risk for TAVR consideration, and what is the durability caveat for TAVR valves?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hazzard defines prohibitive SAVR risk as **STS predicted morbidity and mortality >50%** due to comorbidity or serious irreversible conditions. It describes TAVR valve durability **beyond five years as unknown** (p1153).
> Current practice (not the book): the NOTION randomized trial subsequently reported **10-year** outcomes in lower-risk patients — [NOTION follow-up, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehae043).

</details>

**6. What annual rate of LV dysfunction, symptoms or death was reported in asymptomatic aortic insufficiency with an end-systolic dimension greater than 50 mm, versus 40–50 mm?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

19%/year if LVESD > 50 mm, versus 6%/year if LVESD is 40–50 mm (p1153).

</details>

**7. In asymptomatic severe aortic regurgitation with preserved LV function, how do LV end-systolic and progressive LV enlargement affect referral for surgery?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Table 75-6 lists **LVESD >50 mm** as a reason to consider surgery and progressive LV dilatation **>65 mm** as a possible indication when surgical risk is low. The table leaves the latter dimension unspecified; the accompanying prose calls it **LVESD** (p1155).
> [Current practice note](#current-practice-1)

</details>

**8. What mitral valve area range typically corresponds to symptom onset in mitral stenosis, and what is the normal MVA?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Symptoms usually develop once the valve area falls to 2.5–1.5 cm²; normal MVA is 4.0–5.0 cm² (p1156).

</details>

**9. In mitral stenosis, how does 10-year survival compare between patients who remain in sinus rhythm and those who develop atrial fibrillation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

46% 10-year survival in sinus rhythm versus 25% with atrial fibrillation (p1156).

</details>

**10. What echocardiographic scoring system selects patients for percutaneous mitral balloon valvuloplasty, and what features give the most favorable (lowest) score?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The Wilkins Score; the lowest (most favorable) scores go to valves with the greatest leaflet mobility, least subvalvular thickening, most normal leaflet thickness, and least calcification (p1157).

</details>

**11. What is the most common cause of mitral regurgitation, and roughly what fraction of the population does it affect?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Mitral valve prolapse, present in 1% to 2.5% of the population (p1158).

</details>

**12. Compare operative mortality for mitral valve repair versus replacement, both in the overall population and in a cohort of patients older than 70.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Overall: 2% (repair) vs 6% (replacement). In a 292-patient cohort older than 70: 0.7% (repair) vs 14% (replacement) in-hospital mortality (p1160).

</details>

**13. What INR range and duration of anticoagulation apply to a bioprosthetic aortic valve without additional risk factors?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Table 75-15 gives **INR 2–3 for the first 3 months plus aspirin 75–100 mg daily** after bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement without additional risk factors. For a mechanical aortic valve it gives **INR 2–3**, increasing to **2.5–3.5** with thromboembolic risk factors, plus **aspirin 75–325 mg daily** (p1161).
> Current practice (not the book): ACC/AHA considers VKA to a target INR of **2.5 for 3–6 months** reasonable after selected surgical bioprosthetic aortic or mitral replacement — [ACC/AHA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000923).

</details>

**14. Before elective surgery, how long can warfarin be held preoperatively in a patient with a mechanical aortic valve and no risk factors, and when is it restarted — with or without heparin bridging?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hazzard gives a **48–72-hour** preoperative hold for a mechanical aortic valve without thromboembolic risk factors. Restart warfarin **within 24 hours after surgery**. In this setting the book specifies **no bridging heparin** (p1162).
> [Current practice note](#current-practice-2)

</details>

**15. Which oral regimens does Table 75-14 give for dental endocarditis prophylaxis in an eligible patient with penicillin allergy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The printed table lists **clindamycin 600 mg PO** or **cephalexin 2 g PO**, given once **1 hour before the procedure** (Table 75-14, p1161).
> Current practice (not the book): AHA dental guidance removes clindamycin. For eligible adults with penicillin allergy, give cephalexin 2 g, azithromycin or clarithromycin 500 mg, or doxycycline 100 mg PO **30–60 minutes before** the procedure. Avoid cephalosporins after anaphylaxis, angioedema or urticaria with penicillin or ampicillin. [AHA prevention card, dental regimens based on the 2021 scientific statement](https://www.heart.org/-/media/Files/Health-Topics/Infective-Endocarditis/Infective-Endocarditis-Wallet-Card.pdf).

</details>
