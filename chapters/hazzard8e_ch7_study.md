# Chapter 7 Study: Decision Making and Advance Care Planning: What Matters Most

## Summary

**Start with what matters**
- Decisions should integrate medical evidence with the person's goals, preferences, function and life context (p109–111).
- Multiple conditions, atypical presentations and uncertain prognosis complicate disease-specific recommendations (p110).
- Ask who the patient wants involved in decisions, including a possible future surrogate (p111).
- Social circumstances, cognition, mood and practical function are part of the decision-making assessment (p111).

**Use tests purposefully**
- Ask whether a test is accurate, tolerable and likely to change management toward outcomes the patient values (p112).
- Consider pretest probability, treatment availability, comorbidity and whether the patient would accept the proposed intervention (p112).
- A technically informative result may offer little benefit when the subsequent treatment does not fit the patient's goals (p112–113).

**Share the decision**
- Clinicians contribute medical expertise; patients contribute expertise about themselves. Shared decisions require a conversation about both (p113).
- Elicit priorities across longevity, comfort, cognition and physical function, rather than demanding isolated choices about every treatment (p113).
- Adapt the setting for sensory/cognitive impairment; use a quiet, well-lit, unhurried discussion and additional encounters when needed (p113).
- Explore prognosis and help patients identify feasible goals when their preferred outcome cannot be achieved (p113).

**Advance care planning**
- ACP is an iterative process: explore values, identify a surrogate and translate preferences into care plans (p113).
- Document discussions through advance directives, clinical records or appropriate portable medical orders (p113).
- For serious life-limiting illness, including advanced frailty, voluntary discussion of life-sustaining treatment preferences may lead to a POLST form in U.S. settings where available (p113).
- Revisit planning across care settings and illness changes; use communication tools and account for family, cultural, spiritual and religious values (p114–115).
