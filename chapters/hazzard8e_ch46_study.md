# Pressure Injuries

Identify the tissue injury and relieve the load first. Stage, perfusion, exudate and infection then determine the next wound-care decision.

## Tissue injury and risk

PI = localized skin/soft-tissue damage over a bony prominence or device; sacrum and heels are the most common sites (<a href="?chapter=46#p679">p679</a>).

**~70%** of PI occur in people **>65y**; prevalence **9–22%** in nursing-home (NH) residents, **5–32%** in hospitalized patients (<a href="?chapter=46#p679">p679</a>).

Joint Commission estimate: **2.5 million** US acute-care patients treated for PI/year (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>); separate annual cost estimate **~$27 billion** (2016) (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>).

Pain: of people with PI who are able to report pain, **87%** report pain with dressing changes; only **6%** of those reporting PI pain receive any pain medication (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>).

When PI is the source of bacteremia, mortality is **48%** (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>).

Hospital-acquired PI: **11%** in-hospital mortality, **15%** mortality at 30 days post-discharge (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>).

NH residents: PI unhealed at 6 months → **64%** mortality vs **11%** if healed (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">p680</a>).

4 mechanisms: direct high-magnitude deformation, capillary-occlusion ischemia, shear-induced ischemia, and reduced tissue tolerance (malnutrition, arterial disease, neuropathy, moisture, prior scar) (<a href="?chapter=46#p682">p682</a>).

Muscle cell membranes rupture at **>80%** deformation; **2 h** of strain **>50%** causes irreversible muscle damage (<a href="?chapter=46#p681">p681</a>).

Inflammatory/edema changes can occur **2–7 days** before visible skin breakdown (preclinical stage) (<a href="?chapter=46#p681">p681</a>).

## Assessment and prevention

In one monitored geriatric study, none developed PI with **>50** spontaneous body movements/night vs **90%** PI with **≤20** movements/night (<a href="?chapter=46#p682">p682</a>).

High-risk flags: age **>75y**, age >65 with scheduled surgery **≥3h**, hospital transport time **>1h** (<a href="?chapter=46#p682">p682</a>); ICU surgery **≥8h** (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>).

Braden scale scores **six** risk domains (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>).

Reassessment cadence: acute care every shift; home health weekly ×**4** weeks then every other week; NH weekly ×**4** weeks then quarterly (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>).

General turning interval **q2–3h** (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>); RCT of **942** NH residents on high-density foam mattresses randomized to q2h/q3h/q4h turning (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>) found no significant difference in PI incidence: **2.5%** vs **0.6%** vs **3.1%** (<a href="?chapter=46#p684">p684</a>).

Position at **30°** side-lying; chairbound patients repositioned **hourly** (shift weight **q15min**), hip flexion **90°** (<a href="?chapter=46#p684">p684</a>).

Standard mattress **4-inch** viscoelastic foam; OR risk = surgery **>3h**, ASA **≥3**, or prone position (<a href="?chapter=46#p684">p684</a>).

Prophylactic silicone foam dressings (critical care patients) cut heel PI (**3%** vs **12%**) and sacral PI (**1%** vs **5%**), NNT **10** (<a href="?chapter=46#p685">p685</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=46#book-table-46-1">Table 46-1</a>** risk-factor–specific interventions: incontinence/moisture → scheduled toileting/prompted voiding if responsive, check-and-change programs otherwise, plus protective creams; friction/shearing → cornstarch/lubricants between surfaces plus footboards; dry skin → gentle cleansers, moisturize, inspect daily, avoid massaging reddened areas (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>).

**Pitfall.** **Table heading.** <a href="?chapter=46#book-table-46-1">Table 46-1</a>'s column header reads "PREVENTION INTERVENTONS" (sic, typo in the original) (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">p683</a>).

## Recognizing and staging injury

### Read the skin and remove the load

A purple buttock lesion can represent extensive damage before the skin opens. Intense pressure transfers force through the soft tissue to the bone–muscle interface. Ischemic muscle is especially vulnerable to deformation. Persistent maroon, purple or deep red color, a dark wound bed after epidermal separation, or a blood-filled blister suggests **deep tissue pressure injury**. A serum-filled blister can also occur in stage 2. Skin color can obscure erythema, so compare temperature, firmness and tenderness with adjacent tissue. Direct palpation of a deep structure is sufficient for stage 4. Do not assign a shallower new stage as granulation fills a healing wound (<a href="?chapter=46#p681">pp. 681</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p685">685</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p688">688</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#book-figure-46-1">Figure 46-1</a>).

**Stage 1**: intact skin, nonblanchable erythema; reversible in **1–3 weeks** (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

**Stage 3**: full-thickness, fat visible, no bone/muscle/tendon exposed (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>-<a href="?chapter=46#p687">687</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#book-figure-46-1">Figure 46-1</a>).

**Stage 4**: full-thickness with bone/muscle/tendon/cartilage exposed (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

**Deep tissue PI (DTPI)**: discoloration can appear **~48h** after intense pressure (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

Early work suggests ultrasound may detect damage before clinical signs: **~80%** with abnormal images had no documented erythema (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

**Who needs assistance?** Ask whether the patient can change position enough to unload the affected site. The Norton scale rates physical condition, mental condition, activity, mobility and incontinence. Activity concerns getting up or walking, whereas mobility concerns the ability to change body position. Lower scores indicate greater vulnerability. Braden evaluates six risk domains. Both approaches should lead to action such as offloading, help with mobility or moisture protection. Hazzard describes risk assessment on admission, with reassessment each shift in acute care or when the clinical condition changes (<a href="?chapter=46#p682">pp. 682</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p683">683</a>). [Norton scale, AHRQ Tool 3E](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/resource/pressureulcer/tool/pu7b.html)

**Prevent heel and sacral injury after stroke or sepsis.** Support the calf so the heel floats, and confirm that it stays clear of the mattress. A pillow can work for prevention if it stays in place. For an established heel wound the chapter favors a heel-offloading device because pillows collapse or move. A thin padded heel protector reduces rubbing but may leave pressure unchanged. In bed, a 30-degree side tilt unloads the sacrum. Keep head elevation as low as the patient's condition allows to reduce sliding and shear. In the chair, address posture and foot support, help dependent patients change position hourly and encourage those who can to shift weight every 15 minutes (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">pp. 683</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p685">685</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p690">690</a>).

**Which surface helps?** A foam or similar reactive surface redistributes weight by allowing the body to sink in and by supporting its contours. An active alternating-air surface cycles support between air cells, useful at high risk when frequent movement is difficult. A support surface complements assisted turning. The usual interval in bed is two to three hours, adjusted to mobility, clinical condition and surface. In 942 nursing-home residents on high-density foam, injury rates were similar across two-, three- and four-hour schedules. That result cannot prescribe a single interval for a patient in septic shock. For an established stage 3 wound, consider a specialty surface and keep weight off the wound. A retrospective comparison found faster area reduction on air-fluidized beds than on the two comparison surfaces. June 2025 cited <a href="?chapter=46#p698">p. 698</a> for pressure redistribution, while the treatment comparison appears on <a href="?chapter=46#p692">p. 692</a> (<a href="?chapter=46#p683">pp. 683</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p684">684</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p689">689</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p692">692</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p698">698</a>).

**Stage 2**: partial-thickness, open dermis; should heal in **2–4 weeks** if properly treated (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

**Unstageable**: full-thickness, depth obscured by slough/eschar (<a href="?chapter=46#p687">p687</a>).

**Blanchable erythema** pales with gentle pressure and the color returns on release. The chapter describes a flat patch **larger than 1 cm** (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>). Size alone does not determine blanchability.

Thermography: in one NH study, areas of blanchable erythema with cooler skin temperatures were more likely to develop necrotic ulcers within **7 days**; capacitance devices read in **3–8 seconds** (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

**Pitfall.** **Pressure injury staging.** The chapter uses the 2016 NPIAP revision (<a href="?chapter=46#p686">p686</a>).

## Wound bed, infection and debridement

### Remove necrosis safely and recognize deep infection

**What determines the pace of debridement?** A large necrotic buttock wound with spreading redness, a purulent collection or sepsis needs prompt source control. Surgical or sharp debridement can rapidly expose the bed, drain a pocket and define concealed tracts. Check perfusion and bleeding risk, including anticoagulants. A stable wound with limited necrosis may instead undergo autolysis, with macrophages and endogenous enzymes working under a moist occlusive dressing. Autolysis is slow and is unsuitable with untreated infection, extensive necrosis, large undermined or tunneled wounds, impaired immunity or severe malnutrition. Topical collagenase is faster than autolysis and may follow sharp debridement. Iodine inhibits collagenase. Wet-to-dry gauze is nonselective and can remove new granulation as well as dead tissue (<a href="?chapter=46#p695">pp. 695</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p696">696</a>).

**Five** methods: surgical/sharp, mechanical, autolytic, enzymatic, bio-surgical (<a href="?chapter=46#p695">p695</a>).

Collagenase (enzymatic): iodine inhibits its activity; nanocrystalline silver "can be inhibited when used in combination with collagenase" (<a href="?chapter=46#p696">p696</a>).

Surgical repair: complication rate up to **50%**, but **70–80%** healed at discharge; same-site recurrence lower in older patients (**40%**) than younger paraplegics (**>70%**); mortality in older patients ranges from nearly **50% to 68%** (long-term outcomes; <a href="?chapter=46#p695">p695</a>).

**Stable eschar on an ischemic heel is an exception.** Dry, adherent, intact eschar without erythema or fluctuance can protect tissue that would not heal after excision. Offload, assess arterial supply and follow for new drainage, bogginess, spreading inflammation or systemic illness. A photograph cannot establish perfusion. The clinical plan supported by the dry eschar and peripheral arterial disease is offloading and vascular assessment. Antibiotics, debridement and compression each require their own indication (<a href="?chapter=46#p688">pp. 688</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p689">689</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p696">696</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#book-figure-46-1">Figures 46-1</a> and <a href="?chapter=46#book-figure-46-2">46-2</a>).

Maggot (bio-surgical) density **5–8/cm²**; complete debridement **80%** vs **48%** with standard care (<a href="?chapter=46#p696">p696</a>).

**Colonization, cellulitis and osteomyelitis require different actions.** Chronic wounds can carry bacteria without invasive infection. A routine swab of drainage samples these organisms. If deep infection is suspected, obtain tissue after cleansing or debridement when feasible. Spreading cellulitis, systemic illness or bone infection calls for systemic treatment. An afebrile patient can still have cellulitis and marked inflammatory abnormalities. When bacteremia is suspected, empiric therapy covers aerobic gram-negative rods, gram-positive cocci and anaerobes while reliable cultures are obtained. Narrow therapy when the causative pathogens are identified (<a href="?chapter=46#p693">pp. 693</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p695">695</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p697">697</a>).

A nonhealing heel, new severe pain, warmth and elevated CRP merit investigation even with a normal leukocyte count. Visible or palpable rough bone increases suspicion of osteomyelitis. Imaging can show its extent, and bone biopsy for culture and histology can confirm the diagnosis and direct treatment. Assess the need to remove infected bone. For serial follow-up, document area, drainage, tissue and the surrounding skin. Reconsider the plan if there are no signs of improvement in two weeks, and assess a deteriorating wound immediately (<a href="?chapter=46#p688">pp. 688</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p689">689</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p695">695</a>). [WHS pressure ulcer guidelines, 3.7–3.8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11403384/)

Osteomyelitis in **32%** of stage 4 PI; antibiotic course may be **<6 weeks** if infection is limited to cortical bone (<a href="?chapter=46#p695">p695</a>).

## Dressings, healing and comfort

### Match cleansing and dressings to the wound

**Start with the bed, the edges and the old dressing.** A clean granulating wound can be gently irrigated with physiologic saline. Avoid hydrogen peroxide and routine povidone-iodine on clean healing tissue because these agents can injure fibroblasts. Heavy debris or clinical infection calls for reassessment of cleansing, debridement and infection treatment. A hydrocolloid holds moisture and handles low to moderate drainage. A hydrogel hydrates, promotes autolysis, cools and lifts off with little trauma. The amount of drainage helps distinguish the two indications. With scant exudate, avoid overdrying the wound. The named Flaminal Hydro formulation suits slight to moderate drainage. A standard PolyMem polymeric membrane contains both a moisturizer and absorbent material, unlike a simple dry absorbent pad (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">pp. 692</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p694">694</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p697">697</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#book-table-46-2">Table 46-2</a>). [Flaminal formulations](https://www.flenhealth.com/faq/flaminal-faq) · [PolyMem instructions, initial application](https://polymem.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/IFU-5930-PolyMem-Pad-or-Roll-Dressing-NLDEGKPLSLPTHEITARTHKOZHSKFRESEN.pdf)

Hazzard says saline and water should **seldom** be used to cleanse a colonized pressure injury because they have no antiseptic activity. It lists **0.25% sodium hypochlorite** and dilute povidone-iodine (**10% povidone with 1% free iodine**) among the antiseptic solutions (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p692</a>). Low-pressure lavage at **4–15 psi** can lift fibrin and slough. For a **clean wound**, gentler cleansing and physiologic saline are appropriate (<a href="?chapter=46#p693">p693</a>). Avoid cytotoxic antiseptics on clean, healing tissue (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>).

> **Current practice: Wound cleansing.** choose a pH-balanced, nonirritating, nontoxic cleanser for routine care. Tap water from a reliable source is acceptable — [WHS pressure ulcer guideline, 2023 update, recommendations 3.5 and 4.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11403384/).

Avoid on clean/healing PI (fibroblast-toxic in vitro): povidone-iodine, iodophor, sodium hypochlorite, hydrogen peroxide, acetic acid (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>).

**A white, waterlogged edge calls for more absorption.** Wound fluid supports epithelial migration when confined to the bed. When it pools on surrounding skin it produces maceration, including during autolysis. Increasing absorptive capacity can protect the margins while maintaining a moist bed. Alginate ropes or pads fit draining cavities and undermined areas. They handle moderate to heavy exudate and need a secondary cover. They can dry out when drainage is scant. Hydrofibers also absorb moderate to heavy drainage and are available in ribbon form. For a heavily draining infected full-thickness wound, a silver-containing alginate or hydrofiber adds local antimicrobial activity to fluid control (<a href="?chapter=46#p693">pp. 693</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p695">695</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p697">697</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#book-table-46-2">Table 46-2</a>).

Ground metronidazole on the wound bed for odor when debridement isn't possible; antiseptics in full-thickness PI — cadexomer iodine/silver/honey/PHMB dressings reduce bioburden; mupirocin is effective against MRSA (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>).

**The ankle wound after debridement.** Persistent purulence and exposed bone warrant reassessment even if systemic antibiotics have started. Silver alginate addresses the continuing local drainage and microbial burden. Continue the evaluation for bone infection. Negative pressure can reduce wound volume after debridement and selected infected wounds can receive instillation therapy. The official <a href="?chapter=46#p696">p. 696</a> discusses this specialized use. The alginate and silver indications appear on <a href="?chapter=46#p694">pp. 694</a> and <a href="?chapter=46#p697">697</a>.

Moist dressings re-epithelialize up to **40% faster** than wounds left open to air; hydrocolloid/foam changed **q3–5 days**; PI are typically colonized with **≥10⁵** organisms/mL and may carry this load for long periods without clinical signs of infection (<a href="?chapter=46#p693">p693</a>-<a href="?chapter=46#p694">694</a>).

### Feed for healing and anticipate complications

**Calculate what the patient needs, then check what reaches the patient.** Hazzard gives 30–35 kcal/kg and 1.3–1.5 g protein/kg per day for healing. A 60-kg patient losing weight therefore needs about 1,800–2,100 kcal and 78–90 g of protein daily at those targets. June 2022 used a slightly wider protein target of 1.25–1.5 g/kg/day, also given in WHS guideline 2.4. Address restricted diets, poor dentition, appetite, eating assistance and fluid losses. Use oral energy- and protein-rich food or supplements when feasible. Weight change and actual intake are more informative than using serum albumin alone, which also responds to inflammation (<a href="?chapter=46#p685">pp. 685</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p692">692</a>). [WHS pressure ulcer guidelines, 2.4–2.5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11403384/)

Malnutrition/weight loss → **fourfold** higher PI risk; oral nutritional supplements → **25%** relative reduction in incidence (modest evidence) (<a href="?chapter=46#p685">p685</a>).

**What does zinc evidence mean?** Enriched high-energy, high-protein formulas containing arginine, zinc, antioxidants and micronutrients have shown benefit when given for more than four weeks. That does not isolate the effect of zinc. The indication for zinc depends on the nutritional assessment. Hazzard's evidence concerns the combined supplement. Replace micronutrients for a suspected or confirmed deficiency, and avoid substituting isolated vitamins or minerals for calories and protein (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p. 692</a>).

Healing targets: **30–35 kcal/kg** calories, **1.3–1.5 g/kg** protein/day; high-energy, high-protein supplements are beneficial, especially with arginine/zinc/antioxidants/micronutrients, when given **>4 weeks** (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p692</a>).

Megestrol acetate (Megace): never shown effective as an appetite stimulant in older/NH patients; has steroidal side effects (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p692</a>).

**Tube feeding has no guaranteed wound-healing benefit.** A tube can dislodge, aspiration pneumonia can occur, and weight may continue to fall. Death has been reported in the frail populations studied. Tube formulas can also cause diarrhea, increasing wound contamination. Constipation is another recognized gastrointestinal complication of enteral nutrition. Each outcome listed can occur, which makes a single clinical exception untenable (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p. 692</a>). [ESPEN guideline on home enteral nutrition, monitoring and complications](https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614%2819%2930198-0/fulltext)

One retrospective study: air-fluidized beds healed fastest: **5.2 cm²/wk** vs **1.5 cm²/wk** (pressure-reduction) vs **1.8 cm²/wk** (low-air-loss) (<a href="?chapter=46#p692">p692</a>).

### Reduce pain and odor

Keeping the wound covered and moist reduces adherence and trauma during a dressing change. Hydrogel can also cool a painful wound. Excess drainage may instead call for an absorbent nonadherent dressing. Assess pain at rest, with dressing removal and during turns. Severe procedural pain may require analgesia beforehand (<a href="?chapter=46#p680">pp. 680</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p694">694</a>, <a href="?chapter=46#p697">697</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p698">698</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=46#book-table-46-2">Table 46-2</a>**: pain-reducing dressing categories are hydrocolloids (<a href="?chapter=46#p693">p693</a>), plus hydrogels (cooling effect), alginates, and hydrofibers (<a href="?chapter=46#p694">p694</a>) — composite, transparent-film, wound-filler, and foam dressings list no pain-relief use in the table.

Oral MSSA/strep: cephalexin, cefadroxil, dicloxacillin, clindamycin. Oral MRSA: clindamycin, amoxicillin+doxycycline, or TMP-SMX (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>).

Parenteral empiric: cefazolin, ceftriaxone, clindamycin; vancomycin may be required for MRSA; metronidazole/carbapenem/beta-lactamase inhibitor for anaerobes (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>).

EMLA cream (lidocaine **2.5%** + prilocaine **2.5%**) or opioids/NSAIDs **30 min** pre-procedure for severe wound pain (<a href="?chapter=46#p697">p697</a>); EMLA cut venous-wound pain scores by **21 mm** (<a href="?chapter=46#p698">p698</a>).

When a patient is dying, the aim may be freedom from distress even if closure is unattainable. Topical metronidazole on the bed can reduce offensive odor. An absorbent dressing permits fewer changes, and analgesia 30–40 minutes before repositioning can ease movement. Adjust turns to the patient's tolerance. Consider debridement when it relieves a symptom or drains pus, taking account of the burden and the patient's wishes (<a href="?chapter=46#p696">pp. 696</a>–<a href="?chapter=46#p698">698</a>).

Reconsider the treatment plan if no healing signs in **2 weeks** (<a href="?chapter=46#p689">p689</a>).

**75%** of stage 2 PI heal within **60 days**; only **17%** of stage 3/4 PI do (<a href="?chapter=46#p689">p689</a>).

PUSH scores wound area **0–10** (zero for a closed wound plus ten positive-size bands), exudate **0–3**, and tissue type **0–4** (<a href="?chapter=46#p689">p689</a>; NPIAP PUSH Tool 3.0).

BWAT: **13** characteristics, each scored **1–5**, total range **13–65** (<a href="?chapter=46#p689">p689</a>).

Goal = comfort, not healing; treat odor with topical metronidazole; give analgesia **30–40 min** before repositioning (<a href="?chapter=46#p698">p698</a>).

## Key numbers

- Healed scar tissue retains only **40%** of native tissue tensile strength → higher future PI risk at old sites (<a href="?chapter=46#p682">p682</a>).

- **60%** of chronic wounds contain a biofilm (<a href="?chapter=46#p695">p695</a>).

**בחירת מסגרת.** סיעוד מורכב מתאים בשילוב של פגיעה קשה וקבועה בתפקודי היום־יום, אי־יציבות רפואית וצורך בטיפול ובניטור קבועים. פצע לחץ עמוק הוא דוגמה. בשאלת יוני 2022 תואר מטופל עם פרפלגיה ופצע סקרלי שעומקו מוסתר. חשוב לתעד את התלות ואת צורכי הטיפול בפצע. ירידה זמנית לאחר ניתוח עשויה להתאים לשיקום, והנשמה ממושכת מצריכה מסגרת מתאימה להנשמה. ועדת הסיווג בלשכת הבריאות שוקלת את המצב הרפואי והתפקודי. [משרד הבריאות, איך לבחור מוסד אשפוז סיעודי, סעיף התאמת מוסד אשפוז וטבלת רמת התפקוד](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/services-rights/hospitalization/nursing-hospitalization/choosing-a-facility/)
