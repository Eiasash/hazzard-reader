## Cancer screening when benefit takes years

**Start with the outcome that matters to the patient.** A test may find cancer today, yet several years may pass before screening reduces deaths. Estimate prognosis from function, comorbidity and age, then compare that estimate with the time to a benefit large enough to matter. Discuss the whole pathway, including follow-up tests and treatment. Figure 11-2 illustrates the range at one age: among US women aged 80, about one quarter lived five years or less and another quarter lived more than thirteen years. These are population estimates, not individual predictions (pp157–160, Figures 11-1 and 11-2).

**Colorectal screening and prognosis.** Across fecal occult blood screening trials, it took about ten years to avert one colorectal cancer death per thousand people screened. Sigmoidoscopy trials suggested a similar interval. For an asymptomatic 82-year-old with prior normal screening and an estimated five years of life, switching from colonoscopy to FIT leaves the time-to-benefit problem in place. The chapter authors recommend stopping routine screening below a ten-year prognosis. Near that horizon, discuss the patient's preferences and previous screening. An otherwise healthy person who was never screened may have more to gain (pp161–163).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e8ffe5e4732e7d63293662ad" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**What the 9.5-year figure means.** It came from a cost-effectiveness model of biennial mammography. A separate meta-analysis estimated about eleven years to avert one breast cancer death per thousand women screened. The authors consider biennial mammography reasonable for healthy women with more than ten years of life expectancy, even after age 75. None of the mammography trials summarized in the chapter included women older than 75. Among women aged 75 or older screened every two years, the ten-year cumulative probability of a false positive was 14%–27% and nearly doubled with annual testing. A false positive leads to work-up for a lesion that proves benign. Overdiagnosis means that a real cancer was found that would never have troubled the patient during her lifetime (pp160–161).

**What colonoscopy adds.** An average-risk patient can choose annual stool blood testing, sigmoidoscopy every five years or colonoscopy every ten years in the chapter's comparison. Colonoscopy examines the colon and permits polypectomy. A positive FIT requires colonoscopic evaluation, so ask before ordering FIT whether the patient would pursue that evaluation. Bowel preparation can cause nausea or dizziness, and colonoscopy can cause bleeding, perforation and cardiopulmonary complications. The September 2024 question awarded credit to both the mammography and the colonoscopy answers. The text presents colonoscopy as one acceptable option. Selection at ages 76–85 depends particularly on health and prior screening (pp161–163, Table 11-1).

**Why the other two answers fell short.** The cited diabetes chapter describes fasting plasma glucose or A1C testing after age 65, with the interval guided by risk. The geriatric assessment chapter describes a hearing question, bedside whispered voice test and office audiometry as screening approaches. An abnormal screen or reported difficulty can lead to diagnostic audiology (pp119 and 1561).

**A ten-year prognosis has different meanings for different treatments.** Intensive blood pressure control may reduce events in one to three years, although orthostasis and falls can occur soon after treatment starts. After a recent myocardial infarction statin benefit may begin within months, while primary prevention can take several years. Intensive glycemic treatment raises hypoglycemia risk now and may take eight to fifteen years to reduce vascular complications. A treatment aimed at distressing depressive symptoms can offer relief on a shorter time scale. This distinction explains why colorectal screening was the choice in June 2022 Q29 (pp161, 163–165, Table 11-2).

## Vaccination by age, disease and exposure

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-6a3a4989b7a53b19593f44a7" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**The pneumococcal sequence in the 2023 question.** COPD increases the risk of pneumonia and exacerbations. The historical regimen for a previously unvaccinated 80-year-old was one PCV13 dose first, then one PPSV23 dose six to twelve months later. Giving both products every five years, alternating either product, or routinely adding Hib for COPD changes that regimen. Pain and redness at the injection site were common with either pneumococcal product, while serious reactions were rare. The COPD chapter starts its influenza discussion on p1255 and gives its pneumococcal advice on p1256. The current Israeli product and eligibility are described below (pp165 and 1255–1256).

**The exception in the 2022 question.** Influenza, pneumococcal and zoster vaccines all have broad age-based indications. For an older adult, meningococcal vaccination depends on a specific condition or exposure, such as functional or anatomic asplenia, a complement deficiency, or a complement inhibitor. The serogroup-specific product depends on the indication (p165 for age-based vaccination). [CDC, Indications for MenACWY and MenB vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/risk-indications.html)

**Immunity and timing.** Influenza vaccination is seasonal because winter epidemics repeatedly threaten older adults. The high-dose preparation discussed in the book elicited a stronger response than standard dose. The chapter describes Td or Tdap boosters every ten years, including a Tdap dose where prior adult pertussis vaccination is absent. For zoster, the recombinant glycoprotein E provides the antigen and the adjuvant strengthens the innate response. A two-dose recombinant series is indicated even after prior live vaccination. For an immunocompetent adult the usual interval is two to six months. Protecting against persistent postherpetic neuralgia matters as well as preventing the rash (pp165, 1256 and 1728–1729).

## Medication review and preventable harm

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-a542a3a627e208dabd1e6265" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**An alert patient can still be harmed by a sedative.** For the 90-year-old receiving clonazepam three times a day, review the indication, dose, duration, falls and daytime function. Increased sensitivity and slower elimination of long-acting benzodiazepines can contribute to delirium, impaired balance and fractures. Chronic use can produce physical dependence. Where no continuing indication justifies treatment, plan a gradual reduction with monitoring. Regular use alone does not diagnose a substance use disorder (p169 and p319, AGS Beers Criteria 2023, Table 2, pp2059–2060).

**The rest of the medication list still matters.** Quetiapine warrants an indication review, and a normal cognitive examination does not tell us why it was prescribed. Venlafaxine and mirtazapine can contribute to adverse effects and should be reviewed against their individual benefits. If a regularly used opioid such as tramadol is stopped around surgery, anxiety, tremor and agitation raise concern for withdrawal. Review dose timing and exposures before attributing these symptoms to alcohol or benzodiazepines (p169).

## Other cancer screening decisions

- **Cervix.** At age 65, check the actual screening history. The chapter defines adequate prior screening as three consecutive negative cytology results or two negative HPV co-tests in the preceding ten years, with the most recent within five years. A healthy older woman without adequate prior screening may still benefit. Consider whether she could undergo treatment if a lesion is found. False positives can lead to colposcopy and biopsy (p163).
- **Lung.** Low-dose CT reduced lung cancer mortality in trials of heavy smokers. In the National Lung Screening Trial about four lung cancer deaths were averted per thousand people screened over six years. A positive nodule can lead to repeat imaging or invasive sampling. Screening offers little prospect of benefit if comorbidity precludes cancer-directed treatment. Chest radiography did not produce the same mortality benefit (pp163–164).
- **Prostate.** A PSA signal may trigger biopsy, and detection of a slow-growing cancer may lead to treatment with incontinence or sexual dysfunction. The small possible mortality benefit takes over ten years. The chapter authors advise against routine PSA screening of older men (p164).

## Nutrition, activity and social health

Weigh the patient and ask how weight and intake have changed. Use the Mini Nutritional Assessment or Malnutrition Screening Tool to characterize risk. When taste or appetite changes after a medication change, review the timing before adding supplements. Dental problems, depression, food insecurity, lonely meals and an unnecessarily restrictive diet can each reduce intake. A normal appearance or current BMI can conceal meaningful recent loss (pp165–166, Table 11-3).

An older adult with arthritis or fear of falling can start with five to ten minutes of comfortable low-impact activity and increase gradually. Combine strength and endurance with balance and flexibility. Moderate activity on most days is a goal to work toward. Short bouts can confer similar coronary protection to a continuous session when total energy expenditure is comparable (pp166–167 and 807).

Ask whether the patient wants more social contact and what kind. Someone living alone may value privacy, while someone surrounded by people may feel lonely. Hearing, mobility and cognitive difficulties can prevent participation in otherwise accessible groups. Address the identified barrier and connect the person with an activity they want. A positive PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 prompts clinical assessment, treatment when appropriate and follow-up (pp166–168, Table 11-3).

## Tobacco, alcohol, body weight and serious illness

Smoking cessation helps even late in life. Establish current use and readiness, agree on a quit attempt, offer counseling and appropriate pharmacotherapy, and arrange follow-up. Ask about quantity and pattern of alcohol use, injuries and interacting sedatives. The chapter also encourages asking directly about nonprescribed drugs. With obesity, assess the consequences for mobility, sleep and joint pain, while recognizing that unintentional weight loss and loss of lean mass can themselves signal risk in later life (pp168–169).

In a nursing home or with serious illness, ask what a positive test would require of the patient, including transport, an invasive procedure or a treatment they may decline. Prevention can still address immediate priorities such as nutrition, manageable activity and vaccination. Review preventive medications whose burdens have become more immediate than their expected benefit, and involve the patient or surrogate in choosing goals (pp169–170).

## Summary

**Decision framework**
- Prevention acts before symptoms but can impose immediate burdens. Individualize using values, overall health, life expectancy, harms and time-to-benefit (p157).
- Many prevention trials exclude adults over **75**; extrapolation to older or frail patients therefore carries uncertainty (p157).
- Consider cancer screening primarily when life expectancy exceeds **10 years**, after weighing the particular test's benefits and harms (p157).

**Cancer screening**
- Mammography trial meta-analysis: approximately **11 years** to prevent **1** breast-cancer death per **1000** women screened (p160).
- Fecal occult blood screening takes approximately **10 years** to prevent **1** colorectal-cancer death per **1000** screened. Serious harm comes chiefly from downstream procedures, including bleeding or perforation during colonoscopy (p161).
- Flexible sigmoidoscopy has a similar estimated time to benefit (p161). In the later COLONPREV trial of adults aged 50–69, invitation to biennial FIT was noninferior to invitation to one-time colonoscopy for colorectal cancer mortality at ten years. That comparison does not give a time to benefit versus no screening. [COLONPREV, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40158525/).
- Prior cervical screening is central to decisions in older women. Direct time-to-benefit data are absent for those at least **65** (p163).
- Low-dose chest CT trials show lung-cancer mortality reduction after **6 years**; chest x-ray screening did not provide such benefit (p163).

**Management & prevention**
- Treating asymptomatic hypertension, diabetes or dyslipidemia is also preventive: immediate treatment burdens must be balanced against delayed benefits (p157, p164).
- Intensive glycemic treatment immediately increases serious hypoglycemia risk; antihypertensive initiation appears to increase hip-fracture risk in the first **45 days** (p164).
- Immunization has a more favorable near-term risk–benefit balance than many screening interventions; the chapter emphasizes influenza, pneumococcal and zoster vaccination (p165).
- Support healthy behaviors and smoking cessation; maintaining behavior change requires the patient's participation (p165, p168).

**Exam traps**
- Detecting more disease is not the same as producing net benefit during the patient's lifetime; timing of harms and benefits matters (p157, p164).
- For nursing-home residents or serious illness, prioritize near-term harms, competing needs and time-to-benefit rather than simply continuing routine preventive care (p169).
