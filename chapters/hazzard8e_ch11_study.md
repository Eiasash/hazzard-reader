# Prevention and Screening

Start with baseline risk and the time until a preventive intervention can help. Compare that interval with life expectancy, competing illness and the person's goals.

## Individualizing prevention

Prevention acts before symptoms but can impose immediate burdens. Individualize using values, overall health, life expectancy, harms and time-to-benefit (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">p157</a>).

Many prevention trials exclude adults over **75**; extrapolation to older or frail patients therefore carries uncertainty (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">p157</a>).

Consider cancer screening primarily when life expectancy exceeds **10 years**, after weighing the particular test's benefits and harms (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">p157</a>).

Treating asymptomatic hypertension, diabetes or dyslipidemia is also preventive: immediate treatment burdens must be balanced against delayed benefits (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">p157</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#p164">p164</a>).

**Pitfall.** Detecting more disease is not the same as producing net benefit during the patient's lifetime; timing of harms and benefits matters (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">p157</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#p164">p164</a>).

## Cancer screening

### Cancer screening when benefit takes years

**Start with the outcome that matters to the patient.** A test may find cancer today, yet several years may pass before screening reduces deaths. Estimate prognosis from function, comorbidity and age, then compare that estimate with the time to a benefit large enough to matter. Discuss the whole pathway, including follow-up tests and treatment. <a href="?chapter=11#book-figure-11-2">Figure 11-2</a> illustrates the range at one age: among US women aged 80, about one quarter lived five years or less and another quarter lived more than thirteen years. These are population estimates, not individual predictions (<a href="?chapter=11#p157">pp157</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p160">160</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#book-figure-11-1">Figures 11-1</a> and <a href="?chapter=11#book-figure-11-2">11-2</a>).

**Colorectal screening and prognosis.** Across fecal occult blood screening trials, it took about ten years to avert one colorectal cancer death per thousand people screened. Sigmoidoscopy trials suggested a similar interval. For an asymptomatic 82-year-old with prior normal screening and an estimated five years of life, switching from colonoscopy to FIT leaves the time-to-benefit problem in place. The chapter authors recommend stopping routine screening below a ten-year prognosis. Near that horizon, discuss the patient's preferences and previous screening. An otherwise healthy person who was never screened may have more to gain (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">pp161</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p163">163</a>).

Fecal occult blood screening takes approximately **10 years** to prevent **1** colorectal-cancer death per **1000** screened. Serious harm comes chiefly from downstream procedures, including bleeding or perforation during colonoscopy (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">p161</a>).

Flexible sigmoidoscopy has a similar estimated time to benefit (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">p161</a>).

> **Current practice: FIT versus colonoscopy.** In the later COLONPREV trial of adults aged 50–69, invitation to biennial FIT was noninferior to invitation to one-time colonoscopy for colorectal cancer mortality at ten years. That comparison does not give a time to benefit versus no screening. [COLONPREV, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40158525/).

**What the 9.5-year figure means.** The model found biennial mammography cost-effective with life expectancy of at least **9.5 years** (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">p161</a>). A separate meta-analysis estimated about eleven years to avert one breast cancer death per thousand women screened. The authors consider biennial mammography reasonable for healthy women with more than ten years of life expectancy, even after age 75. None of the mammography trials summarized in the chapter included women older than 75. Among women aged 75 or older screened every two years, the ten-year cumulative probability of a false positive was 14%–27% and nearly doubled with annual testing. A false positive leads to work-up for a lesion that proves benign. Overdiagnosis means that a real cancer was found that would never have troubled the patient during her lifetime (<a href="?chapter=11#p160">pp160</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p161">161</a>).

**What colonoscopy adds.** An average-risk patient can choose annual stool blood testing, sigmoidoscopy every five years or colonoscopy every ten years in the chapter's comparison. Colonoscopy examines the colon and permits polypectomy. A positive FIT requires colonoscopic evaluation, so ask before ordering FIT whether the patient would pursue that evaluation. Bowel preparation can cause nausea or dizziness, and colonoscopy can cause bleeding, perforation and cardiopulmonary complications. The September 2024 question awarded credit to both the mammography and the colonoscopy answers. The text presents colonoscopy as one acceptable option. Selection at ages 76–85 depends particularly on health and prior screening (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">pp161</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p163">163</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#book-table-11-1">Table 11-1</a>).

**Why the other two answers fell short.** The diabetes chapter describes fasting plasma glucose or A1C testing after age 65, with the interval guided by risk. The geriatric assessment chapter describes a hearing question, bedside whispered voice test and office audiometry as screening approaches. An abnormal screen or reported difficulty can lead to diagnostic audiology (<a href="?chapter=8#p119">pp119</a> and <a href="?chapter=99#p1561">1561</a>).

**A ten-year prognosis has different meanings for different treatments.** <a href="?chapter=11#book-table-11-2">Table 11-2</a> gives **1–5 years** for hypertension treatment and **3 months–2.5 years** for statins, from first detectable to near-maximal population benefit. The accompanying prose gives a shorter estimate of **under 3 months** for statins after a recent myocardial infarction, and several years for primary prevention. Orthostasis and falls can occur soon after starting blood pressure treatment. Intensive glycemic treatment raises hypoglycemia risk now and may take eight to fifteen years to reduce vascular complications. A treatment aimed at distressing depressive symptoms can offer relief on a shorter time scale. Life expectancy matters especially in colorectal screening (<a href="?chapter=11#p161">pp161</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#p163">163</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p165">165</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#book-table-11-2">Table 11-2</a>).

### Other cancer screening decisions

**Cervix.** At age 65, check the actual screening history. The chapter defines adequate prior screening as three consecutive negative cytology results or two consecutive negative HPV co-tests in the preceding ten years, with the most recent within five years. A healthy older woman without adequate prior screening may still benefit. Consider whether she could undergo treatment if a lesion is found. False positives can lead to colposcopy and biopsy (<a href="?chapter=11#p163">p163</a>).

Prior cervical screening is central to decisions in older women. Direct time-to-benefit data are absent for those at least **65** (<a href="?chapter=11#p163">p163</a>).

**Lung.** Low-dose CT reduced lung cancer mortality in trials of heavy smokers. In the National Lung Screening Trial about four lung cancer deaths were averted per thousand people screened over six years. A positive nodule can lead to repeat imaging or invasive sampling. Screening offers little prospect of benefit if comorbidity precludes cancer-directed treatment. Chest radiography did not produce the same mortality benefit (<a href="?chapter=11#p163">pp163</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p164">164</a>).

> **Current practice: Eligibility for lung cancer screening.** Annual low-dose CT screening is recommended by the USPSTF for eligible adults aged 50–80 with at least 20 pack-years who smoke or quit within 15 years. The American Cancer Society’s 2023 guideline uses the same age and exposure thresholds and drops the 15-year quit limit. Screening presumes sufficient health and willingness to undergo treatment. [USPSTF, 2021 and ACS, 2023](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening).

**Prostate.** A PSA signal may trigger biopsy, and detection of a slow-growing cancer may lead to treatment with incontinence or sexual dysfunction. The small possible mortality benefit takes over ten years. The chapter authors advise against routine PSA screening of older men (<a href="?chapter=11#p164">p164</a>).

## Medicines and immunization

### Vaccination by age, disease and exposure

**The pneumococcal sequence in the 2023 question.** COPD increases the risk of pneumonia and exacerbations. The historical regimen for a previously unvaccinated 80-year-old was one PCV13 dose first, then one PPSV23 dose six to twelve months later. Giving both products every five years, alternating either product, or routinely adding Hib for COPD changes that regimen. Pain and redness at the injection site were common with either pneumococcal product, while serious reactions were rare. The COPD chapter starts its influenza discussion on <a href="?chapter=81#p1255">p1255</a> and gives its pneumococcal advice on <a href="?chapter=81#p1256">p1256</a>.

**The exception in the 2022 question.** Influenza, pneumococcal and zoster vaccines all have broad age-based indications. For an older adult, meningococcal vaccination depends on a specific condition or exposure, such as functional or anatomic asplenia, a complement deficiency, or a complement inhibitor. The serogroup-specific product depends on the indication (<a href="?chapter=11#p165">p165</a> for age-based vaccination). [CDC, Indications for MenACWY and MenB vaccination](https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/risk-indications.html)

Immunization has a more favorable near-term risk–benefit balance than many screening interventions; the chapter emphasizes influenza, pneumococcal and zoster vaccination (<a href="?chapter=11#p165">p165</a>).

**Immunity and timing.** Influenza vaccination is seasonal because winter epidemics repeatedly threaten older adults. The high-dose preparation discussed in the book elicited a stronger response than standard dose. The chapter describes Td or Tdap boosters every ten years, including a Tdap dose where prior adult pertussis vaccination is absent. For zoster, the recombinant glycoprotein E provides the antigen and the adjuvant strengthens the innate response. A two-dose recombinant series is indicated even after prior live vaccination. For an immunocompetent adult the usual interval is two to six months. Protecting against persistent postherpetic neuralgia matters as well as preventing the rash (<a href="?chapter=11#p165">pp165</a>, <a href="?chapter=81#p1256">1256</a> and <a href="?chapter=107#p1728">1728</a>–<a href="?chapter=107#p1729">1729</a>).

> **Current practice: US adult vaccines.** For PCV-naïve adults or those with unknown history, CDC recommends PCV15, PCV20 or PCV21 from age 50. Follow PCV15 with PPSV23 according to prior vaccination and risk. RSV vaccination is one dose at age 75 or older, or ages 50–74 at increased risk of severe disease. At age 65 or older, preferentially use a high-dose inactivated, recombinant or adjuvanted inactivated influenza vaccine. If none is available, give another age-appropriate product. [CDC pneumococcal](https://www.cdc.gov/pneumococcal/hcp/vaccine-recommendations/index.html) · [RSV, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/rsv/hcp/vaccine-clinical-guidance/adults.html) · [influenza, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/hcp/acip/index.html).

> Current practice (not the book): In Israel, the Ministry of Health recommends COVID-19 vaccination for people aged 65 and older. The recommendation applies regardless of previous infection or vaccination. [Ministry of Health immunization guide, June 2026, §§1.1 and 3.1](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/policy/v-corona/he/files_publications_units_epidemiology_vaccine_guidelines_365_Corona.pdf).

### Medication review and preventable harm

**An alert patient can still be harmed by a sedative.** For the 90-year-old receiving clonazepam three times a day, review the indication, dose, duration, falls and daytime function. Increased sensitivity and slower elimination of long-acting benzodiazepines can contribute to delirium, impaired balance and fractures. Chronic use can produce physical dependence. Where no continuing indication justifies treatment, plan a gradual reduction with monitoring. Regular use alone does not diagnose a substance use disorder (<a href="?chapter=11#p169">p169</a> and <a href="?chapter=23#p319">p319</a>, AGS Beers Criteria 2023, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">Table 2</a>, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">pp2059</a>–<a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">2060</a>).

**The rest of the medication list still matters.** Quetiapine warrants an indication review, and a normal cognitive examination does not tell us why it was prescribed. Venlafaxine and mirtazapine can contribute to adverse effects and should be reviewed against their individual benefits. If a regularly used opioid such as tramadol is stopped around surgery, anxiety, tremor and agitation raise concern for withdrawal. Review dose timing and exposures before attributing these symptoms to alcohol or benzodiazepines (<a href="?chapter=11#p169">p169</a>).

## Activity, nutrition and social health

Weigh the patient and ask how weight and intake have changed. Use the Mini Nutritional Assessment or Malnutrition Screening Tool to characterize risk. When taste or appetite changes after a medication change, review the timing before adding supplements. Dental problems, depression, food insecurity, lonely meals and an unnecessarily restrictive diet can each reduce intake. A normal appearance or current BMI can conceal meaningful recent loss (<a href="?chapter=11#p165">pp165</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p166">166</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#book-table-11-3">Table 11-3</a>).

An older adult with arthritis or fear of falling can start with five to ten minutes of comfortable low-impact activity and increase gradually. Combine strength and endurance with balance and flexibility. Moderate activity on most days is a goal to work toward. Short bouts can confer similar coronary protection to a continuous session when total energy expenditure is comparable (<a href="?chapter=11#p166">pp166</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p167">167</a> and <a href="?chapter=54#p807">807</a>).

Ask whether the patient wants more social contact and what kind. Someone living alone may value privacy, while someone surrounded by people may feel lonely. Hearing, mobility and cognitive difficulties can prevent participation in otherwise accessible groups. Address the identified barrier and connect the person with an activity they want. A positive PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 prompts clinical assessment, treatment when appropriate and follow-up (<a href="?chapter=11#p166">pp166</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p168">168</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#book-table-11-3">Table 11-3</a>).

Support healthy behaviors and smoking cessation; maintaining behavior change requires the patient's participation (<a href="?chapter=11#p165">p165</a>, <a href="?chapter=11#p168">p168</a>).

## Tobacco, alcohol and serious illness

Smoking cessation helps even late in life. Establish current use and readiness, agree on a quit attempt, offer counseling and appropriate pharmacotherapy, and arrange follow-up. Ask about quantity and pattern of alcohol use, injuries and interacting sedatives. The chapter also encourages asking directly about nonprescribed drugs. With obesity, assess the consequences for mobility, sleep and joint pain, while recognizing that unintentional weight loss and loss of lean mass can themselves signal risk in later life (<a href="?chapter=11#p168">pp168</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p169">169</a>).

In a nursing home or with serious illness, ask what a positive test would require of the patient, including transport, an invasive procedure or a treatment they may decline. Prevention can still address immediate priorities such as nutrition, manageable activity and vaccination. Review preventive medications whose burdens have become more immediate than their expected benefit, and involve the patient or surrogate in choosing goals (<a href="?chapter=11#p169">pp169</a>–<a href="?chapter=11#p170">170</a>).

Intensive glycemic treatment immediately increases serious hypoglycemia risk; antihypertensive initiation appears to increase hip-fracture risk in the first **45 days** (<a href="?chapter=11#p164">p164</a>).

**Pitfall.** For nursing-home residents or serious illness, prioritize near-term harms, competing needs and time-to-benefit rather than simply continuing routine preventive care (<a href="?chapter=11#p169">p169</a>).

## Key numbers

- Mammography trial meta-analysis: approximately **11 years** to prevent **1** breast-cancer death per **1000** women screened (<a href="?chapter=11#p160">p160</a>).

- Low-dose chest CT trials show lung-cancer mortality reduction after **6 years**; chest x-ray screening did not provide such benefit (<a href="?chapter=11#p163">p163</a>).

## Israeli context

**בדיקות סקר בקהילה.** בבדיקה לאיתור סרטן המעי הגס באוכלוסייה בסיכון רגיל, משרד הבריאות מתאר בדיקת דם סמוי בצואה אחת לשנה מגיל 50 ועד 75, במסגרת הסל דרך קופות החולים. תוצאה חיובית מובילה להפניה לקולונוסקופיה. סיפור משפחתי מצריך התאמה פרטנית של המעקב. [משרד הבריאות, בדיקות תקופתיות בגיל השלישי, סעיף דם סמוי בצואה](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/services-rights/benefits-and-eligibilities/checkups/) · [סרטן בגיל המבוגר, סעיף בדיקות תקופתיות](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/keep-me-healthy/common-conditions/cancer/)

**ממוגרפיה ושמיעה.** סל הבריאות כולל ממוגרפיה אחת לשנתיים לנשים בגילים 50–74, דרך קופת החולים. משרד הבריאות מציין שהחיוב תלוי בזכאות לביקור במכון; מכבי מפרסמת בדיקת סקר ללא השתתפות עצמית במסלולים מסוימים. [מכבי, זכאות לממוגרפיית סקר](https://www.maccabi4u.co.il/eligibilites/1806/?id=1401). מישוש גוש חדש מצריך בירור רפואי מיידי. בבירור ירידה בשמיעה מתוארת תחילה בדיקה אצל רופא המשפחה. לפי הממצאים אפשר להפנות לאף־אוזן־גרון ובהמשך למכון שמיעה לבדיקה אצל קלינאי תקשורת. בדיקות שמיעה אבחנתיות כלולות בסל. [משרד הבריאות, בדיקות תקופתיות בגיל השלישי, סעיפי ממוגרפיה ובדיקת שמיעה, 23.09.2026](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/services-rights/benefits-and-eligibilities/checkups/) · [סרטן בגיל המבוגר, סעיף בדיקות תקופתיות](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/keep-me-healthy/common-conditions/cancer/)

**פנאומוקוק.** מפברואר 2025 משתמשים בישראל ב־PCV20 לחיסון מבוגרים. הוא מומלץ מגיל 65 ומגיל 18 בקבוצות סיכון, ובהן מחלת ריאה כרונית. לרוב מספיקה מנה אחת. לאחר PCV13 או PPV23 נותנים אותה לפחות שנה אחרי המנה האחרונה. אחרי השתלת מח עצם יש לוח נפרד. [משרד הבריאות, תדריך נובמבר 2024, סעיפים 6–7](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/policy/pneumo/he/files_publications_units_epidemiology_vaccine_guidelines_Pneumo-Adult-120723.pdf#page=3) · [המעבר ל־PCV20, ינואר 2025](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/policy/pneumo/he/files_publications_units_epidemiology_vaccine_guidelines_epi-08700425.pdf).

**שלבקת חוגרת ושפעת.** שינגריקס ניתן בשתי מנות. משרד הבריאות ממליץ עליו מעל גיל 50 ומציין זכאות בסל מגיל 65 וכן מגיל 18 למדוכאי חיסון בקבוצת סיכון, בהשתתפות עצמית. לקבלת החיסון נדרשים מרשם והפניה מרופא המשפחה. חיסון נגד שפעת מומלץ בכל עונה.
