# Chapter 11 Study: Prevention and Screening

## Summary

**Decision framework**
- Prevention acts before symptoms but can impose immediate burdens. Individualize using values, overall health, life expectancy, harms and time-to-benefit (p157).
- Many prevention trials exclude adults over **75**; extrapolation to older or frail patients therefore carries uncertainty (p157).
- Consider cancer screening primarily when life expectancy exceeds **10 years**, after weighing the particular test's benefits and harms (p157).

**Cancer screening**
- Mammography trial meta-analysis: approximately **11 years** to prevent **1** breast-cancer death per **1000** women screened (p160).
- Fecal occult blood screening: approximately **10 years** to prevent **1** colorectal-cancer death per **1000** people; serious complications are also approximately **1 in 1000** (p161).
- Flexible sigmoidoscopy has a similar estimated time-to-benefit; the chapter notes that mortality timing for colonoscopy awaits ongoing trials (p161).
- Prior cervical screening is central to decisions in older women. Direct time-to-benefit data are absent for those at least **65** (p163).
- Low-dose chest CT trials show lung-cancer mortality reduction after **6 years**; chest x-ray screening did not provide such benefit (p163).

**Management & prevention**
- Treating asymptomatic hypertension, diabetes or dyslipidemia is also preventive: immediate treatment burdens must be balanced against delayed benefits (p157, p164).
- Intensive glycemic treatment immediately increases serious hypoglycemia risk; antihypertensive initiation appears to increase hip-fracture risk in the first **45 days** (p164).
- Immunization has a more favorable near-term risk–benefit balance than many screening interventions; the chapter emphasizes influenza, pneumococcal and zoster vaccination (p165).
- Support healthy behaviors and smoking cessation; maintaining behavior change requires the patient's participation (p165, p168).

**Exam traps**
- Detecting more disease is not the same as producing net benefit during the patient's lifetime; timing of harms and benefits matters (p157, p164).
- For nursing-home residents or serious illness, prioritize near-term harms, competing needs and time-to-benefit rather than simply continuing routine preventive care (p169).
