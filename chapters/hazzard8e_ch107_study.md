# Chapter 107 Study: Other Viruses: Human Immunodeficiency Virus Infection and Herpes Zoster

## Summary

**HIV in older adults**
- More than **50%** of people living with HIV in the United States are at least **50**; treatment has transformed HIV into a chronic disease of aging (p1719).
- Consider HIV with unexplained anemia, fatigue, weight loss or pneumonia; nonspecific symptoms and assumptions about age can delay diagnosis (p1719–1720).
- Link newly diagnosed patients rapidly to HIV care. ART is recommended regardless of CD4 count, with greatest long-term benefit when counts remain above **500 cells/μL** (p1720).
- Viral suppression reduces transmission, but does not generally eradicate HIV. Multimorbidity, polypharmacy and functional decline remain concerns even with controlled infection (p1719).

**Zoster: diagnosis**
- Reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus causes unilateral dermatomal pain and rash; postherpetic neuralgia is pain at least **90 days** after rash onset (p1725). Zoster risk increases with age and immunocompromise, including HIV, hematologic malignancy, transplant, **systemic lupus erythematosus** and immunosuppressive drugs; the chapter also lists female sex, White race, psychological stress and physical trauma (pp1725–1726).
- Pain, burning or hyperesthesia can precede rash by days and mimic visceral or neurologic disease. Zoster sine herpete can cause dermatomal neuralgia without rash (p1726).
- Typical dermatomal vesicles with pain support the clinical diagnosis; recurrent localized lesions also raise herpes simplex in the differential (p1727).

**Antiviral treatment**
- Acyclovir **800 mg five times daily for 7 days**, famciclovir **500 mg every 8 hours for 7 days**, or valacyclovir **1 g three times daily for 7 days** reduced pain in trials treating within **72 hours** (p1727).
- Common adverse effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and headache. Famciclovir and valacyclovir allow less frequent dosing than acyclovir (p1727).
- The chapter makes exceptions to the **72-hour** treatment threshold for ophthalmic zoster or continuing new vesicle formation; topical antivirals are ineffective (p1727).

**Pain, prevention & exam traps**
- Keep lesions clean and dry; treatment aims to shorten the acute attack and relieve pain (p1727).
- Corticosteroids do not prevent postherpetic neuralgia and are not for routine use; selected patients may obtain acute pain benefit, but steroids must accompany antivirals (p1728).
- Recombinant zoster vaccine combines viral glycoprotein antigen with an adjuvant and helps prevent zoster and postherpetic neuralgia (p1728).
- Do not equate absence of rash with absence of zoster, or viral suppression with cure of HIV (p1719, p1726).


### Earlier recognition of HIV and ongoing geriatric care

- Take a sexual and exposure history without assuming age or partnership excludes HIV. Recurrent pneumonia, oral candidiasis, zoster, peripheral neuropathy or unexplained cytopenias can justify diagnostic testing. Ask explicitly about previous testing, since patients may assume routine blood work included it (p1720).
- Link a newly diagnosed patient promptly to treatment. Older adults can achieve effective viral suppression but may recover CD4 cells more slowly. Consider kidney and other organ function, drug interactions, cost and patient preference when choosing therapy. Continued suppression reduces incident non-AIDS conditions as well as opportunistic disease (p1720–1721).
- Distinguish a complication that occurs earlier from one that is simply more prevalent. Falls, frailty, bone health and cardiovascular risk warrant attention during ordinary follow-up even when the viral load remains suppressed (p1721–1722).

### A painful facial eruption or visual change

- Age and impaired cellular immunity increase zoster incidence. In North American and European population studies the estimated incidence after age **65** was about **10–14 per 1,000 per year**. SLE, leukemia, lymphoma, transplant and immunosuppressive treatment are among the conditions named. The pain can precede the rash and misdirect the initial evaluation (p1725–1726).
- Eye pain or visual blurring with a facial zoster rash needs systemic antiviral treatment and ophthalmology assessment. The **72-hour** trial window does not preclude treatment for ophthalmic disease or continuing new vesicles. Corneal and uveal injury can threaten sight. Topical ocular anti-inflammatory treatment belongs under ophthalmologic supervision (p1726–1728).
- Table 107-1 gives its oral regimens for immunocompetent older adults and cranial nerve involvement. Adjust acyclovir for renal function. For mild immune compromise its treatment duration is **7–10 days**. Severe compromise changes the regimen and requires assessment for parenteral therapy (p1727–1728).
- In the United States the book describes recombinant zoster vaccine for immunocompetent adults **50 or older** and reports that at the time ACIP had no recommendation for immunocompromised people (p1729–1730).

> Current practice (not the book): ACIP recommends two doses for adults aged 19 or older who are or will be immunocompromised. The usual interval is 2–6 months. It can be shortened to 1–2 months when completing the series earlier would be beneficial. - [CDC clinical considerations, 2024](https://www.cdc.gov/shingles/hcp/vaccine-considerations/immunocompromised-adults.html).

### Two reasons an older adult may first come to HIV care

Distinguish an individual newly acquiring HIV in later life from a person infected years earlier whose diagnosis has been delayed. The distinction matters when interpreting an apparently recent decline in strength, weight or cognition. In the US data cited, more than **15%** of new diagnoses were in people aged 50 or older. A new diagnosis is a date of recognition, not a reliable date of acquisition. At the same time, effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) has allowed many people treated earlier in life to reach older age. By 2018, about **20%** of people living with HIV in the United States and Europe were aged 65 or older (p1719).

Discuss partners, barrier protection and prior testing in the same matter-of-fact way as at younger ages. Patients and clinicians may both underestimate risk. A person may have a new relationship after loss of a partner, or may see no reason to use condoms when pregnancy is no longer a concern. Ask what previous blood tests actually included. For an HIV-negative person at risk, the chapter considers barrier protection and pre-exposure prophylaxis. Few older adults participated in the PrEP studies it discusses, so kidney disease, other medicines and treatment burden deserve attention when counseling an older individual (p1719–1720).

Screening an asymptomatic person is a different decision from investigating an unexplained illness. Hazzard reports the US screening ranges used by the CDC and USPSTF as **13–64** and **15–65 years**, respectively, with testing at other ages when risk warrants it. An older person with unexplained weight loss, recurrent pneumonia, candidiasis, cytopenias or neuropathy raises a diagnostic question regardless of exemption from an age-based screening offer. The value of making the diagnosis includes linkage to treatment and preventing both HIV-related illness and onward sexual transmission through sustained suppression (p1719–1720).

### Diagnosing HIV is the beginning of a care pathway

Connect a positive result to an actual appointment and a plan for starting therapy and staying in care. In untreated disease, progressive loss of CD4 cells increases vulnerability to opportunistic infection and certain cancers. The chapter gives **approximately 8–12 years** from infection to symptomatic AIDS in the usual untreated course. This historical interval does not apply to an individual on effective therapy. Once treatment suppresses HIV RNA, the patient can remain clinically well for many years; stopping therapy because a laboratory report is undetectable removes the treatment maintaining that state (p1719–1721).

Separate the two laboratory responses after treatment starts. HIV RNA measures circulating viral burden. The CD4 count indicates immune status; recovery may lag in older patients despite effective viral suppression. Early treatment protects the opportunity for immune recovery. The chapter stresses retaining people in care after a good early response, checking tolerability and safety and continuing to assess for organ disease. In the SMART study, consistently suppressive treatment also reduced incident non-AIDS conditions, including cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal and liver disease (p1720–1721).

The regimens described usually combine an integrase inhibitor with one or two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors. Choosing among regimens calls for HIV expertise, previous treatment and resistance information, organ function, likely interactions, affordability and the patient's preferences. The chapter gives classes and selection principles rather than a universal dose for ART or PrEP. A simplified regimen may improve acceptability while still requiring medication reconciliation. New weight gain may be welcome in someone who was underweight, but excess gain has been associated with hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes and fatty liver. Review nutrition and opportunities for activity at treatment initiation (p1720–1721).

### Applying the geriatric assessment to treated HIV

Figure 107-1 connects four influences on late-life health: aging biology, HIV and treatment history, health behaviors and structural inequalities. Persistent immune changes or past treatment toxicity can coexist with smoking, inactivity or limited access to housing and care. Several pathways can lead to the same outcome, such as a fall or a myocardial infarct. A suppressed viral load makes a new problem worth evaluating on its own merits. It neither establishes HIV as the cause nor removes the influence of previous disease, cumulative drug exposures or the patient's environment (p1721–1724).

Work through the six domains in the figure. For **Mind**, ask about memory, executive difficulties, mood, loneliness, stigma and substance use. A cognitive change can reflect more than one process, including an age-associated neurodegenerative disorder. For **Multimorbidity**, consider which conditions are occurring earlier or more often than in people without HIV and how their treatments interact. Record the onset and functional effect of the problem rather than inferring “accelerated aging” simply because a diagnosis is present (p1721–1724).

For **Medications**, put every prescription, nonprescription product and recent dose change on the same list as ART. Ask about missed doses and affordability. A new interaction, an increasing pill burden and existing kidney or liver injury may threaten the benefit of an otherwise successful regimen. A pharmacist and an HIV interaction resource can assist with both a new ART regimen and a new non-HIV medicine. For **Mobility**, observe chair rises, balance and walking, and ask about falls directly. The chapter reports fall rates in people with HIV aged **45–65** similar to those in comparison populations aged **65 or older** without HIV (p1722–1724).

**What matters most** determines the expected value of another screening procedure or long-term treatment. A robust older adult and a younger person with substantial frailty can have quite different time horizons for benefit. **Modifiable** draws attention to remaining opportunities, including exercise, strength training, nutrition, smoking and access to support. Include the patient's own goals and social situation in that conversation. Completing a disease-specific checklist may leave untreated the main obstacle to daily life, such as poor balance or inability to obtain prescriptions between appointments (p1722–1724).

### Who develops zoster and why the site of pain matters

Zoster is reactivation of VZV remaining in sensory ganglia after earlier varicella. A susceptible contact exposed to a patient with infectious lesions can develop chickenpox. The contact does not acquire the index patient's dermatomal shingles syndrome. Figure 107-2 plots incidence against age across several countries. The curves differ in absolute height but show increasing incidence, particularly from approximately **50–60 years** onward. In the population studies summarized, lifetime risk was estimated at about **30%**, potentially reaching **50%** among people surviving to 85 or older. These are population estimates, not annual probabilities (p1725).

June 2026 Q22 links the appearance of the rash to susceptibility to reactivation. The chapter names systemic lupus erythematosus, HIV, hematologic malignancies, transplantation and immunosuppressive treatment. Age and impaired cellular immunity help explain the clustering. It also lists associations with female sex and White race in the populations studied. The answer in that item is lupus. A demographic association in a study cannot exclude zoster in an individual outside that demographic group; the distribution, evolution and associated pain guide recognition in the patient in front of you (p1725–1726).

Before the eruption, localized dysesthesia may be the only clue. Chest pain can be mistaken for cardiac or pleural disease, and unilateral abdominal or flank pain for biliary or renal disease. Inspect and compare sensation over the symptomatic dermatome while still evaluating other time-sensitive causes of pain. In Figure 107-3, lesions at different stages illustrate why a patient may describe a changing eruption. Macules or papules generally become vesicles within **24 hours**, then pustules and crusts over **7–10 days**. Healing usually takes **2–4 weeks**. Some older adults have a small patch or never develop typical vesicles (p1726).

Ask the patient to distinguish pain at rest from pain provoked by touch. A breeze or clothing causing pain is allodynia. A mildly painful bump causing an exaggerated or prolonged response is hyperpathia. These distinctions explain why sleep, bathing, dressing or travel can become difficult even after the visible eruption improves. Pain persisting at least **90 days from rash onset** meets the chapter's definition of postherpetic neuralgia. A person whose pain becomes severe earlier still needs treatment; the definition is an outcome boundary, not a waiting period for analgesia (p1725–1726).

### Recognizing disease outside the skin

Eye pain or blurring with forehead or facial zoster is a threat to vision. Keratitis and intraocular inflammation can require treatment beyond systemic antiviral therapy. Facial paresis with ipsilateral lesions of the ear canal, external ear or palate, sometimes with tinnitus, vertigo or hearing loss, suggests Ramsay Hunt syndrome. Pharyngeal or laryngeal involvement can reflect other cranial neuropathies. Weakness in muscles anatomically related to the affected dermatome can accompany motor involvement. These findings change the urgency and scope of assessment even if the surface area of the rash is small (p1726–1728).

A characteristic unilateral painful vesicular eruption usually gives the diagnosis clinically. Repeated crops in the same area also raise the possibility of herpes simplex. PCR testing of a lesion can settle uncertainty when the eruption is atypical, the patient is immunocompromised, or the consequences of a wrong diagnosis are substantial. Suspected visceral or neurologic disease requires evaluation directed at the organ involved. Pain without a rash remains diagnostically more difficult because the most readily recognized physical sign and usual lesion specimen are absent (p1725–1727).

### Antiviral treatment and the facial-zoster exam vignette

June 2023 Q12 describes an older adult who arrives on day four with eye pain and visual blurring; no new vesicles appeared since yesterday. Most antiviral trials enrolled patients within **72 hours**. The authors explicitly make an exception for ophthalmic zoster. The answer is systemic oral acyclovir in addition to eye care. Normal renal function in that vignette matters for dosing. The fact that no new vesicles appeared overnight does not cancel the independent ocular indication. Cutaneous acyclovir ointment and watchful waiting fail to address it; the famciclovir distractor uses the wrong frequency (p1727–1728).

Read across the immune-status row before choosing a dose from Table 107-1. For immunocompetent patients aged 50 or older, or at any age with cranial nerve involvement, the courses are acyclovir **800 mg orally five times daily**, valacyclovir **1 g orally every 8 hours** or famciclovir **500 mg orally every 8 hours**, each for **7 days**. For mild immune compromise, the table gives the same oral doses for **7–10 days**. Acyclovir requires renal adjustment. Check the ability to take frequent oral doses as well as kidney function and adverse effects when selecting a regimen (p1727).

For severe immune compromise the table lists acyclovir **10 mg/kg every 8 hours for 7–10 days**, with renal adjustment. The weight-based parenteral regimen is distinct from the fixed oral doses. For acyclovir-resistant infection it lists IV foscarnet **40 mg/kg every 8 hours** until healed. In an older adult with rapidly progressive, extensive or visceral illness, assess severity and involve an experienced clinician. The oral dose from an uncomplicated shingles vignette is not interchangeable with a parenteral treatment plan (p1727).

> Current practice (not the book): In people with HIV who have extensive skin lesions or visceral disease, NIH guidance starts IV acyclovir and allows an oral switch after clinical improvement to complete 10–14 days in total. For resistant VZV, IV foscarnet is nephrotoxic and warrants specialist involvement. - [NIH adult and adolescent opportunistic infection guidelines, 2026](https://clinicalinfo.hiv.gov/en/guidelines/hiv-clinical-guidelines-adult-and-adolescent-opportunistic-infections/varicella-zoster).

### Acute pain, persistent pain and what treatment can change

Keep lesions clean and dry, and ask which activities pain is preventing. Mild pain may respond to acetaminophen or an NSAID. For more severe pain the book discusses an opioid such as oxycodone, with regular reassessment of benefit and tolerability. Gabapentin or pregabalin may be considered when acute pain remains inadequately controlled, with particular attention to sedation, dizziness, ataxia and edema. The chapter does not give a single analgesic dose applicable to all older adults. Relief of acute pain, prevention of chronic neuralgia and preservation of alertness are separate outcomes when choosing a treatment (p1727–1728).

The prednisone schedule was a trial regimen in selected patients without relative contraindications: **60 mg daily for the first week**, **30 mg daily for the second**, then **15 mg daily for the third**. Some short-term outcomes, including sleep and return to activity, improved. Trials did not show prevention of postherpetic neuralgia. The authors discuss steroids for selected patients with otherwise unrelieved acute pain or certain cranial neuropathies, together with an antiviral. A trial of an epidural block using bupivacaine and methylprednisolone similarly distinguished short-term pain relief from prevention of persistent neuralgia (p1728).

### Explaining vaccine benefits and reactions

Recombinant zoster vaccine is preventive. The trials compared new episodes in vaccinated and placebo groups, not the speed of resolution of an active eruption. Figure 107-4 gives pooled efficacy of **97.2%** at ages 50–59, **96.6%** at 60–69, **91.3%** at 70–79 and **91.4%** at 80 or older. These are relative estimates within the study groups. The slightly smaller percentage in older groups still represents substantial protection in people whose baseline incidence is higher. In a secondary analysis by frailty, protection remained substantial across categories, although estimates in smaller groups were less precise (p1728–1729).

Table 107-2 separates occurrence of a reaction from interference with daily life. Among participants aged 70 or older, **69%** reported injection-site pain, while **4.0%** reported grade 3 pain that prevented normal activity. In this group **35%** reported myalgia and **37%** fatigue. Explain that local pain and temporary fatigue are common and usually resolve over **1–3 days**. A strong reaction to the first dose does not reliably predict a similarly strong reaction to the second. For a person living alone, planning meals or errands around the expected brief symptomatic period makes the information practical (p1729–1730).

The book describes a **0.5 mL intramuscular** dose, with two doses ordinarily **2–6 months apart**. A delayed return calls for completion rather than restarting. A prior episode of zoster or earlier live zoster vaccine does not remove the indication in an otherwise eligible older adult. Read the book-era position on immune compromise alongside the labeled current-practice note above. The population eligible for vaccination has changed since the evidence summarized in the text (p1729–1730).

## Exam traps

- HIV RNA suppression and CD4 recovery can follow different time courses in older adults (p1720–1721).
- Earlier onset of a comorbidity and higher prevalence at the same age support different conclusions about aging with HIV (p1721).
- A susceptible contact may develop varicella; the patient's own latent infection is the source of a zoster episode (p1725).
- Pain from light clothing is allodynia. Hyperpathia is an exaggerated response to an already painful stimulus (p1726).
- Trial evidence for reducing acute zoster pain does not establish prevention of postherpetic neuralgia (p1728).
- Vaccine efficacy, frequency of any injection-site pain and frequency of pain preventing normal activity have different denominators and meanings (p1729).


- Pain can precede the zoster eruption or occur without the expected rash. The history and distribution still matter (p1726).
- The trial enrollment window is not a reason to withhold treatment from a patient with ophthalmic zoster or continuing new vesicles (p1726–1728).
- Acute pain relief with a corticosteroid does not establish prevention of postherpetic neuralgia (p1728).

## Recall drill

**1. Why might HIV diagnosis be delayed in an older person?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Symptoms such as fatigue, weight loss or anemia can be attributed to other illnesses, and clinicians or patients may underestimate exposure risk. (p1719–1720)</details>

**2. What findings can prompt diagnostic HIV testing independent of routine screening age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examples include unexplained cytopenias, oral candidiasis, neuropathy, recurrent pneumonia or another HIV-associated illness. (p1720)</details>

**3. Should treatment wait for CD4 decline in newly diagnosed HIV?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The book recommends ART regardless of count and prompt linkage to care. Older adults may have slower CD4 recovery despite virologic suppression. (p1720–1721)</details>

**4. What factors influence ART selection in an older adult?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Consider comorbidities, existing organ injury, concomitant medications, likely resistance, patient preference and cost. (p1721)</details>

**5. How do accelerated and accentuated comorbidity differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Accelerated describes earlier onset. Accentuated describes higher prevalence compared with peers. Both influence screening and follow-up. (p1721)</details>

**6. What causes shingles decades after chickenpox?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Reactivation of a person's latent varicella-zoster virus. Aging and impaired cellular immunity increase risk. (p1725)</details>

**7. Which autoimmune disease is explicitly listed among zoster risk conditions?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Systemic lupus erythematosus. Transplant, hematologic malignancy, HIV and immunosuppressive medicines are additional settings. (p1725)</details>

**8. How can zoster mimic a noncutaneous disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dermatomal pain or hypersensitivity can precede visible lesions and mimic visceral, cardiac or neurologic pain. Zoster sine herpete can occur without rash. (p1726)</details>

**9. When might the clinical appearance alone be insufficient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An atypical eruption, suspected organ involvement or a need to distinguish recurrent HSV from zoster can justify confirmatory testing. (p1727)</details>

**10. What defines postherpetic neuralgia in this chapter?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Pain persisting at least 90 days after onset of the rash. (p1725)</details>

**11. What should prompt ophthalmology involvement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ophthalmic zoster with ocular symptoms or findings, including painful visual change, because corneal and intraocular inflammation can threaten sight. (p1726–1728)</details>

**12. Can ocular involvement still warrant antiviral treatment after three days?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Yes. Ophthalmic zoster and continuing new vesicles are exceptions to the trial-based 72-hour window. (p1727–1728)</details>

**13. What acyclovir regimen is listed for immunocompetent adults with cranial nerve involvement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Oral acyclovir 800 mg five times a day for seven days, with renal adjustment. Oral valacyclovir or famciclovir are less frequent alternatives. (p1727)</details>

**14. Why avoid routine corticosteroids for zoster?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>They have not prevented postherpetic neuralgia in trials and may cause harm. If used for selected acute indications, they accompany antiviral therapy. (p1728)</details>

**15. What should patients expect after recombinant vaccination?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Local pain and systemic symptoms may occur. Serious adverse events were similar between vaccine and placebo groups in the cited trials. (p1728–1729)</details>
