# Chapter 10 Study: Assessment of Decisional Capacity and Competencies

## Summary

**What capacity means**
- Assess understanding, appreciation, reasoning and the ability to express a choice (p151–152).
- Capacity is decision-specific and may be time-limited; impairment in one task does not establish incapacity for every decision (p154).
- Legal incompetence is a court determination; diagnosis, age or eccentricity alone does not establish it (p152).
- A choice that seems unreasonable to others does not itself negate self-determination; assess the reasoning process (p152).

**How to assess**
- Explain the decision clearly and address hearing, literacy, language and communication barriers (p152).
- Ask patients to describe the situation, options, consequences and their reasons in their own terms (p152).
- Immediate memory, language and frontal/executive functions support decision making (p153).
- Cognitive test scores can prompt further assessment, but no single cutoff determines capacity (p153).
- Consider pain, distress, coercion, trust, culture and the quality of communication; these may change a person's ability to participate (p153–154).
- Document the specific decision and demonstrated abilities or deficits; in many cases, a thoughtful interview is sufficient without a formal cognitive score (p154).

**Support & surrogate decisions**
- Medical treatment, finances, self-care, wills and appointing a proxy involve different tasks and requirements (p154–155).
- Not every incapacitated person requires guardianship; existing powers of attorney and supportive family arrangements may suffice (p153).
- During potentially reversible incapacity, use an interim surrogate arrangement while clarifying recovery and prior wishes (p155).
- Continue involving the person even when they cannot give full informed consent; seek assent and attend to expressed wishes (p155).
- Surrogates use the person's known preferences through substituted judgment; when these cannot be established, use a best-interest approach (p155).
