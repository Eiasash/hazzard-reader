# Chapter 10 Study: Assessment of Decisional Capacity and Competencies

## Summary

**What capacity means**
- Assess understanding, appreciation, reasoning and the ability to express a choice (p151–152).
- Capacity is decision-specific; impairment in one task does not establish incapacity for every decision (p154).
- Legal incompetence is a court determination; diagnosis, age or eccentricity alone does not establish it (p152).
- A choice that seems unreasonable to others does not itself negate self-determination; assess the reasoning process (p152).

**How to assess**
- Explain the decision clearly and address hearing, literacy, language and communication barriers (p152).
- Ask patients to describe the situation, options, consequences and their reasons in their own terms (p152).
- Immediate memory, language and frontal/executive functions support decision making (p153).
- Cognitive test scores can prompt further assessment, but no single cutoff determines capacity (p153).
- Consider pain, distress, coercion, trust, culture and the quality of communication; these may change a person's ability to participate (p153–154).
- Document the specific decision and demonstrated abilities or deficits; in many cases, a thoughtful interview is sufficient without a formal cognitive score (p154).

**Support & surrogate decisions**
- Medical treatment, finances, self-care, wills and appointing a proxy involve different tasks and requirements (p154–155).
- Not every incapacitated person requires guardianship; existing powers of attorney and supportive family arrangements may suffice (p153).
- During potentially reversible incapacity, use an interim surrogate arrangement while clarifying recovery and prior wishes (p155).
- Continue involving the person even when they cannot give full informed consent; seek assent and attend to expressed wishes (p155).
- Surrogates use the person's known preferences through substituted judgment; when these cannot be established, use a best-interest approach (pp153, 155).

### Four questions for one real decision

**Understanding** asks whether the person can explain the situation, proposed intervention, alternatives and consequences in their own words. **Appreciation** goes a step further: ask what those consequences mean in this person's own circumstances. A patient may accurately describe the general hazards of a procedure yet deny that the illness or its consequences could apply personally (p151–152).

**Reasoning** is the ability to compare alternatives and explain a choice in the light of the patient's priorities. A person may reasonably choose comfort over a longer life. **Expression of choice** asks for a communicated decision. Reliable communication can take forms other than fluent speech. It is possible to decline the decision-making role and voluntarily ask another person to choose (p152).

In September 2024 Q77, memory, language and executive function are distractors because they support the assessment without replacing any of its required elements. Record the patient's own explanation and how the decision relates to their life, including the option of no treatment. A chart entry consisting only of an orientation score or a yes/no declaration provides little basis for later review (p151–154).

### When capacity differs between tasks or over time

A person who can decide about a treatment may make serious errors with bills. Assess actual performance in the relevant task and the consequences of the difficulty. A person unable to make a complex choice may still express stable preferences about daily care or take part in selecting a trusted decision-maker. Where recovery is possible, address reversible causes and revisit the specific decision (p154–155).

## Exam traps

- Remembering the facts of a proposed treatment does not show that the person appreciates their personal implications (p152).
- A refusal, a risky choice or a preference to delegate is not itself evidence of incapacity (p151–152).
- Clinical capacity for a particular task can fluctuate, and a court's legal determination is a separate question (p152–155).

## Recall drill

**1. Which four abilities are assessed in the September 2024 item?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Understanding, appreciation, reasoning and expressing a choice (p151–152).</details>

**2. Which question tests understanding?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask the patient to explain the condition, available options and likely consequences in their own words (p152).</details>

**3. How do you move from understanding to appreciation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask how the described risks, benefits and outcomes would affect this individual personally (p152).</details>

**4. How can a patient show reasoning while declining the clinician's recommendation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Compare the options and explain how their consequences fit a consistent personal or religious priority (p152).</details>

**5. What must the assessment establish about the patient's choice?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>That the patient can communicate a decision. A person with capacity may also choose to delegate decision making (p152).</details>

**6. Why clarify hearing and language before reaching a conclusion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A failure of communication can look like poor understanding. Present the information so the patient can access it and then reassess (p152).</details>

**7. Where do memory, language and frontal executive functions fit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>They enable comprehension, retention, comparison and communication. They are underlying abilities rather than substitutes for the four criteria (p151–153).</details>

**8. Can a screening score establish capacity for a particular decision?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No cutoff settles the question. Evaluate the abilities needed for the actual decision under consideration (p153–154).</details>

**9. Why ask a patient why they are making a choice in private when appropriate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The answer may clarify their values or reveal coercion or undue influence. Interpretation depends on the circumstances (p153–154).</details>

**10. Does inability to manage bills establish incapacity to consent to medical treatment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. These require different abilities. Look for observed financial errors and examine capacity for the treatment separately (p154).</details>

**11. What belongs in documentation of a treatment decision?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The options including no treatment, the patient's account of potential outcomes, how they apply personally, the reasons and the expressed choice (p154).</details>

**12. What arrangement can suffice when financial capacity is impaired?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A previously authorized power of attorney or appropriate assistance may make formal guardianship unnecessary, depending on circumstances (p153–154).</details>

**13. How should an uncertain period of incapacity be approached?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use interim decision support and an appropriate surrogate while treating reversible problems and reassessing potential recovery (p155).</details>

**14. How do substituted judgment and best interests differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Substituted judgment follows what the person would have chosen. Best interests guides decisions when those preferences cannot be established (p153, p155).</details>

**15. How can an individual participate even if unable to give full consent?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Seek assent and attend to preferences or distress, alongside the surrogate's informed decision; cooperation may be essential to the intervention (p155).</details>
