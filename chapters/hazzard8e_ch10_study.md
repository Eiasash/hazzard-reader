# Chapter 10 Study: Assessment of Decisional Capacity and Competencies

## Summary

**What capacity means**
- Assess understanding, appreciation, reasoning and the ability to express a choice (p151–152).
- Capacity is decision-specific; impairment in one task does not establish incapacity for every decision (p154).
- Legal incompetence is a court determination; diagnosis, age or eccentricity alone does not establish it (p152).

Israeli context: A decision-making supporter helps the person obtain information, understand it and communicate a choice. Decision-making authority stays with the person. See [Chapter 7](?chapter=7s) and [the Ministry of Justice supported decision-making service](https://www.gov.il/he/service/supported_decision_making).
- A choice that seems unreasonable to others does not itself negate self-determination; assess the reasoning process (p152).

**How to assess**
- Explain the decision clearly and address hearing, literacy, language and communication barriers (p152).
- Ask patients to describe the situation, options, consequences and their reasons in their own terms (p152).
- Immediate memory, language and frontal/executive functions support decision making (p153).
- Cognitive test scores can prompt further assessment, but no single cutoff determines capacity (p153).
- Consider pain, distress, coercion, trust, culture and the quality of communication; these may change a person's ability to participate (p153–154).
- Document the specific decision and demonstrated abilities or deficits; in many cases, a thoughtful interview is sufficient without a formal cognitive score (p154).

**Support & surrogate decisions**
- Medical treatment, finances, self-care, wills and appointing a proxy involve different tasks and requirements (p154–155).
- Not every incapacitated person requires guardianship; existing powers of attorney and supportive family arrangements may suffice (p153).
- During potentially reversible incapacity, use an interim surrogate arrangement while clarifying recovery and prior wishes (p155).
- Continue involving the person even when they cannot give full informed consent; seek assent and attend to expressed wishes (p155).
- Surrogates use the person's known preferences through substituted judgment; when these cannot be established, use a best-interest approach (pp153, 155).

### Four questions for one real decision

**Understanding** asks whether the person can explain the situation, proposed intervention, alternatives and consequences in their own words. **Appreciation** goes a step further: ask what those consequences mean in this person's own circumstances. A patient may accurately describe the general hazards of a procedure yet deny that the illness or its consequences could apply personally (p151–152).

**Reasoning** is the ability to compare alternatives and explain a choice in the light of the patient's priorities. A person may reasonably choose comfort over a longer life. **Expression of choice** asks for a communicated decision. Reliable communication can take forms other than fluent speech. It is possible to decline the decision-making role and voluntarily ask another person to choose (p152).

In September 2024 Q77, memory, language and executive function are distractors because they support the assessment without replacing any of its required elements. Record the patient's own explanation and how the decision relates to their life, including the option of no treatment. A chart entry consisting only of an orientation score or a yes/no declaration provides little basis for later review (p151–154).

### When capacity differs between tasks or over time

A person who can decide about a treatment may make serious errors with bills. Assess actual performance in the relevant task and the consequences of the difficulty. A person unable to make a complex choice may still express stable preferences about daily care or take part in selecting a trusted decision-maker. Where recovery is possible, address reversible causes and revisit the specific decision (p154–155).

### Frame the consultation narrowly enough to answer it

A request to “assess competence” leaves the essential clinical question unanswered. Does the team need consent for an operation today, a plan for safe medication use at home, or help with the patient's financial affairs? Which alternatives are available, and does the decision need to be made now? A person may be capable of consenting to familiar treatment while unable to organize a complex household. Document the particular task, circumstances and time of assessment. A finding about that task should be usable by the team without implying a much broader loss of rights (p151–152, p154).

Two obligations can pull in different directions: respecting self-determination and protecting a person who cannot make the relevant decision. The clinician has strong grounds to examine capacity when a choice may have grave consequences, but the seriousness of the outcome is not itself evidence that an ability is absent. A patient who accepts the recommendation deserves the same quality of disclosure as one who refuses. Conversely, an agreeable answer from a patient who cannot explain what is proposed is a weak basis for calling the consent informed (p151–152).

Start with the patient's own account before supplying the answer. “What do you think is happening?” identifies misconceptions and the language the patient uses. Then explain the choices in a form the person can hear and understand. Avoid medical shorthand and check whether a hearing, language or literacy barrier has interfered. Ask again at the end of the discussion what the patient understands and expects. A change after clarification may indicate that the first explanation was inadequate, rather than a fundamental inability to decide (p151–152).

### Taking Table 10-1 from labels to evidence

Understanding includes comprehending the information, retaining it long enough for discussion and recognizing the relationship between an intervention and its possible outcome. It is possible to remember the name of an operation without understanding what it will change. Ask the patient to explain the illness, the purpose of the proposed intervention and the consequence of declining it in their own words. Repeating the consent form word for word can conceal an inability to make that link. Use follow-up questions rather than rewarding familiarity with a clinical term (p152).

Appreciation asks what those facts mean *to this person*. Imagine a patient who correctly says that untreated infection can worsen and treatment can help, but insists that the positive finding could not possibly concern them. Explore why. They may reasonably dispute an uncertain result or have misunderstood who was tested. Alternatively, they may be unable to recognize any connection to their own circumstances despite a clear explanation. The informative evidence is the patient's response to clarification, not the mere fact that the clinician and patient disagree (p151–152).

Reasoning involves comparing alternatives and their consequences within the patient's own values. Ask which consequence matters most, what disadvantage the person is prepared to accept, and why one alternative fits their aims better than another. A person may prefer less time alive at home to more time receiving burdensome treatment. Another person with the same illness may accept intensive treatment for the chance of attending a family event. Opposite choices can both reflect intact reasoning; the evidence lies in the comparison and its connection to a coherent personal priority (p152).

Expression of a choice is a distinct ability. A person may communicate a clear refusal but give no indication of understanding or appreciation. Conversely, expressive aphasia can make speech halting or introduce a wrong word that does not reflect what the person intends. Find a reliable means of communication and check that the reported answer actually represents the person's choice. A relative who answers before the patient has a chance to respond may make communication look easier while hiding the very ability under assessment (p152–153).

Consider a patient who initially chooses treatment, later declines and finally asks a daughter to decide. These changes call for exploration, not automatic disqualification. Did the patient hear a new fact, forget an important consequence, change priorities after reflection, or feel pressured when the daughter arrived? Ask which explanation fits and see whether the choice remains intelligible in the patient's own account. A capable patient can deliberately delegate part or all of the decision. Establish that delegation is voluntary and determine how much information and participation the patient still wants (p152–154).

### Cognitive testing, communication and the conditions of the interview

Cognitive testing and observation of decision making answer related but different questions. A memory test tells the examiner something about retention; asking the patient to recall the options just discussed shows whether memory compromised *this* decision. A language examination may reveal receptive or expressive difficulty but does not determine whether the patient can communicate meaning by another route. Validate the underlying function when a response is ambiguous. The mapped examination item asks for the abilities demonstrated during a decision, rather than a list of neuropsychological processes that contribute to them (p151–153).

Executive abilities can be impaired when ordinary conversation sounds intact. A patient may recite that there are several alternatives, yet be unable to compare their consequences, form a plan or notice an error. In a self-care evaluation this can appear as a plausible description of an intended routine that repeatedly fails in practice. Ask for an example, how the plan would be implemented and what the patient would do if it failed. Compare that account with demonstrated performance. Neither fluent conversation nor a reassuring impression during a short social visit tests the complete task (p153–154).

A brief mental status examination can identify a reason to look more closely, prompt a more detailed evaluation or help explain a failure already observed. It does not yield a universally valid score below which every decision belongs to someone else. Someone with poor short-term memory may still understand a simple choice when it is explained and make a consistent decision. Someone with relatively preserved memory may have marked frontal dysfunction and no workable plan for self-care. Record both the cognitive finding and *how it affected the decision* if testing was used (p153–154).

Consider the conditions under which the interview took place. Pain, anxiety, emotional distress, an unfamiliar clinician or mistrust may limit participation. A person may need extra time to take in information or a different explanation that fits their language and background. A clinician who presents a complex choice once in technical terms and then accepts silence as incapacity has not established whether the information was appropriately communicated. Identify a correctable barrier, address it where possible and assess the relevant abilities again (p152–154).

Voluntariness deserves its own inquiry even when understanding, appreciation, reasoning and expression appear intact. Family members can help explain personal values, support communication or provide a trusted role in shared decisions. Their involvement may be culturally expected and freely chosen. It can also introduce pressure, threats or inducements. Ask the patient why they are making this decision and whose preferences it reflects. If possible, let the patient speak without the person exerting the possible influence. Distinguishing ordinary influence from undue pressure may require judgment about the actual circumstances (p153–154).

### Assessing treatment refusal and self-care

For a treatment decision, provide the condition, realistic alternatives including no treatment, and the consequences sufficiently clearly for the patient to weigh them. In a refusal, identify the specific option declined. Declining an invasive procedure may coexist with acceptance of analgesia, monitoring or another intervention. The question “Will you follow the doctor's advice?” compresses these choices into obedience. A better interview asks the patient to describe what each option would mean, state which outcome matters and choose among the real alternatives (p152, p154).

An illustrative note about such an interview might say that the patient explained the illness and what the procedure was intended to accomplish, identified a meaningful risk of declining it, and favored a less burdensome course because maintaining a particular ability mattered most. If an ability was absent, describe the finding just as specifically: for example, the patient repeatedly forgot the alternatives before any comparison could be made despite an accessible explanation. The chart should let a later clinician see the reasoning behind the assessment and its limits, whether or not a formal test score was obtained (p154).

Self-care raises a different problem. A person may lack the physical dexterity to open medication containers but understand the risk of missed doses and accept a reliable person to prepare and check the medication. The task of independently organizing the pills is impaired; the ability to solve the problem by arranging appropriate help may be intact. A person who repeatedly claims to manage well despite demonstrated dangerous errors and cannot consider any practical support raises a different concern. Investigate the specific functional failure and the person's response to it before concluding that remaining at home is unsafe (p154).

A structured conversation about home safety can begin with a concrete example such as repeated difficulty preparing a meal. Ask what went wrong, whether it could recur, which supports are available and whether they are feasible. A meal-delivery plan that the person cannot arrange or does not recognize when it arrives may not resolve the risk. A service the patient understands, accepts and can actually access may permit independence in other domains. Distinguish a choice to tolerate a recognized risk from a failure to appreciate that the risk exists (p154).

Collateral information is useful here because self-care has an observable outcome. A family member may report spoiled food, missed bills or unsuccessful attempts to provide help. Record what was actually seen, the patient's explanation and whether the proposed intervention would address it. “Eccentric” tells the clinician little about planning, error detection or safety. Someone whose home or habits differ from the family's expectations can still understand the circumstances and make a reasoned choice. The strongest evidence relates the demonstrated failure to a deficit in one or more of the abilities required for the particular task (p153–154).

### Financial decisions, legal instruments and scope of protection

Financial management can fail before other capacities. Repeated unpaid bills or major errors in handling money are more informative than a general impression that an older person is forgetful. Assess the nature and practical significance of the errors and the person's insight. They may recognize the problem and freely choose a reliable helper. Occupational therapy or neuropsychological assessment can contribute when the functional picture is uncertain. A conclusion that someone needs financial supervision does not, on its own, make them unable to consent to medical care or choose daily activities (p153–154).

The chapter distinguishes judicial findings and the clinician's functional assessment in a US legal context. Guardianship of finances concerns money and property; guardianship of the person can encompass broader issues such as safety, care and living arrangements. Limited guardianship can restrict the appointed person's role to an area in which the individual cannot act. Previously arranged powers of attorney or an agreed, workable support arrangement sometimes make additional court involvement unnecessary. Where monitoring or formal authority is needed, court appointment can add oversight. The exact legal framework varies by jurisdiction (p152–153).

A family relationship alone does not determine the quality of a substitute decision. In discussing court-appointed conservators the chapter reports family appointments in **75%–85%** of cases in which a court acted. That proportion says nothing about whether a particular relative understands the patient's preferences, is free of conflict or can manage the practical responsibility. Ask what the person understood and chose before capacity was lost, what the proposed representative knows and which decision actually requires representation. Oversight and the scope of authority are separate from familiarity with the person (p153).

The capacity to execute one legal document is not interchangeable with the capacity to execute all of them. The chapter describes the possibility that a person who cannot manage ongoing financial affairs may still understand what they wish to do with an estate and explain the logic of that choice. Similarly, someone may identify a person they trust to make health decisions and explain why, even when the details of a complex treatment are beyond them. Document the task the interview addressed. A decision about appointing an agent is not a retrospective declaration that all earlier documents are valid or invalid (p154–155).

A living will can pose a different challenge: it asks the person to anticipate clinical conditions they have never experienced and imagine the consequences of treatments in those settings. Even people with intact cognition may find that abstraction difficult. Clarify terms and explore the person's values and examples, rather than assuming that a signature demonstrates understanding of every hypothetical future event. Preferences expressed about concrete experiences can remain informative when a person later has difficulty discussing a broad hypothetical scenario (p155).

A referral letter about finances, for example, should say which records or tasks were reviewed, who provided the observations, what errors actually occurred and how the individual addressed them. If the person accurately identifies a trusted agent but cannot describe the estate or the consequences of a particular transaction, report those findings separately. The assessor need not turn an answer about one document into an opinion on every aspect of personal and financial life. This preserves the person's remaining abilities while giving the decision maker specific information about the problem that prompted the referral (p153–155).

### Fluctuating capacity, surrogate decisions and assent

Delirium or a recent stroke can impair decision making while leaving recovery uncertain. Determine whether the decision can wait and what harm might arise from delay. If it can wait, address reversible barriers and reassess when participation is more likely. If it cannot, identify an appropriate interim decision maker and look for previously expressed wishes. The temporary need for a surrogate is not a declaration of permanent global incapacity. Record who will review the issue and restore the patient's role as abilities return (p155).

The chapter gives special weight to earlier wishes when an interim decision must be made. If those wishes are unknown and recovery remains possible, it discusses protecting life or continued function until the patient can participate again or the impairment is better understood. This is a response to uncertainty in the specific situation described. A reliable, relevant prior refusal would change the reasoning, as would new information about prognosis. Keep the factual uncertainty, the patient's values and the decision-maker's authority distinct in the documentation (p155).

Substituted judgment asks what this individual would choose based on earlier statements and long-held beliefs. Best-interest reasoning becomes relevant when preferences cannot be established. A surrogate's statement “I would want this treatment” answers a different question from “She repeatedly said she would accept this burden to achieve that outcome.” Ask for the experiences or statements that support the latter account. Continue observing present preferences and signs of comfort, distress or willingness to cooperate even if those observations cannot substitute for informed consent to the entire plan (p153, p155).

Research adds uncertainty beyond a familiar therapeutic choice. The participant may need to understand that the personal benefit is uncertain and that a study has purposes beyond choosing what is best for this one patient. Ask the person or surrogate why participation is proposed and assess voluntariness. The chapter discusses combining surrogate consent with the patient's assent where appropriate. A person who cannot grasp the full protocol can still indicate whether the procedures cause pain or whether they wish to cooperate. That information matters especially when participation requires the patient's active cooperation (p155).

## Exam traps

- Remembering the facts of a proposed treatment does not show that the person appreciates their personal implications (p152).
- A refusal, a risky choice or a preference to delegate is not itself evidence of incapacity (p151–152).
- Clinical capacity for a particular task can fluctuate, and a court's legal determination is a separate question (p152–155).
