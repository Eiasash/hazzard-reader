## Refeeding after prolonged poor intake

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-9133456e88257f8d41edd4d4" data-exam-read-page="432" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**The first few days matter.** The June 2023 patient could not swallow after a hemorrhagic stroke and started nasogastric feeding a month later. Carbohydrate delivery increases insulin-driven nutrient uptake and the intracellular need for phosphate. Serum phosphate can fall just as cellular metabolism accelerates. Potassium also moves into cells. A high potassium from muscle breakdown describes a different process. The older stroke question added hyperglycemia to low potassium and phosphate. [Mechanism, p. 432](?chapter=30#exam-p432-mcq-9133456e88257f8d41edd4d4).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-9133456e88257f8d41edd4d4" data-exam-read-page="431" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Monitoring answers a different question from diagnosis.** Check phosphate, magnesium and potassium when feeding restarts in a severely depleted patient. Follow glucose, daily weight, fluid balance and gastrointestinal tolerance until stable. Renal or cardiac impairment increases the consequences of fluid retention. Total intake before the stroke, duration of subsequent poor intake and recent weight change tell you more about risk than the name of the feeding route. [Table 30-12, p. 431; monitoring discussion, p. 432](?chapter=30#exam-p431-mcq-9133456e88257f8d41edd4d4).

## Weight loss, BMI and serum proteins

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7b26cdc007e22b5187d35b3b" data-exam-read-page="424" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Ask for the usual weight and the date it changed.** At 54 kg and a BMI of 19.2, a 78-year-old outpatient may have always been lean or may have lost substantial tissue. Compare measured weights, check whether loss was intentional and consider fluid shifts. Loss of at least 5% over 6–12 months warrants investigation in the outpatient setting. An obese patient can also lose substantial lean tissue without ever crossing into the underweight BMI range. [Outpatient assessment, p. 424](?chapter=30#exam-p424-mcq-7b26cdc007e22b5187d35b3b).

**Why albumin and prealbumin can mislead.** Illness, inflammation, redistribution and protein losses influence serum albumin. A normal value can accompany early deterioration, and a low value predicts poor outcomes without pinpointing the cause. Prealbumin responds more rapidly but shares the specificity problem. September 2024 accepted both albumin and a history of recent weight loss. The book gives greater weight to the outpatient weight trajectory. In acute illness, compare observed intake with estimated requirements and assess muscle, fat, edema and function. [Serum proteins, pp. 422–425](?chapter=30#ch30-albumin).

**Screen, then assess.** MST, MUST and MNA identify people who need a fuller assessment. Establish who buys, prepares and serves meals, whether the patient can chew and swallow, and what is actually consumed. Starvation tends to conserve protein while fat stores are mobilized. Inflammatory cachexia can accelerate muscle catabolism despite added calories. When a single cause explains involuntary loss, depression, gastrointestinal disease and cancer are common. Guide further investigation by the history, examination and initial tests. A negative initial assessment calls for active surveillance. [Starvation and cachexia, p. 424; Figure 30-8, p. 427](?chapter=30#ch30-loss).

## Appetite, medicines and eating with others

**Treat the barrier you find.** A drug started shortly before appetite fell deserves a review of indication, timing and alternatives. The June 2022 question credits levetiracetam as a possible cause of decreased appetite; it cites the earlier edition. Table 30-2 (8e p410) lists medication classes, including drugs for seizures, among potential contributors to appetite suppression or altered smell. Sedatives can interfere with alertness at meals. Dysgeusia directs attention toward oral health, medicines and possible zinc deficiency. Painful dentition calls for dental care. Isolation or forgetfulness calls for company, meal cues or help eating. [Medication classes, p. 410](?chapter=30#ch30-drugs) · [weight-loss algorithm, p. 427](?chapter=30#ch30-loss).

**Decide what a treatment should achieve.** In community-dwelling older adults with poor appetite, review medicines and food access before prescribing a stimulant. Megestrol can produce a small, predominantly fat gain at the cost of thrombosis, edema and increased mortality. Better function or survival does not follow from a higher weight. Mirtazapine can help a patient with depression, but evidence for appetite stimulation without depression is weak. The AGS Choosing Wisely recommendation highlighted assistance, social supports and goals. [Oral approaches, pp. 427–429](?chapter=30#ch30-oral).

**Supplement when there is a nutritional deficit.** Offer preferred foods, relax avoidable restrictions, fortify meals and give smaller portions more frequently. A more concentrated supplement supplies 1.5–2 kcal/mL versus roughly 1 kcal/mL for a standard drink. Offer it between meals to limit displacement of food. Trial benefits were most plausible in undernourished older hospital patients. The average gain was modest, and evidence for better function or shorter stays was limited. [Calorie density and timing, p. 428](?chapter=30#ch30-supplement).

## Obesity, muscle and function

**Think beyond the mortality curve.** Excess body weight is associated with urinary incontinence, osteoarthritis, impaired mobility and greater work during everyday activities. BMI does not separate muscle from fat or show its distribution. Visceral adiposity increases with age, while muscle and aerobic reserve may decline. Sarcopenic obesity can leave someone with substantial body mass but insufficient strength to rise from a chair. Smoking, selection of survivors and illness-related weight loss complicate mortality comparisons. [Table 30-4, p. 413](?chapter=30#ch30-obesity).

Before prescribing weight loss, ask whether a weight-related condition is limiting function and whether the patient can exercise and maintain adequate nutrient intake. Diet-only weight loss can deplete muscle and bone. Combine a modest energy deficit with adequate protein and individually tailored resistance and endurance exercise when benefit is expected. In a frail patient already losing weight, a further calorie deficit worsens nitrogen balance. Evidence for drug or surgical treatment in older adults was limited at publication. [Weight reduction, pp. 413–415](?chapter=30#p414).

## Dietary requirements and prevention

- **Energy and protein.** Less lean mass and activity often lower energy needs more than micronutrient needs. Make each meal nutrient dense. The protein RDA discussed in the chapter is 0.8 g/kg/day. Some experts propose 1.0–1.25 g/kg/day for older adults. With acute illness, major surgery or injury the book discusses 1.5 g/kg/day or more when compatible with renal and hepatic function. Pair adequate nutrition with resistance training to rebuild strength. [Energy and protein, pp. 414–415](?chapter=30#p415). Table 30-9 (p425) estimates **1.2–1.4 g/kg/day** for injury or illness and **1.4–1.8 g/kg/day** for severe stress or sepsis.
- **Fats, carbohydrate, fiber and water.** A Mediterranean pattern favors plant oils, nuts, fish and minimally processed foods. Whole grains and legumes supply carbohydrate with fiber. Increase fiber gradually with adequate fluid to avoid bloating or worsening constipation. Impaired thirst, dependency, heat and febrile illness increase dehydration risk. A caregiver may need to offer and document drinks. [Dietary pattern, pp. 416–418](?chapter=30#ch30-med).
- **Specific deficiencies.** Neuropathy, loss of position sense or gait disturbance can occur in B12 deficiency without macrocytosis or anemia. In Table 30-7, B12 below 200 pg/mL is deficient and 200–350 pg/mL is borderline. Symptomatic patients receive intramuscular loading followed by monthly maintenance. Folate deficiency can also cause macrocytosis, so distinguish it from B12 deficiency before treating with folate alone. Calcium carbonate is taken with food and can worsen constipation. Vitamin D evaluation and replacement depend on risk, bone health and deficiency. [B12 treatment, pp. 419–421](?chapter=30#ch30-b12).
- **Supplements and clinical outcomes.** Dietary reference values describe requirements and upper limits. They do not justify high-dose supplementation without an indication. Observational associations between nutrient status and cognition are not proof that vitamins prevent dementia. The original AREDS combination slowed progression in selected eyes with existing macular degeneration. It did not prevent initial AMD or cataracts. Broad benefits for cancer or cardiovascular prevention have not been shown for routine vitamin supplementation in replete adults. [Micronutrients, pp. 418–422](?chapter=30#p421).

## Choosing and monitoring nutritional support

**Start with prognosis, preferences and the gut.** If oral measures are inadequate, a functioning and accessible gut generally favors enteral nutrition. Obstruction, ischemia or severe malabsorption may preclude it. Partial enteral delivery can continue when supplemental parenteral nutrition becomes necessary. Consider timing in light of prior undernutrition and illness severity. When well nourished on ICU admission with an enteral contraindication, the book advises withholding PN in the first week. It discusses earlier PN in severely malnourished high-risk patients who cannot receive EN, and supplemental PN after 7–10 days if EN alone supplies at most 60% of needs. [Choice of route, pp. 429–432](?chapter=30#ch30-route).

**Tube feeding.** A nasal tube commonly provides short-term access. Consider gastrostomy or jejunostomy when longer support is anticipated. Aspiration of endogenous oral and gastric secretions remains possible even after postpyloric placement. Select standard formula for most patients, including many with diabetes. A renal formula can deliver calories while restricting fluid and electrolytes. Formula alone rarely supplies all free water. Once targets are met, overnight delivery can free daytime hours for mobility. [Tube placement and formulas, pp. 430–431](?chapter=30#ch30-aspiration).

**Respond to intolerance.** Distension, nausea or pain warrants clinical review. Correct medication or formula contributors when symptomatic residuals exceed 250 mL. Table 30-12 includes a slower delivery rate for diarrhea, alongside evaluation for infection, particularly C. difficile. Regular water flushes help prevent tube obstruction. Do not resume feeding through a replaced tube until proper placement is confirmed. [Administration and complications, pp. 431–432](?chapter=30#ch30-delivery).

**Parenteral nutrition.** Hypertonic total PN needs central venous access. Peripheral solutions are limited by venous tolerance and often cannot meet full requirements. Prescribe and titrate dextrose, amino acids, lipid, electrolytes, vitamins and trace elements in light of actual organ function and losses. Glucose delivery above 5 mg/kg/min risks hyperglycemia, excess carbon dioxide production and fatty liver. Monitor glucose, daily fluid balance, electrolytes, catheter site and hepatic indices. Introduce oral or enteral nutrition as feasible and taper PN as that route meets requirements. [PN composition and monitoring, pp. 432–435](?chapter=30#p433).

**Different settings, different goals.** After hip fracture, oral supplementation in undernourished patients can reduce complications. Dyspnea, systemic inflammation and early satiety contribute to poor intake in COPD. In nursing homes, fortified favorites, snacks and assisted meals can stabilize weight. For advanced dementia, Hazzard describes the American Geriatrics Society preference for assisted oral feeding. Tube feeding has not demonstrated better survival or protection against aspiration or pressure injury and may increase agitation and the use of restraints. [Special issues, p. 435](?chapter=30#ch30-dementia).

## Summary

**Nutrition & assessment**
- Aging increases the fat-to-body-mass ratio; muscle preservation depends on both diet and activity. Obesity can coexist with serious protein undernutrition (p405–406).
- Weight loss of at least **5%** of baseline weight over **6–12 months** warrants investigation (pp406, 422; voluntary loss also warrants attention in frail older adults).
- Assess intake, physical findings, illness, medications and social barriers. Albumin/prealbumin chiefly reflect inflammation and disease severity and are not stand-alone nutritional diagnoses (p406, p425).
- Starvation-related losses may reverse with feeding; inflammatory cachexia includes muscle catabolism that refeeding may not reverse (p424).

**Management**
- Investigate causes and support intake early; high-protein/high-calorie oral supplements may reduce morbidity and mortality in hospitalized malnourished patients at least **75** (p406).
- Protein RDA is **0.8 g/kg/day**; some advocate **1.0–1.25 g/kg/day** for older adults, but the chapter describes ongoing uncertainty (p415).
- Acutely ill hospitalized patients or those recovering from major surgery/trauma generally warrant **1.5 g/kg/day** or more, unless renal/hepatic disease necessitates restriction (p415). Table 30-9 (p425) estimates **1.2–1.4 g/kg/day** for injury or illness and **1.4–1.8 g/kg/day** for severe stress or sepsis.
- Selected obese older adults may benefit from controlled weight loss, but diet-only weight loss also reduces muscle and bone. Combine nutrition with individualized endurance/resistance exercise (p413–414).

**Artificial nutrition**
- Enteral feeding is preferred when nutritional support is needed and the gut functions; prognosis and preferences remain essential (p406, p429).
- Bowel obstruction, ischemia, major upper-GI bleeding, inadequate bowel surface or intractable vomiting/diarrhea can preclude enteral feeding (p429).
- Refeeding may shift potassium and phosphate intracellularly; monitor tolerance, daily weight, glucose and electrolytes until stable (p432).
- Parenteral glucose should not exceed **5 mg/kg/min**; excess delivery risks hyperglycemia, excess carbon dioxide production and fatty liver (p433).

**Drugs & exam traps**
- Megestrol can cause thrombosis, fluid retention and increased mortality; observed weight gain is often fat rather than improved function (p428).
- Mirtazapine lacks high-quality evidence for weight gain without depression. Routine micronutrient supplements have no demonstrated benefit without deficiency or high deficiency risk (p406, p429).
- Low albumin does not prove inadequate feeding, and obesity does not exclude malnutrition (p406, p423).
