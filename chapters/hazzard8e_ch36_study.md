# Chapter 36 Study: Gynecologic Disorders

## Summary

**Assessment & vulvovaginal symptoms**
- Ask about bleeding and pelvic/vulvovaginal pain even when a visit addresses other concerns; review surgical, hormonal and screening history (p527–528).
- Pelvic pain often arises outside reproductive organs; assess urinary, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal causes (p527, p530).
- Persistent or atypical vulvar lesions merit examination and possible biopsy; lichen sclerosus can scar and has malignant potential (p531–532).
- Discharge requires examination: candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis, sexually transmitted infection and atrophy have different findings (p534).
- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause may cause dryness, dyspareunia and urinary symptoms; low-dose local estrogen is a treatment option (p534–536).

**Bleeding & cancer**
- Postmenopausal bleeding requires endometrial assessment, even if other findings are present (p539).
- Sampling may be deferred at an endometrial thickness of **4 mm or less**, but biopsy is preferable in older women or recurrent/persistent bleeding regardless of imaging (p539).
- Stopping cervical screening depends on adequate previous negative screening and low risk, not age alone (p536–537).
- Ultrasound/CA-125 screening is ineffective in low-risk women; investigate suspicious persistent symptoms (p527, p539).
- Most adnexal masses are benign after menopause; transvaginal ultrasound helps characterize them (p539).

**Hormones & pelvic support**
- Hazzard calls initiation of systemic hormone therapy after age **65** contraindicated. It distinguishes continued treatment begun at menopause, which calls for an individualized decision (p540).
- Prolapse treatment depends on bother, overall health and goals. Observation may suit uncomplicated, minimally bothersome prolapse (p544).
- Refer for nonhealing ulceration, bleeding, retention causing recurrent infection, or hydronephrosis (p544).
- Pelvic-floor exercises and pessaries offer conservative options; assess tissue health and ability to manage the device (p544–545).
- Less mobile or cognitively impaired pessary users may need close follow-up within **1–3 months**; tailor the interval to the patient (p545).

### Localizing bleeding, pain and pruritus

Inspect the vulva, vagina and cervix while assessing postmenopausal bleeding. A visible local lesion may explain discomfort but should not end the evaluation for endometrial disease. Ultrasound and tissue sampling answer different questions: an endometrial measurement estimates risk, while biopsy can identify pathology. The chapter advises biopsy when bleeding persists or recurs even with a thin stripe (p538–539).

> Current practice (not the book): ACOG now recommends ultrasound plus endometrial sampling initially for most people with postmenopausal bleeding. Ultrasound alone is an option in selected low-risk patients with one episode, a fully seen lining no thicker than 4 mm and reliable access to immediate reassessment if bleeding recurs. - [ACOG Clinical Practice Update, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000006275).

Pruritus with white plaques, bruising, fissures or loss of normal architecture suggests lichen sclerosus. Perianal extension may form a figure-of-eight pattern. Persistent or changing lesions merit inspection and consideration of biopsy. By contrast, diffuse dryness, burning and dyspareunia can arise from estrogen deficiency; lack of response to vaginal estrogen should trigger another look for a vulvar disorder (p531–534).

### Local treatment, systemic treatment and prolapse

For symptomatic atrophy the chapter describes a vaginal estradiol tablet **10 micrograms nightly for 2 weeks**, then twice weekly. The low maintenance dose follows an initial phase of tissue remodeling. When the complaint is a bulge, ask about emptying the bladder, bleeding and interference with activities. A pessary may relieve symptoms without surgery, provided that fit, epithelial health and follow-up can be maintained (p534, p544–545).

> Current practice (not the book): Starting systemic menopausal hormone therapy after age 60 or more than 10 years from menopause carries higher absolute vascular risks and requires individualized assessment. Selected well-informed patients may choose to start later; continuation after 65 also calls for periodic review. - [North American Menopause Society position statement, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35797481/).

### Build the history around the organs present and symptoms the patient may not volunteer

Start with the indication for each previous operation. Hysterectomy does not by itself tell you whether the cervix or ovaries were removed, why the operation was performed, whether there was dysplasia, or whether earlier prolapse or incontinence resolved. Those details change the examination, screening decision and differential for bleeding. Ask separately about breast pain or discharge, vaginal staining, dryness or burning, pain with intercourse, pelvic pressure, urinary emptying, constipation and fecal leakage. Direct questions are important because a patient may attribute the symptom to aging or may have come to discuss a different illness (p527–528).

Table 36-1 contrasts two approaches to the same concerns. Someone who can describe her symptoms may distinguish urgency from difficulty initiating urination and can explain how sexual pain affects her. For someone with substantial cognitive impairment, ask what she can report and supplement it with observations: new staining of the bra or underwear, guarding during perineal care, a change in pad use, infrequent hard stools or a change in behavior during toileting. Establish which examinations are tolerable and what question each one answers. A low yield from routine internal examination in an asymptomatic person is different from an examination prompted by bleeding, a new mass or pain (p527–529).

Figure 36-1 makes one cross-system cause visible. A large rectal fecal impaction occupies space in the pelvis and can compress the bladder, urethra and adjacent tissues. A patient reporting new urinary symptoms as well as abdominal discomfort may need an examination for impaction rather than repeated empiric treatment for infection. Similarly, when pain persists after a gynecologic operation, verify the original diagnosis and the course of symptoms. The temporary relationship to surgery does not identify the organ now responsible for pain (p528–531).

### Symptoms in the breast and pelvis: identify a lesion that needs investigation

Inspect both breasts for contour, retraction, edema, erythema and changes of nipple or areola. Describe any palpable finding by site and relation to surrounding tissue. Examination cannot reliably decide whether a mass is solid or cystic, benign or malignant. A new lump merits assessment with imaging and pathology as indicated even if mobile or painless. Breast pain alone has a different differential: ask whether pressure over ribs, chest wall or cervical structures reproduces the symptom. The chapter includes costochondritis, radiculopathy, intercostal neuralgia and zoster among extramammary causes. A new postmenopausal symptom should not simply be treated with medicines used for cyclic mastalgia in younger women (p529–530).

Clarify whether nipple discharge is spontaneous or occurs only with expression, unilateral or bilateral, and from one or several ducts. Spontaneous bloody or serous discharge from a single duct is pathologic. An intraductal papilloma is a common benign explanation, but the chapter cites an underlying malignancy risk of approximately **20%** in older women with pathologic discharge even without other clinical or imaging findings. Eczema can mimic discharge; inspect the skin as well as the duct opening. Persistent breast inflammation in a nonlactating woman likewise requires reconsideration of the diagnosis rather than assuming the same cause as infectious lactational mastitis (p529–530).

For pelvic pain, ask the patient to localize it and describe its relationship to voiding, defecation, movement and contact. Urinary, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal causes are important even if the patient calls the symptom ovarian or vaginal pain. On the Carnett maneuver, greater point tenderness while the patient tightens her abdominal muscles supports an abdominal-wall component. That finding does not eliminate an internal cause. Palpating the pelvic floor can identify painful muscle tension, and inspection may disclose a lesion or atrophy; the workup follows the location and associated findings rather than assuming the uterus or ovaries are responsible (p530–531).

### Examine vulvar architecture before calling persistent pruritus atrophy

Use Figure 36-2 to orient the description. Identify the labia, clitoral hood, vestibule, urethral meatus, Bartholin duct region, posterior fourchette and perineum. A red polyp at the urethral opening can be a caruncle; the figure compares a chronic and an inflamed lesion. Establish whether blood on a pad originates from this lesion, excoriated external skin, the vagina or elsewhere. Record pigment, erosion, scale, fissures, architectural change and the patient's site of maximal symptoms. A discrete lesion that persists after apparently appropriate treatment deserves another examination or biopsy (p531–532, p538–541).

Table 36-2 organizes dermatoses by pathology. An eczematous or spongiotic process includes atopic, allergic-contact and irritant-contact dermatitis; ask about products touching the skin. An acanthotic pattern includes psoriasis and lichen simplex chronicus. Persistent scratching can thicken the skin and perpetuate itch, and the same reaction can overlie another dermatosis. Lichen sclerosus and lichen planus are lichenoid diseases. Vesiculobullous, granulomatous or vasculopathic processes are further alternatives when the appearance is atypical. The table's purpose is to prevent a nonspecific complaint such as burning from being assigned a single empiric treatment indefinitely (p531–532).

Lichen sclerosus can produce pale porcelain-like changes, bruising, fissures and distortion around the vulva and anus. Look for loss of normal labial structures and narrowing that may make intercourse or examination painful. The book estimates malignant potential as possibly as high as **4%–5%** and favors confirming the diagnosis by biopsy with at least annual visual inspection. A persistent focal ulcer or new induration requires reassessment even in someone with known lichen sclerosus. Topical estrogen may improve adjacent atrophic tissue but has little direct effect on the primary dermatosis. Lichen planus can produce painful erosions, involve other mucosa and also scar; specialist assessment and topical or systemic anti-inflammatory treatment may be needed (p531–533).

Distinguish itch with an identifiable cause from vulvodynia. The definition cited for vulvodynia requires **at least 3 months** of vulvar pain without an identifiable cause. Infection, neoplasia, inflammatory disease, trauma, hormonal deficiency and neurologic disorders should be considered first. Localized contact pain and pelvic muscle tenderness can guide nonpharmacologic measures. Gabapentin, pregabalin and carbamazepine are among the adjuncts mentioned for selected patients, alongside physical and cognitive therapies. A persistently abnormal pigmented lesion is a separate diagnostic pathway; vulvar melanoma and squamous cancer can be missed when pain or pruritus is treated without inspection (p532–533).

### Discharge, dryness and sex-related symptoms

Characterize the discharge and inspect the epithelium before prescribing another course of antifungal medication. Candida and bacterial vaginosis can each cause symptoms but differ on examination and microscopy. A thin gray malodorous discharge with epithelial cells densely coated by bacteria favors bacterial vaginosis. The book recommends treatment when it causes symptoms or when vaginal surgery is planned. Consider trichomoniasis and other sexually transmitted infections when relevant; identification of trichomoniasis should lead to partner treatment and assessment for other infection. Do not infer absence of sexual exposure from age or residence in a care facility (p533–534).

Figure 36-3 relates pale, smooth or shiny tissue with petechiae and inflammation to loss of superficial epithelial layers in the low-estrogen state. This can produce burning, dyspareunia, spotting with contact and lower urinary tract symptoms. A vaginal moisturizer used regularly addresses baseline symptoms, whereas a lubricant addresses friction during sexual activity. Persistent symptoms despite local treatment should reopen the differential, particularly if focal, accompanied by bleeding, or associated with loss of architecture. Examination can establish both where pain occurs and whether the tissue response matches the presumed diagnosis (p533–535).

Table 36-3 distinguishes local hormone preparations by amount delivered and systemic exposure. The **4-microgram** estradiol insert or **10-microgram** insert/tablet is used daily for **2 weeks**, then twice weekly. The local **7.5-microgram** estradiol ring is replaced every **90 days**; a different vaginal ring delivers systemic estrogen. The table describes a conjugated-estrogen cream concentration of **0.625 mg/g**: approximately half a gram supplies about 0.3 mg. Larger amounts increase systemic absorption. For estradiol cream **100 micrograms/g**, a half-gram application delivers **50 micrograms** (Table 36-3, p536). The table's usual schedule is 0.5–1 g daily for two weeks and then twice weekly. Intravaginal prasterone is a distinct **6.5-mg** insert used daily. A concentration, a mass of cream and a delivered dose are three different quantities (p534–536).

Match the preparation to the complaint, dexterity and likelihood of continued use. Low-dose local treatment can suffice for genitourinary symptoms without treating systemic vasomotor symptoms. In a woman with previous breast cancer, the chapter recommends trying nonhormonal options first; particular caution and consultation are warranted with aromatase inhibitors. Higher local cream doses or a systemic ring should not be assumed to have the same exposure as a low-dose tablet. Recurrent spotting during treatment remains an indication to investigate its source. The separate question of initiating or continuing systemic therapy after menopause requires a review of vascular and other risks rather than extrapolation from local symptom treatment (p534–536, p540).

### Screening is not the workup for bleeding

Table 36-4 does not permit stopping cervical screening based on a birthday alone. The book combines the age or post-treatment time criterion with low risk and adequate previous negative tests. In its table, adequate screening means **3 consecutive negative cytology tests** or **2 negative cytology/HPV cotests** within **10 years**, with the most recent within **5 years**. Previous high-grade dysplasia, HIV or other immunosuppression change the decision. It explicitly includes an extended interval after hysterectomy performed for CIN2 or worse. Find the indication and pathology report before declaring screening complete. A visually suspicious cervix needs diagnostic evaluation even when screening would otherwise have ended (p535–537, p539).

Table 36-5 starts with the symptom as reported rather than assuming a uterine source. In the uterus consider atrophy, polyps, hyperplasia, hormonal stimulation and cancer. A cervical polyp, cervicitis or cervical cancer can bleed. Vaginal atrophy, trauma, prolapse-related abrasions, a foreign body and vaginal cancer may produce similar staining. Vulvar ulcers, a bleeding urethral caruncle, hematuria, rectal prolapse or hemorrhoids may be mistaken for vaginal bleeding. Review anticoagulants and estrogen exposure, but do not let the presence of a plausible medication explanation remove the need for examination and endometrial assessment (p537–539).

The distinction between imaging and histology is especially important after menopause. Identify the cervix and vaginal walls on examination; biopsy a suspicious cervix. Assess the endometrium even if atrophy or an external lesion is present. The book allows deferring sampling with an adequately assessed thin endometrium in selected patients, while preferring biopsy for older women or persistent or recurrent bleeding regardless of thickness. A focal lesion, an inadequate specimen and a reassuring previous test are not interchangeable with a current adequate evaluation. Discuss what the result might change even if the patient would decline major surgery: knowing the reason for recurrent bleeding can improve palliative plans (p538–539).

### Characterizing an adnexal finding

Ask about new persistent abdominal or pelvic symptoms, bloating and changes in appetite or bowel and urinary function. In an asymptomatic, average-risk population, screening with ultrasound and CA-125 has not shown the hoped-for benefit. A woman with sustained symptoms or a palpable or incidentally identified mass already has a diagnostic question. Transvaginal ultrasound can distinguish a simple fluid-filled cyst from a lesion with septations, solid areas or another complex appearance. MRI can clarify some lesions when ultrasound is technically limited. Use the same imaging method for serial measurements when possible (p527, p539–540).

The book describes observation of selected asymptomatic simple cysts **under 10 cm** with a normal CA-125 and repeat evaluation in **3–6 months**. Complexity, an elevated marker or size at least 10 cm warrants urgent gynecologic consultation in its pathway. A normal marker alone is not sufficient reassurance when the morphology or clinical presentation is concerning. If malignancy is suspected, the initial surgical team matters: complete staging and appropriate debulking at the first operation can avoid an incomplete procedure and subsequent operation. The decision must also take account of operative fitness, symptoms and the patient's goals (p539–540).

> Current practice (not the book): RCOG distinguishes small, asymptomatic simple lesions from larger or complex findings. A postmenopausal unilateral, unilocular simple cyst no larger than 3 cm needs no routine follow-up. Do not apply the book's surveillance approach for a cyst under 10 cm without considering size, morphology, symptoms and current referral guidance. - [RCOG Green-top Guideline 34, 2025 amendment](https://www.rcog.org.uk/guidance/browse-all-guidance/green-top-guidelines/ovarian-cysts-in-postmenopausal-women-green-top-guideline-no-34/).

### Identify which pelvic compartment is causing the symptom

A urethral caruncle usually protrudes from the posterior meatus at the six-o'clock position. A urethral diverticulum may instead cause the combination of dysuria, dyspareunia and postvoid dribbling, sometimes with recurrent infection. Bladder pain or frequency without a convincing infection requires consideration of other causes, including painful bladder syndrome. The book finds no evidence for routine urethral dilation to treat recurrent UTI or voiding dysfunction in most women. Evaluate emptying and pelvic support before attributing every episode of urgency or discomfort to infection (p540–542).

Figure 36-4 shows descent of the anterior vaginal wall with the bladder. As a cystocele enlarges, angulation at the bladder outlet can produce incomplete emptying, hesitancy or retention. Figure 36-5 shows rectum bulging into the posterior wall; ask about obstructed defecation and manual splinting. In Figure 36-6 the bulging compartment contains small bowel, an enterocele. Several defects can coexist. A patient describing a bothersome lump late in the day may not reproduce its maximum size while resting supine. Ask about variation with strain and about the impact on walking, voiding, bowel movements and intercourse (p541–544).

Table 36-6 combines symptom burden, anatomical examination and consequences. Examine the leading edge with maximal Valsalva or cough, note its relationship to the hymen, inspect for abrasions and ulceration, and assess postvoid residual urine and urinalysis when indicated. Figure 36-7 maps standardized POP-Q landmarks: Aa and Ba on the anterior wall, Ap and Bp posteriorly, C at the cervix or cuff and D at the posterior fornix when present. Genital hiatus, perineal body and total vaginal length are separate measurements. Recording the compartment and extent makes serial observations and surgical referrals more informative than documenting a “bulge” alone (p542–545).

Uncomplicated prolapse with little bother can be observed. Nonhealing ulceration, bleeding, retention with recurrent infection or hydronephrosis prompts referral. Pelvic-floor exercises depend on the person's ability to identify and repeatedly contract the correct muscles. A pessary can restore support without an operation, but fit, comfort, ability to void and epithelial health require reassessment. Some users remove and clean it independently. Limited mobility or cognition can require clinician-assisted care and return within **1–3 months**. An erosion or new bleeding requires examination rather than waiting automatically for the planned visit (p544–545).

Reconstructive procedures restore the supported vaginal canal and can use vaginal, abdominal or minimally invasive approaches. Colpocleisis instead reduces the prolapse and closes the canal; its implications for future vaginal intercourse should be discussed before surgery. The choice depends on goals, operative risk, support after the procedure and whether anatomical restoration or relief of the presenting symptom is the priority. After repair, address constipation and straining to limit stress on the repair. Successful anatomical correction does not by itself guarantee resolution of all urinary or bowel symptoms (p542–545).

## Exam traps

- A negative screening history does not replace biopsy of a visibly abnormal cervix or assessment of postmenopausal bleeding (p536–539).
- A low-dose local estradiol ring and a systemic vaginal estrogen ring have different exposure profiles (p536, Table 36-3).
- A caruncle, hematuria or rectal bleeding can mimic vaginal staining; identifying one possible source does not exclude a concurrent endometrial cause (p538–539).
- Rectocele and enterocele may both produce posterior bulging; only the latter contains small intestine (p544).

- A thin endometrium on an earlier scan does not settle new or recurrent bleeding (p539).
- A pelvic mass in a symptomatic patient requires evaluation; screening an asymptomatic, average-risk woman with CA-125 is a different question (p527, p539).
- Systemic hormone therapy and low-dose vaginal estrogen have different indications, exposures and risk considerations (p534–535, p540).

## Recall drill

**1. What should a gynecologic history establish after a previous operation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The indication, the organs removed or retained, and whether symptoms such as pain, prolapse or incontinence resolved (p527–528).</details>

**2. What else can cause pelvic pain when examination of the reproductive organs is unrevealing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Consider gastrointestinal, urinary and musculoskeletal sources and use directed examination to localize tenderness (p530–531).</details>

**3. Which vulvar findings suggest lichen sclerosus?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Porcelain-white plaques, pruritus, bruising, fissures and possible figure-of-eight involvement or architectural loss (p532).</details>

**4. Why examine a persistently itchy area that has been treated as atrophy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A dermatosis or neoplastic lesion can be overlooked. Reassess the vulva and consider biopsy of a suspicious lesion (p531–534).</details>

**5. What symptom cluster suggests genitourinary syndrome of menopause?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dryness, irritation, dyspareunia and lower urinary tract symptoms in the setting of hypoestrogenism (p534–535).</details>

**6. What vaginal estradiol tablet regimen does the book give?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary><strong>10 micrograms nightly for 2 weeks</strong>, followed by twice-weekly maintenance (p534).</details>

**7. Is new vaginal discharge automatically an infection?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Atrophy, a pessary or estrogen-related epithelial turnover can contribute; inspect and assess the character of the discharge (p533–534).</details>

**8. What determines whether cervical screening can stop?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Prior adequate negative screening and individual risk history, including previous abnormalities; chronological age alone is insufficient (p536–537).</details>

**9. Why is an endometrial thickness result insufficient in recurrent bleeding?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A low thickness reduces risk but does not exclude important disease when symptoms persist or return. Obtain tissue assessment (p539).</details>

**10. Why distinguish ovarian cancer screening from evaluating a suspicious mass?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ultrasound and CA-125 screening have not shown benefit in low-risk asymptomatic women; symptomatic or suspicious findings warrant diagnostic investigation (p527, p539).</details>

**11. What is the initial imaging modality to characterize an adnexal mass?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Transvaginal ultrasonography helps characterize complexity and features suggesting malignancy (p539).</details>

**12. What distinction does the book make about systemic hormone use after 65?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It describes a new start as contraindicated and discusses individualized continuation of therapy begun earlier. See the separate current-practice qualification above (p540).</details>

**13. When may prolapse be observed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When symptoms are minimal and complications such as ulceration or urinary obstruction are absent, in accordance with the patient's goals (p544).</details>

**14. Which complications of prolapse prompt specialty referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Persistent ulceration, bleeding, retention associated with recurrent infection, or hydronephrosis (p544).</details>

**15. Who may need closer follow-up after pessary fitting?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Patients with limited mobility, impaired cognition or difficulty with self-care; the chapter suggests reassessment within <strong>1–3 months</strong> for some (p545).</details>
