# Chapter 70 Study: Palliative Care Across Care Settings

## Summary

**Purpose & timing**
- Palliative care seeks quality of life through symptom relief, attention to psychological/spiritual distress and support for patients and families (p1077–1078).
- It can begin at diagnosis of serious or advanced illness, or with progressive frailty and cumulative disease burden; it is not limited to hospice (p1079, p1086).
- Geriatrics and palliative care share attention to multimorbidity, dementia, disability, caregivers and the person's community context (p1078).

**Assessment & care plan**
- Clarify values and preferences through advance care planning; address pain, nonpain symptoms, function, cognition and caregiver needs (p1078).
- Reassess needs as function and care setting change. An interprofessional team and consistent communication can support continuity (p1078–1079).
- Practical support includes adaptive devices, home modifications, caregiver education and access to medication and community resources (p1078, p1084).

**Across settings**
- Inpatient teams provide symptom expertise, prognostic support, family communication and links to community services (p1080). For a patient with a life-limiting condition, Figure 70-3 lists **more than one admission for the same reason in recent months**, a complex home situation or care transition, and failure to thrive as day-1 triggers. On subsequent days, it lists **ICU stay over seven days**, clarification of goals or resuscitation preferences, and help with prognosis. Difficult symptoms and a negative response to the 12-month surprise question apply at either stage (p1082).
- Outpatient clinics permit earlier relationships and follow-up after inpatient consultation; access still depends on mobility and available assistance (p1083).
- Home-based care can reach seriously ill patients who do not qualify for hospice. Completely homebound patients have approximately **40%** mortality over **2 years** in cited data (p1084).
- An ideal home model integrates primary and palliative care, respite, spiritual support and **24/7** clinician access for crises (p1084).
- Long-term-care barriers include prognostic uncertainty in noncancer illness, frequent transitions, staff turnover and reimbursement structures (p1085).

### Applying the inpatient consultation figure

May 2024 Q84 presents a patient with life-limiting illness on the day of admission. Of the choices, repeat admission for the same problem a month earlier directly matches the recurrent-admission box. Pain requiring opioids and delirium also received credit. The figure's actual symptom criterion is difficulty controlling physical or psychological symptoms: ask how much distress remains and what the primary team has already tried. Chronological age by itself is not one of the figure's criteria (p1082).

Look beyond symptoms on the first day. An unsustainable home situation or a difficult transition may be the reason specialist input is needed. The figure uses functional decline, anorexia and weight loss as signs of failure to thrive. On later hospital days, a prolonged ICU stay, uncertainty about resuscitation or need for prognostic discussion can signal a different reason for consultation. Ask the surprise question at either time (p1082).

### Choosing a service that the patient can continue to reach

A clinic can see a patient receiving disease-directed treatment and build a relationship before a crisis. If functional decline makes travel impossible, a referral to that same clinic may leave the patient without care. A bridge program can link the hospital or clinic to a home or facility team. Share the patient's goals, current symptom plan, caregiver concerns and which clinician will respond to deterioration (p1082–1084).

Home visits can reveal whether medicines, equipment and caregiver help are actually available. The ideal described includes after-hours crisis support and respite. Studies of home programs reported better symptom or utilization outcomes, including fewer emergency visits and hospital admissions; overall survival did not differ in the comparison discussed. Local staff and payment arrangements affect which services are available (p1084).

**Hospice & exam traps**
- Hospice is a holistic philosophy addressing medical, emotional, spiritual and family needs in life-limiting illness (p1085).
- Most older adults' late-life care occurs in the community, while specialist palliative access remains concentrated in hospitals and hospice (p1079).
- A hospital consultation alone does not create longitudinal care; transitions need shared goals, follow-up and continued caregiver support (p1078–1079, p1086).
- Uncertain prognosis and ongoing restorative treatment do not erase palliative needs; the chapter advocates integrating care earlier across the trajectory (p1084–1086).

### Recognize the need before a terminal diagnosis is certain

The opening case follows an older adult with heart failure, impaired cognition, arthritis and falls who gradually loses access to ordinary care. Missed clinic visits look like a problem of adherence until the reader learns about the walk-up apartment, unreliable use of a walker and a daughter with competing work and family responsibilities. The patient has pain, weight loss, low mood and increasing isolation long before anyone can confidently predict when she will die. This trajectory is an indication to assess palliative and geriatric needs now. Waiting for a single organ diagnosis to become “terminal” would leave important, tractable problems unattended (p1077–1079).

After a fall causes a hip fracture, surgery and rehabilitation address a real functional goal. Delirium, pain and a more apparent cognitive impairment reveal other needs. A hospital or rehabilitation team can contribute by treating symptoms, explaining what recovery might look like, asking which living arrangement the patient values and assessing whether her daughter can sustain the proposed assistance. The later improvement after moving into a supportive home environment is a reminder that frailty does not make every loss irreversible. It also does not remove the need to prepare for recurrent decline (p1077–1078).

Figure 70-1 shows why both specialties belong in this case. Geriatrics brings attention to gait, frailty, safety, cognition and transitions. Palliative care brings focused assessment of suffering, serious-illness conversations, spiritual support and bereavement care. Functional outcomes, medication management, advance care planning and decisional capacity sit in the overlap. In practice the clinician should ask who can address each unmet need locally. The patient need not be sent through two entirely separate assessments simply because a symptom or decision falls within both fields (p1078).

A goals discussion is useful before the patient faces an emergency decision. Ask what a good day currently looks like, what losses would be especially difficult, who should help make decisions, and how much burden the patient would accept to regain a valued ability. Record the account in terms that the next team can use. “Wants to get better” does not distinguish the wish to walk to the table from willingness to undergo prolonged invasive treatment. Ask again when illness or function changes: a preference formed before living with a disability may change after the patient experiences it (p1078–1079, p1082).

### The care continuum and the gap between specialist visits

The ideal continuum follows need across the home, clinic, hospital and residential facility. It can combine symptom relief with disease-directed treatment and rehabilitation, then increase support for daily care if recovery is incomplete. The benefit is continuity of knowledge as well as continuity of personnel. A change of setting should not erase the patient's previously described priorities, the treatment that relieved a distressing symptom, or the family member who understands the person's usual behavior. If the same team cannot follow the patient, someone must transfer that information and arrange the next contact (p1078–1080).

Figure 70-2 explains why availability of excellent services at either end of an illness is insufficient. Specialty care is often concentrated in hospital and hospice, although much of the patient's last months and years is spent in the community. A person may no longer be able to attend an outpatient clinic, be too stable to need admission and have an uncertain prognosis that precludes hospice enrollment. The resulting gap is practical, not merely diagnostic: nobody may be available to adjust symptom treatment in the home or help the family decide how to respond to a new crisis (p1079–1080).

Frailty complicates prognostication. A cluster of accumulating losses may be highly informative about vulnerability without yielding a precise survival estimate. An older patient can experience repeated acute illnesses, partially recover each time and still have progressively less function between episodes. Track what has changed since the last illness: walking distance, personal care, weight, cognition, support needs and time spent at home. These observations justify a palliative approach and can inform discussion of a proposed intervention even when a particular life-expectancy threshold has not been met (p1079–1080).

Medication management is one example of a decision that spans the continuum. Repeated transitions can add new prescriptions without reconsidering older preventive agents or treatments a patient no longer values. Discuss the expected benefit, time to benefit, burden, adverse effects and what the individual is trying to achieve. Deprescribing is an individualized part of that discussion. A decision to discontinue a burdensome medicine does not imply that all disease-directed treatment, rehabilitation or symptom treatment should stop. Make the change and its rationale visible to the next prescriber (p1078, p1080).

### What the inpatient consultation adds

A hospital team may control an acute infection or fracture while still struggling with pain, breathlessness, agitation, uncertainty about prognosis or disagreement about discharge. The specialist consultation can focus on the unresolved problem and on communication among clinicians, the patient and the family. It can also help identify a service that will actually be able to continue care after discharge. The consultation works alongside the treating team. Calling specialists does not transfer the primary team's responsibility to treat reversible illness, explain decisions or act on the plan (p1080–1082).

The admission-day panel in Figure 70-3 starts with life-threatening or life-limiting illness. The question then is whether there are additional needs: difficult physical or psychological symptoms, repeated admissions for the same problem in recent months, an especially complex home situation or transition, or failure to thrive manifested by decline in function, anorexia or weight loss. A negative answer to the **12-month** surprise question also prompts consideration. These are consultation triggers. No single trigger establishes imminent death or prescribes a particular ceiling of treatment (p1082).

Use the exam alternatives to test the difference. A recent admission for the same problem directly signals recurrence. Delirium can cause profound distress and interfere with communication or safe discharge; pain can remain uncontrolled despite opioids. Both scenarios may justify specialist help, and the item accepted them. To apply the figure clinically, ask what remains difficult to control and how the patient and team are coping. Opioid use alone describes a treatment; it does not describe its effectiveness. Age alone describes neither symptom burden nor the complexity of the decision (p1082).

On later hospital days, the figure prompts a different review. In a prolonged ICU course of **more than 7 days**, the immediate lifesaving question may have given way to uncertainty about the likelihood and quality of recovery. The primary team may need help clarifying prognosis, goals or resuscitation preferences. Refractory symptoms and the surprise question remain relevant. The timing distinction matters in an exam: a later-course example cannot be used as a literal observation on the day a new patient arrives, even though another admission-day trigger may already be present (p1082).

Prepare for a family meeting by clarifying what each clinician has told the patient and how much the patient and family wish to know. A relative may understand “rehabilitation” to mean expected return to the old baseline, while clinicians use it to mean a trial of therapy with uncertain benefit. Identify that discrepancy before the next transfer. A useful discussion leaves the recipient with a shared description of current function, achievable aims, the uncertainty that remains and the planned response if recovery does not proceed as hoped (p1081–1083).

### Postacute rehabilitation is a time to assess the trajectory

The period after hospital discharge is particularly vulnerable to discontinuity. The acute team may have addressed immediate suffering without arranging follow-up. Rehabilitation clinicians may first see a patient on a good or bad day after delirium and have little sense of the previous baseline. The family may expect that a transfer to a skilled facility means the difficult decisions are finished. Instead, the receiving team should determine what can improve with therapy, what symptom is preventing participation and what help would be required to live outside the facility (p1082–1083).

For example, a person whose main goal is getting home may need better pain relief to practice transfers, along with repeated explanation of instructions, help obtaining equipment and training for the person who will provide assistance. It is clinically useful to ask whether the analgesic improves participation or makes the patient too sleepy for therapy. The palliative question here is how to relieve suffering while protecting a restorative goal. The restorative question is how much gain is achievable and whether that gain would change the living arrangement (p1077–1079, p1082–1083).

If the expected improvement does not occur, reconvene with the person and family. What has changed in the medical condition, the willingness to continue intensive rehabilitation, the capacity of the caregiver and the likely burden of future transfers? One option may be ongoing therapy aimed at maintaining a specific ability; another may be greater emphasis on comfort and support in a different setting. A bridge service can carry forward these decisions and reassess them in the new environment rather than treating each placement as a fresh start (p1082–1084).

### Outpatient clinics and home-based programs

A clinic makes possible an ongoing relationship before a serious crisis occurs. The patient can bring concerns that are easy to ignore during a short disease-focused appointment: distressing symptoms between treatments, fears about losing independence, caregiver exhaustion or uncertainty about what to expect. When palliative and disease-specialty services communicate, the same clinician need not reconstruct the entire treatment history at every visit. Clinic follow-up after an inpatient consultation also allows the plan to be adjusted in light of how the patient actually functions outside hospital (p1082–1083).

Figure 70-4 distinguishes community models by where and how the service reaches the patient. Consider an ambulatory patient who can attend specialty visits with a relative, a patient in a facility receiving skilled therapy, and someone confined to the house. A referral to the same building does not give all three equal access. Before choosing a model, ask whether transport and a companion are available, how a plan will be implemented in the home and whether there is anyone to call after the clinic closes. A clinic that works well during daytime appointments may still leave an evening crisis uncovered (p1083–1084).

Home-based palliative care can reach patients with advanced illness who do not meet hospice eligibility or who continue treatments inconsistent with a hospice election. This describes the high needs of a population; it cannot be substituted for an individual estimate of survival. Assess not only symptoms but also how the patient obtains food, medication and equipment, whether stairs or the bathroom create a barrier, and who can safely assist when the usual caregiver is absent (p1084).

A visit can make mismatches visible. The medication list may assume a complex dosing schedule that a person with memory impairment cannot carry out. A walker that was appropriate on a wide unit may be difficult to maneuver around furniture. A daughter prepared to help in the evening may be unavailable during work hours. The plan can combine simplification of medicines, assistive devices, environmental adaptations, caregiver coaching and community services. The value lies in addressing the actual barrier to comfort or function, rather than merely recording that the patient has the necessary prescription or equipment (p1077–1079, p1084).

The chapter's ideal home service includes coordinated primary and palliative expertise, spiritual support, respite and access to a clinician during crises. The program evaluations reported improved scores for depression, dyspnea and anxiety, fewer emergency visits or admissions, lower overall costs, more completed advance directives, a greater likelihood of dying at home and more hospice referrals. In the comparison described, less hospital use was observed without a difference in overall survival. Service organization and availability varied. The outcome to discuss with a family is whether this program can provide the help they need, not a promise that a home visit will prevent every admission (p1084).

When home care precedes hospice, the home team may already know the patient's priorities and who can provide overnight help. Pass that information to the hospice team at enrollment so the first visit can address the family's immediate concerns (p1084–1085).

### Long-term care and hospice: distinct services that can overlap

Long-term-care residents can have persistent pain, depression, existential distress or difficulty adapting to a permanent change in residence. Advanced cognitive impairment may make a direct account of symptoms less reliable. Involve staff who know the resident and family who can describe prior preferences, while continuing to include the person as far as possible. A familiar aide may notice a pattern of distress with turning that is entirely absent during a brief physician visit. Communication between staff and the palliative clinician is part of symptom assessment itself (p1078, p1085).

A facility may have limited specialist access outside hospice. Other barriers include uncertain trajectories in noncancer illness, staff turnover, payment arrangements and repeated transfers across services. A care plan that lives only in the memory of a nurse or in a consultant's hospital note will be particularly vulnerable when a crisis happens overnight. Arrange for the facility team to know the agreed approach to symptoms, the circumstances in which hospital evaluation could help and the person who should participate in decisions. The goal is appropriate transfer when it fits the need, not a blanket rule to keep every resident in place (p1084–1085).

The chapter describes an association between earlier consultation and fewer burdensome transitions among long-term-care residents. In the study, earlier involvement was associated with an **almost 50% reduction** in outcomes such as an emergency visit within **30 days** of death or hospice admission within **3 days** of death. These are measures of service use near death, not proof that an emergency visit or short hospice stay was inappropriate for every individual. Interpret the outcome with what the resident wanted, whether symptoms could be treated locally and how much warning the team had (p1085).

Hospice adds a philosophy of holistic care and a defined service model. In the US Medicare framework the chapter gives a prognosis of **6 months or less** for hospice eligibility. Many palliative needs arise much earlier, and palliative intervention need not await that prognosis. Ask what support a particular hospice can provide, where the patient hopes to receive it and what the caregiver would have to do. Families may assume that enrollment ensures continuous bedside professional presence, but organizations vary in staffing, intensity and available services (p1085–1086).

Late referral limits the time available for symptom management, preparation and caregiver support. The chapter reports a **median hospice length of service of 18 days** and describes particularly short enrollments near death. A patient who would have valued home support may therefore remain dependent on repeated acute-care visits until very late. Revisit hospice when prognosis and preferences fit, but keep addressing symptoms and function while eligibility or readiness is uncertain. In the opening trajectory, home support, rehabilitation, long-term care and hospice each answer a different need at a different time (p1077–1078, p1084–1086).

## Exam traps

- Difficult symptoms can prompt a palliative consultation on admission. An opioid prescription alone does not establish that symptoms are difficult to manage (p1082).
- A person receiving restorative care or lacking a clear hospice prognosis can still benefit from palliative care at home (p1083–1085).
