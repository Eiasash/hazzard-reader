# Emergency Department Care

A quiet emergency patient may have delirium, abuse or functional decline despite a reassuring triage impression. Establish baseline function before deciding on discharge.

## The geriatric emergency setting

<a id="p207"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p207">p. 207</a>]**

Rapid evaluation of an isolated acute complaint is insufficient for many older adults. Geriatric emergency care coordinates emergency medicine, geriatrics, therapy, nutrition, inpatient teams, primary care, and community services. Accreditation and shared learning networks support measurable process improvement (<a href="?chapter=15#p207">p207</a>).

> **Current practice: GED Guidelines 2.0 development.** Geriatric ED Guidelines 2.0 evaluates individual clinical recommendations using GRADE. The 2014 consensus document remains a useful description of geriatric emergency-department services. [GED Guidelines 2.0 development, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12476112/).

<a id="p208"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p208">p. 208</a>]**

A geriatric-friendly room alone is insufficient: staffing, continuing education, syndrome-specific protocols, and local resources matter more. A general hospital policy becomes geriatric ED work when adapted to relevant geriatric processes and outcomes. Medication-safety work benefits from pharmacy expertise (<a href="?chapter=15#p208">p208</a>).

## Delirium and cognitive screening

### A quiet patient after a fracture

A patient who was managing independently yesterday and is now quiet, distractible and slow to respond may have hypoactive delirium. Ask someone who knows the patient about the time of onset and fluctuations during the day. Hypoactive delirium is more common than hyperactive delirium in older ED patients, yet is recognized less often. Delirium is associated with longer admission, cognitive and functional decline, and depression after hospitalization. [Delirium, p. 209](?chapter=15#exam-p209-mcq-c040b7a76725684cad927088).

<a id="p209"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p209">p. 209</a>]**

Delirium signals an acute physiological insult. Clinician judgment alone is inadequate for identification. The chapter favors the balance of brevity, accuracy, and ED acceptability of bCAM, RASS, and the 4AT; identifying the precipitating illness remains essential (<a href="?chapter=15#p209">p209</a>).

Environmental modifications: soundproof curtains reduce ambient noise; hearing assistance improves communication; reclining/padded seating improves comfort and limits pressure injury; clocks, signs, and staff-name boards support orientation; nonslip floors, rails, lighting, and commodes address falls; diurnal light supports sleep. Protocols address delirium, dementia/caregiver capacity, abuse, falls, frailty, medication safety, and vulnerability (<a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-1">Tables 15-1</a>/<a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-2">15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p209">p209</a>).

Separate baseline vulnerability from the immediate insult. Dementia, sensory impairment and limited mobility can lower the threshold at which an infection, hypoxia, a medication change or pain precipitates delirium. <a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-3">Table 15-3</a> lists male sex among the baseline factors. Identify and treat a plausible trigger, and pass the time course and observed attention deficit to the receiving team. [Risk factors, p. 210](?chapter=15#exam-p210-mcq-c040b7a76725684cad927088).

**Predisposing vulnerabilities:** advanced age, educational attainment and male sex; dementia, depression, renal insufficiency, cirrhosis or terminal illness; restricted ADLs/immobility; substance/alcohol misuse, polypharmacy or psychoactive drugs; impaired hearing/vision; dehydration or malnutrition (<a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-3">Table 15-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p210">p210</a>).

**Precipitants:** infection, undertreated pain and temperature disturbance; stroke, intracranial bleeding, CNS infection, seizure or mass; medication changes, drug use or withdrawal; sodium/calcium or thyroid disorders and acute hepatic/renal failure; acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypertensive encephalopathy or shock; serotonergic, paraneoplastic or anti-NMDA syndromes (<a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-3">Table 15-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p210">p210</a>).

<a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-3">Table 15-3</a> — all delirium vulnerabilities and precipitants

In the hip-fracture case, age and place are already wrong, giving **2** AMT4 points. The patient starts months backward but then moves forward, giving **1** attention point. To interpret sleepiness, determine whether it clears in **less than 10 seconds after waking**, according to the 4AT (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>). To score acute change, ask about the past two weeks and whether the change was still apparent in the last 24 hours. A subtotal of three cannot settle the case. A positive UB-2 begins a second-stage CAM interview. Clinical CAM requires **acute onset and fluctuating course** (Ch 58 <a href="?chapter=58#p880">p880</a>) and inattention plus disorganized thinking or altered consciousness. [4AT scoring, p. 211](?chapter=15#exam-p211-mcq-8c8695c6e0e9292f086c2501) · [Table 58-3, p. 884](?chapter=58#exam-mcq-8c8695c6e0e9292f086c2501).

bCAM probes acute/fluctuating mental status, attention, level of consciousness and disorganized thinking. The diagram uses backward spelling/months, simple reasoning questions and a hand command (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p210">p210</a>). The AMT4 items tested by the official question citing this page are printed on the next page: [p211](#p211).

Treat this as the figure's screening shorthand, not a stand-alone diagnosis; the book's delirium chapter requires assessment of attention, acuity and cognitive change (Chapter 58, <a href="?chapter=58#p879">pp879</a>–<a href="?chapter=58#p880">880</a>). The abbreviated panel shows only part of the RASS scale.

### What AMT4 measures in each instrument

Ask for age, birth date, the present hospital or building, and the current year. Asking someone to state their name and address can help assess alertness in the 4AT, but neither answer contributes to AMT4. <a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a> shows the bCAM and the 4AT on consecutive pages. Its 4AT continuation contains the orientation questions. [Cited figure start, p. 210](?chapter=15#exam-p210-mcq-e3a98b6ee3b58c73a12e6ed4) · [AMT4 panel, p. 211](?chapter=15#exam-p211-mcq-e3a98b6ee3b58c73a12e6ed4).

<a id="p210"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p210">p. 210</a>]**

All scoring and time windows above: <a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>. A single introductory prompt asking the month before December is permitted. Asking name/address assists **alertness** assessment; **name is not an AMT4 item** (<a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>).

The figure's abbreviated **RASS** panel prints: **+3** very agitated, removing tubes/catheters or aggressive; **+2** frequent nonpurposeful movement/fighting ventilator; **+1** restless/anxious without vigorous aggression; **0** alert/calm; **−1** drowsy with voice contact >**10 seconds**; **−2** briefly awake with voice eye contact <**10 seconds**; **−3** movement/eye opening to voice without eye contact; **−4** movement/eye opening only to physical stimulation; **−5** no response to either. It gives **30–60 seconds** for administration (<a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>).

Within **4AT**, no AMT4 errors earns zero, one earns one, and two or more errors or inability to test earns two. Inattention, arousal and acute change are assessed separately because longstanding cognitive impairment can also cause errors. Four or more total points raises concern for delirium with or without underlying cognitive impairment. [Figure 15-2, p. 211](?chapter=15#exam-p211-mcq-be0342d1614ce894a9a67d87).

<a id="p211"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p211">p. 211</a>]**

Treat the precipitant and communicate with inpatient teams. Discharging a patient with untreated delirium invites return visits. Implementation, differing tools across services and limited ED intervention evidence impede improvement (<a href="?chapter=15#p211">pp211</a>–<a href="?chapter=15#p212">212</a>).

Within **ED dementia screening**, even one AMT4 error flags higher risk. A well-educated patient may still answer all four questions correctly despite a change in daily functioning. Ask someone who knows the patient whether tasks such as paying bills or keeping appointments have become more difficult. This is why the caregiver AD8 can add information when a bedside test seems reassuring. [Figure 15-3, p. 212; accompanying discussion p213](?chapter=15#exam-p212-mcq-be0342d1614ce894a9a67d87).

<a id="p212"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p212">p. 212</a>]**

After delirium is excluded, approximately **31%** of ED patients over **65** still have concern for cognitive dysfunction. This is associated with longer stays, falls, admissions, returns/readmissions and dissatisfaction. The chapter stresses that proof of ED delirium-prevention/treatment benefit remains limited, even for plausible orientation and lighting strategies (<a href="?chapter=15#p212">p212</a>).

**Caregiver AD8:** a reliable informant identifies change over the preceding years in judgment, hobbies/interest, repetitive statements, learning appliances/tools, remembering month/year, complex finances, appointments and consistent thinking/memory problems. Each affirmative response scores **1**; **≥2** indicates high dementia risk (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-3">Figure 15-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p212">p212</a>).

**AMT4:** age, birthday, place and year. **Any error** indicates high dementia risk in this dementia-screening use. This differs from the graded AMT4 subscore within the 4AT (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-3">Figure 15-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p212">p212</a>; <a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>).

**Ottawa 3DY:** day, date, spelling “world” backward and year. Any incorrect item is screen-positive in the printed figure. The label describes the instrument; it does not mean there are only three questions (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-3">Figure 15-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p212">p212</a>).

<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-3">Figure 15-3</a> — complete ED dementia screening instruments

Informant cAD8 can be used when the patient is unavailable or uncooperative and assesses **change from baseline**. It avoids the education-related false negatives seen with some performance-based instruments. These tests **do not diagnose dementia**: reported positive likelihood ratios are mostly **1–4**, negative ratios **0.17–0.39**, against imperfect reference standards (<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p213</a>).

**Elder abuse:** physical/psychological harm, financial exploitation and neglect. Up to **10%** of older US adults experience abuse annually; as few as **1 in 14** cases are reported. Fear of retaliation can suppress disclosure. The chapter describes Senior Aid as sensitive and reasonably specific, while still requiring external validation, feasibility and outcome-impact assessment (<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p213</a>).

**Falls:** among community-dwelling adults over **65**, about **one-third** fall yearly, increasing to **50%** after **80**. Following an initial fall, **36–50%** experience recurrent fall, ED revisit or death within **1 year**. These are a composite adverse outcome, not a recurrent-fall rate alone. Risk is multifactorial, and no widely accepted post-ED risk instrument exists (<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p213</a>).

Before deciding on discharge, ask whether the patient can understand the plan now, recall it later and obtain practical help with new medicines or follow-up. Brief instruments identify patients who may need this extra support, but do not by themselves diagnose dementia or determine admission. [Interpreting a cognitive screen, p. 213](?chapter=15#exam-p213-mcq-be0342d1614ce894a9a67d87).

<a id="p213"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p. 213</a>]**

Before discharge assess whether the patient can understand and remember instructions, medication changes and follow-up, and whether the setting is safe. Suspected dementia does not automatically justify admission. Communicate limitations and adapt transitions; screening alone has no established direct therapeutic response or demonstrated ability to select those benefiting from admission (<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p213</a>).

Evaluation and protection require coordinated social work, nursing, physicians and law enforcement; some centers use vulnerable-elder protection teams. Simplified documentation, injury photographs and coordinated radiology communication support evaluation (<a href="?chapter=15#p213">p213</a>).

Within **Senior Aid**, orientation helps judge how much to rely on the patient's account. Ask about assistance, unmet needs, intimidation, coercion involving money and physical harm. When reliability or suspicion remains uncertain, look for concerning injuries and signs of neglect. The decision integrates the conversation, presenting complaint and examination. None of the AMT4 questions assesses mood or suicidal intent. [Figure 15-4, p. 214](?chapter=15#exam-p214-mcq-be0342d1614ce894a9a67d87).

## Safeguarding and falls

A person may fear retaliation or depend on the individual who accompanied them. A private conversation and observations from nursing, social work and medical staff can reveal discrepancies or unmet needs. Dehydration and poorly controlled illness merit investigation. They are possible signs of neglect, not proof of who caused it. Contact with a guardian does not resolve the medical admission need or the safeguarding concern. [Hazzard, p. 213](?chapter=15#exam-p213-mcq-632ba530eb4611e16d60adfe) · [הגנה ושחרור בישראל](?chapter=15#ch15-il-protection).

<a id="p214"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p214">p. 214</a>]**

Begin with **AMT4 cognitive assessment**, ask abuse questions, then judge whether the patient can reliably report abuse. If confident, integrate the answers, chief complaint and observations; if uncertain or not confident, perform the physical assessment before forming suspicion (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-4">Figure 15-4</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p214">p214</a>).

Abuse questions cover the preceding **6 months**: need for bathing/dressing/shopping/banking/meal help and whether needed assistance is reliably available; insulting or belittling language; being told one causes too much trouble; threats or emotional harm; coercion to sign papers or misuse money; and attempted physical harm (<a href="?chapter=15#p214">p214</a>).

Findings **highly suggestive** of abuse: unusual/multiple/large bruises, intentional-pattern burns, patterned injuries, suspicious abrasions/lacerations and neglect. Findings that **may suggest** abuse: dehydration, poorly controlled illness, malnutrition and tender/swollen areas. Specific circumstances: genital trauma/infection suggesting sexual abuse, concerning fractures, and an unusual delay in seeking help (<a href="?chapter=15#p214">p214</a>).

**Distinguish the screening instruments.** The same AMT4 orientation questions appear within **4AT delirium screening**, **ED dementia screening**, and **Senior Aid abuse screening**; this chapter does not place AMT4 in an inpatient depression/suicide screen (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figures 15-2</a>/<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-3">15-3</a>/<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-4">15-4</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p211">pp211</a>–<a href="?chapter=15#p214">214</a>).

Fall interventions show mixed results: ED medication deprescribing alone did not reduce falls at **1 year**, whereas a brief individualized PT/pharmacy intervention reduced overall and fall-related returns at **6 months**. Local champions, coordination and accessible follow-up matter. Existing vulnerability instruments do not accurately identify both high- and low-risk subsets for the broad short-term outcomes under discussion (<a href="?chapter=15#p214">p214</a>).

## Functional vulnerability

**Function after discharge.** Establish earlier ability to transfer, dress, take medicines and obtain food. Then ask what changed with the acute episode and who can help tonight. Someone already receiving paid help may still lose additional function. Someone living alone may have reliable nearby support. The chapter compares ED vulnerability tools against several distinct outcomes, including return visits, institutionalization and functional loss. Most do not separate high- and low-risk groups well enough to settle disposition. The Clinical Frailty Scale shows more promise for 30-day mortality than for predicting preventable loss of function. [Current-edition discussion, pp. 214, 217](?chapter=15#ch15-vulnerability).

Frailty denotes vulnerability to incomplete restoration of homeostasis after a stressor. The Clinical Frailty Scale appears feasible and useful for **30-day mortality** prediction, but prediction of returns/functional decline and benefit from acting on its result remain unestablished here. <a href="?chapter=15#book-table-15-4">Table 15-4</a> compares Pro-Age, CFS, APOP, interRAI, ISAR, Rowland, Runciman, Silver Code, TRST and Variables Indicative of Placement; their outcomes and accuracy vary (<a href="?chapter=15#p217">p217</a>).

**Prevent the next fall.** Once the injury is treated, look at gait, vision, standing blood pressure, medicines, footwear and the home environment. A universal medication reduction strategy did not reduce falls at a year in the ED trial, while a different, brief individualized PT and pharmacy intervention reduced ED revisits at six months. The outcomes and follow-up intervals differ. [ED fall prevention, pp. 213–216](?chapter=15#ch15-falls).

<a id="p216"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p216">p. 216</a>]**

Fall prevention links emergency and inpatient care, PT/pharmacy, community programs, home modification and shared decision-making. Implementation must be assessed in real-world settings (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-6">Figure 15-6</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p216">p216</a>).

**Who can act on the findings?** Match a geriatric protocol to a person who can deliver it. EQUiPPED uses prescriber education, individual feedback and electronic decision support to improve medication safety. Observation permits reassessment of treatment response and time to obtain therapy, consultation or discharge support. These approaches can be delivered without building a separate geriatric ED. [EQUiPPED, p. 218](?chapter=15#ch15-equipped) · [Observation, p. 218](?chapter=15#ch15-observation).

<a id="p215"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p215">p. 215</a>]**

Distinguish factors causing a fall from impaired protective responses and factors worsening injury. Examples: balance/vision/mobility deficits, sedating drugs and environmental hazards cause falls; poor reflexes, proprioception and core strength impair recovery; osteoporosis, cervical degeneration, cerebral atrophy, antithrombotics, reduced muscle/padding and fragile skin increase injury or the consequences of prolonged time on the floor (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-5">Figure 15-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p215">p215</a>).

## Medication safety and follow-up

<a id="p217"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p217">p. 217</a>]**

Beers criteria inform ED medication safety, but the chapter cautions that brief ED prescriptions can have a different, relatively untested risk–benefit profile from chronic prescribing (<a href="?chapter=15#p217">p217</a>).

<a id="p218"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p218">p. 218</a>]**

EQUiPPED combines clinician education, individual feedback and electronic decision support; potentially inappropriate prescribing fell sustainably at **1 year** across **four** VA hospitals. Consider adverse drug effects/interactions and hypoglycemic/antihypertensive overtreatment; start appropriate deprescribing and communicate with the usual prescriber (<a href="?chapter=15#p218">p218</a>).

Geriatric care can use observation units, focused practitioners or local clinical leaders instead of a dedicated physical ED. Observation allows response assessment, tests/consults and transition planning (<a href="?chapter=15#p218">p218</a>).

<a id="p219"></a>**[<a href="?chapter=15#p219">p. 219</a>]**

Transitional-care nursing can reduce admission/readmission while increasing early returns; specialized nurses and volunteer models require training, leadership and quality control. Telemedicine and technology-assisted screening are emerging approaches whose proposed uses still require evaluation. Sustainable care depends on local, collaborative and patient-centered improvement (<a href="?chapter=15#p219">p219</a>). Further reading on these models continues on <a href="?chapter=15#p220">p220</a>.

## Key numbers

- In the emergency department, **6–38%** of adults older than **65** present with or develop delirium (<a href="?chapter=15#p209">p209</a>).

- On the 4AT, **≥4:** possible delirium with or without cognitive impairment; **1–3:** possible cognitive impairment; **0:** either unlikely, but delirium remains possible if acute-change information is incomplete. Administration takes **1–2 minutes**. These are screening interpretations, not proof of etiology (<a href="?chapter=15#book-figure-15-2">Figure 15-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=15#p211">p211</a>).
