# Rehabilitation

Identify the task the person wants to regain, the help it requires and the barrier to doing it safely. Those findings guide the device, team and setting.

## Goals and functional evaluation

**Five timed rises from a chair** test lower-extremity strength. <a href="?chapter=55#book-table-55-1">Table 55-1</a> links that attribute to walking and stair climbing. Gait speed measures dynamic performance, progressively narrower standing positions challenge static balance, and life-space records where the person actually moved in the preceding month (<a href="?chapter=55#p820">p820</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p821">821</a>).

SPPB (gait speed + chair stands + balance): worse scores → falls, NH placement, mortality; UAB Life-Space Assessment = mobility over the preceding month (<a href="?chapter=55#p820">p820</a>).

Assess rehab potential after acute illness resolves — delirium, hemodynamic instability, occult fracture, acute infection may delay referral to rehabilitation (<a href="?chapter=55#p821">p821</a>).

A diagnosis identifies the disease. A functional history identifies the task that has become difficult and the circumstances in which it fails. Two patients who cannot walk the same distance may be limited by dyspnea or by pain on loading a joint. The impairment, intervention and suitable destination can differ (<a href="?chapter=55#p818">p818</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p820">820</a>).

**Occupational therapy** turns improvement into something the patient can do, such as eating, dressing or grooming. A patient may still benefit from rehabilitation when long-term nursing care is expected. The destination changes how training is planned, but self-care still matters. The explicit list of self-care evaluation, training, assistive technology and home assessment is in <a href="?chapter=55#book-table-55-4">Table 55-4</a> on <a href="?chapter=55#p823">p823</a>.

Nagi model (1960s): active pathology → impairment → functional limitation → disability (<a href="?chapter=55#p818">p818</a>).

WHO ICF (2001): biopsychosocial — body functions/structures, activities, participation (<a href="?chapter=55#p818">p818</a>).

**US Medicare inpatient rehabilitation** includes 24-hour rehabilitation nursing and generally **3 hours of therapy per day on at least 5 days/week**, or **15 hours in 7 consecutive days** in documented cases. Multiple therapy disciplines participate, including PT or OT (<a href="?chapter=55#p822">p822</a>; [CMS inpatient rehabilitation criteria](https://www.cms.gov/training-education/medicare-learning-networkr-mln/compliance/medicare-provider-compliance-tips/inpatient-rehabilitation-hospitals)).

SNF: 24-hr nursing, no required minimum therapy intensity (<a href="?chapter=55#p822">p822</a>).

Home health ("homebound"): face-to-face encounter **within 90 days before or 30 days after** start; recertified **every 60 days** (<a href="?chapter=55#p822">p822</a>).

## The rehabilitation team and mobility aids

### Selecting an aid by the task

A **pick-up walker** provides bilateral support on a stationary frame. The patient can unload a leg completely, which explains the choice before a unilateral amputee has a prosthesis. Lifting the frame for each step costs strength and coordination and slows gait (<a href="?chapter=55#p828">p828</a>, <a href="?chapter=55#book-table-55-5">Table 55-5</a>).

A **two-wheeled walker** advances more easily and permits a smoother gait. Its fixed wheels make turns less smooth than a rollator's, but two wheels give more stability than four. The table lists Parkinson disease as a situation in which it may help. June 2023 described freezing and backward falls (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p828">828</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=55#book-table-55-5">Table 55-5</a>** (<a href="?chapter=55#p828">p828</a>): power wheelchair needs cognitive ability to operate safely (controls don't require intact UEs) — may be beneficial in neurologic disease (high-level SCI, MS, ALS) or multiple-limb amputation; rollator is good for outside walking because of large wheels and has a seat for resting.

A **four-wheeled rollator** turns easily, negotiates outdoor surfaces and provides a seat. For a patient limited by exertional dyspnea, the chance to sit and recover matters. The price of that mobility is less stability and the need to coordinate the hand brakes before sitting or standing (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p828">828</a>).

A **straight cane** widens the base of support and provides sensory feedback through the hand. Advance the cane with the involved leg, holding it in the opposite hand. The June 2026 patient had left hip abductor weakness and needed minimal help with balance, making a single point in the right hand appropriate. A quad cane accepts more weight and can help in hemiparesis, but its heavier, wider base may catch the foot or fit poorly on a stair (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p828">828</a>).

**Stairs reverse the sequence.** Ascend first with the uninvolved leg while the cane and involved leg support from below. Descend first with the cane and involved leg, keeping the stronger leg above to control lowering. For a right ankle fracture, identify which leg leads on descent (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>).

Interdisciplinary rehab team includes easily-forgotten members: chaplain/spiritual care provider (spiritual support to patient and family) and dietician (assesses nutritional status, alters diet to maximize nutrition) (**<a href="?chapter=55#book-table-55-4">Table 55-4</a>**, <a href="?chapter=55#p823">p823</a>).

MIRE: **890-nm**, **30-min × 3/week × 12 treatments**. Most studies showed no improvement in sensation over placebo (<a href="?chapter=55#p826">p826</a>).

"Cool" pool **<90°F** for heat-intolerant patients (e.g., MS) (<a href="?chapter=55#p826">p826</a>).

Canes: opposite the involved leg; "up with the good, down with the bad" on stairs (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>).

Diabetes and severe diabetic foot disease: Medicare covers **one pair** of therapeutic shoes and inserts or shoe modifications **each year** (<a href="?chapter=55#p829">p829</a>).

The team should set a measurable activity goal with the patient and family. Exercise to improve strength or range is most useful when it serves a task such as rising from a chair, reaching a shelf or transferring into a bath. Persistent symptoms after a session prompt reconsideration of its dose (<a href="?chapter=55#p822">p822</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p825">825</a>).

## Rehabilitation after fracture or surgery

Early mobilization after hip fracture limits deconditioning and the complications of bed rest. Fracture stability determines permissible loading after repair. When feasible, weight bearing as tolerated is easier for older adults to achieve than touchdown loading. After total hip replacement, rehabilitation emphasizes strengthening and gait training, with dislocation precautions appropriate to the surgical approach. For a repaired hip fracture, fracture stability determines loading, and the chapter favors weight bearing as tolerated when feasible (<a href="?chapter=55#p832">p832</a>).

After a **traditional posterolateral hip replacement**, crossing the legs, excessive flexion and rotating the leg may increase the risk of early dislocation. A raised toilet seat reduces the flexion required to sit down. May 2024 tested crossing the legs. Knee replacement calls for a different early task: controlling pain and swelling so that movement and quadriceps work can restore range (<a href="?chapter=55#p832">p832</a>).

**PAD with claudication.** The book advises intermittent walking **to the onset of pain** to increase walking distance before claudication begins (<a href="?chapter=55#p825">p825</a>). It describes a program lasting **at least 6 months** and reports better gains with **treadmill walking than strength training** (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

> **Current practice: Walking training for claudication.** supervised exercise alternates walking to moderate-to-maximum claudication with rest, working toward 30–45 minutes of active walking per session, at least 3 sessions weekly for at least 12 weeks. [2024 ACC/AHA PAD guideline, Table 14](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001251).

Heat, cold and electrical stimulation serve different purposes. Impaired circulation, sensation or cognition can make a thermal treatment unsafe. Buoyancy can assist movement, whereas whole-body warming can be poorly tolerated. The chapter distinguishes stimulation for pain from stimulation for muscle recruitment and wound healing (<a href="?chapter=55#p825">p825</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p827">827</a>).

Adapt the environment as well as the person. A bathing aid, an appropriate toilet seat or a device for dressing may restore independence in a specific task. Assess whether the patient can operate it and whether it fits at home. Electronic controls and communication aids can help when movement or speech limits participation (<a href="?chapter=55#p827">p827</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p829">829</a>).

## Cardiac, vascular and stroke rehabilitation

### Cardiac rehabilitation and medical stability

The patient in June 2023 had reduced ejection fraction but no active congestion. Exercise can address peripheral muscle conditioning as well as cardiovascular adaptations, reducing exercise intolerance and improving daily function. Hazzard calls for controlled fluid status and clinical stability for at least three to four weeks before starting exercise in heart failure. A remote admission alone does not establish readiness (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

Heart failure: stable **at least 3 to 4 weeks** before starting; CR → **28%** lower mortality/hospitalization, **29%** lower death rate, peak VO2 **+15% to 30%**, symptoms/ADLs/well-being **+15% to 50%**; QoL gains seen as early as **2 months** (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

In Parkinsonism, auditory rhythm or visual cues can reduce freezing for some patients. In osteoarthritis, strengthening can improve function and reduce pain, but the exercise that provokes pain should be modified or avoided. Following amputation, previous walking ability, the energy demands of prosthetic gait and capacity to learn matter when planning mobility (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p832">832</a>).

A cardiac rehabilitation program also provides monitoring, risk-factor management and support for adherence. In the Medicare cohort cited by Hazzard, participants had lower mortality than nonparticipants. That observation describes outcomes in the cohort and does not quantify an individual patient's expected benefit (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

**≥25 sessions** → **20% lower** 5-yr mortality vs. fewer sessions (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

Lower extremity amputation: **60% to 80%** get phantom limb pain; **~10%** of those with phantom pain rate it severe enough to be disabling (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

### Stroke recovery and readiness to participate

In apraxia, the patient understands the instruction and has recovered strength but cannot execute a learned action ([Stroke Rehabilitation Clinician Handbook p15](?chapter=62#ch62-praxis)). A different pattern appears in [Figure 4](?chapter=bank&q=mcq-5323c51583402673a5d61738). Transfers and communication improved, while walking and stairs stayed at the lowest FIM score. A continuing weight-bearing restriction explains this pattern. The chart measures performance and does not identify the fracture.

After stroke, inability to swallow safely and inability to communicate are separate barriers to rehabilitation. Aspiration can be silent in up to one-third of **patients with dysphagia**. Absence of coughing therefore cannot establish safe swallowing. The 2020 paper tested the denominator, alongside rates of incontinence, depression and shoulder pain (<a href="?chapter=55#p831">p831</a>).

Bladder incontinence **50% to 70%** month 1, back to general-population levels by **6 months** (<a href="?chapter=55#p831">p831</a>).

Shoulder pain **34% to 84%**; depression **15% to 70%** (<a href="?chapter=55#p831">p831</a>).

When tone and motor control change in the hemiplegic shoulder, positioning protects the joint from subluxation and early range-of-motion work helps prevent contracture. Those are separate aims of the treatment in 2020 Q50. The page supports positioning without naming a particular sling (<a href="?chapter=55#p831">p831</a>).

Stroke: rehab can begin as early as **24 to 48 hours** poststroke once the patient is medically stable (<a href="?chapter=55#p831">p831</a>).

Cognitive impairment calls for an assessment of ability to learn and follow instructions. A reversible acute problem such as delirium can delay that assessment. June 2022 Q33 contrasts a patient who can follow simple commands with patients whose current illness or participation limits effective treatment. Establish what the patient could do before the event and reassess after stabilization (<a href="?chapter=55#p820">p820</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p821">821</a>).

Strength: **60% to 80%** of max lifting ability; older beginners start at **~40% to 50%** (<a href="?chapter=55#p824">p824</a>).

Aerobic: usually **≥20 minutes** at **50% to 80%** VO2max; beginners start with **5 to 10 minutes**; needs **three to five times per week** (<a href="?chapter=55#p824">p824</a>).

Flexibility: **at least 90 degrees** knee flexion for stairs; **120 to 150 degrees** shoulder flexion/abduction to reach overhead (<a href="?chapter=55#p825">p825</a>).

CIMT: **4 to 6 hours/day** forced use of affected limb; greater upper-extremity motor gains than conventional treatment, but the between-group differences were not significant at 6 months (<a href="?chapter=55#p825">p825</a>).

Sarcopenia: **60% to 80% of 1RM**, **two to three times a week** (<a href="?chapter=55#p832">p832</a>).

Pulmonary: exercise **three times a week or more**, target moderate dyspnea (<a href="?chapter=55#p829">p829</a>).

Post-THA (posterolateral): may need to avoid **more than 90 degrees** hip flexion early (<a href="?chapter=55#p832">p832</a>).

At discharge, residual impairments can limit activity and expose the patient to further complications of inactivity. A transition to feasible community exercise extends the gains of a time-limited rehabilitation episode (<a href="?chapter=55#p832">p832</a>–<a href="?chapter=55#p833">833</a>).

## Key numbers

- Cardiac rehab: only **one-third** of eligible patients participate (**12%** in a cohort of more than 600,000 Medicare patients) (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

- Mortality with cardiac rehabilitation versus no rehabilitation was **2.3% vs. 5.3%**; benefit sustained at 5 years, **16% vs. 25%** (<a href="?chapter=55#p830">p830</a>).

**Day rehabilitation in practice.** The Kiryat Bialik center initially accepted patients who needed at least two rehabilitation disciplines. Its aim was to shorten or replace hospitalization for suitable patients, mainly people recovering from stroke or living with other neurological disorders. This was the center's admission policy during the 2009–2011 evaluation. Brookdale, [RR-622-12 (2012)](https://brookdale.jdc.org.il/en/publication/multidisciplinary-rehabilitation-daycare-center-community-kiryat-bialik-evaluation-study/), publication summary, paragraphs 1–4.

**Access and outcome are different measures.** In the Israeli 2009–2010 hospital survey, 56.5% of interviewed stroke survivors and 73.5% of interviewed hip-fracture survivors received inpatient rehabilitation. These proportions describe service receipt among the interviewed patients. Incomplete discharge recommendations limited the investigators' ability to distinguish failure to refer from failure to receive a service. The accompanying commentary called for measuring function across transitions as well as recording referral and receipt. [Zucker et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2045-4015-2-27), Results, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1186/2045-4015-2-27">Table 1</a> and Discussion; [Resnik, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2045-4015-2-28), commentary on discharge documentation and outcome assessment.
