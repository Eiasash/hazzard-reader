## AML arising after myelodysplasia

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-68050a5cca6b5c86ca5a1754" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

In an older patient with AML, ask about a previous myelodysplastic syndrome. The antecedent disorder points to a malignant clone that evolved over time and helps explain resistance to conventional induction. Deletions involving the long arms of chromosomes 5 and 7 are more frequent, another unfavorable feature. In the studies summarized here, blasts from older patients underwent less apoptosis after cytarabine and daunorubicin, and marrow expression of RAS and TNF pathways was increased. June 2025 Q5 turns each of those directions around except for the frequent history of MDS (pp. 1385–1386).

## Depression during cancer treatment

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-3f018bbcbb31f1f29b15dfb4" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Preserved appetite and sleep.** Symptoms of cancer, pain, treatment toxicity and depression overlap. Check the clinical history and possible contributors, including thyroid function, vitamin B12, calcium and liver function. For confirmed depression, an SSRI such as sertraline is a first-line option. In the bladder-cancer vignette, cardiac disease, prostatic enlargement and glaucoma make the anticholinergic and cardiac effects of a tricyclic particularly unattractive. Review interactions before choosing a specific agent (p1392).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-1d48795cfd7c82a0d2603459" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Depression with little appetite.** Mirtazapine can treat depression while also helping appetite and sleep. The lung-cancer vignette makes depression a condition of that choice. Also look for treatable barriers to eating, including nausea, constipation, pain, gastritis and oral candidiasis. Cancer cachexia involves muscle wasting and metabolic change, so an appetite response alone is an incomplete measure of recovery (p1392).

**Palliation during active treatment.** Ask about symptoms proactively. Some cancer trials found a survival benefit when symptoms were identified and treated early, in addition to improved quality of life. For fatigue, first look for a contributor such as anemia, depression, pain or medication. Tailor activity to function. The chapter includes antidepressants, modafinil, corticosteroids and methylphenidate among pharmacologic approaches to cancer fatigue, and says nonpharmacologic approaches are used more often (pp1391–1392).
> Current practice (not the book): At the end of life a short course of corticosteroids may help selected patients. Routine psychostimulants have limited support. Distinguish treatment of depression from prescribing an antidepressant solely for fatigue. [ASCO–SIO fatigue guideline, recommendations for advanced cancer](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO.24.00541).

## Persistent nausea in advanced cancer

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-765f7737231c2578f2a85a38" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Hazzard lists metoclopramide, haloperidol and 5-HT3 antagonists among options for cancer treatment-related nausea. Prevention, bowel assessment and small, frequent, low-fat meals are part of the chapter’s approach (pp1391–1392).
> Current practice (not the book): Persistent nausea after ondansetron and metoclopramide calls for reassessment of its cause and a change in symptom treatment. Haloperidol is a dopamine antagonist used for nausea in advanced cancer. Review QT and extrapyramidal risks, especially if other dopamine blockers are still prescribed. Passage of flatus and the absence of constipation do not support adding lactulose for the symptom described. Review bowel function, medicines, hydration and signs of obstruction as the illness evolves. [MASCC advanced-cancer antiemetic guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34398289/)

## Screening and androgen deprivation

**Who can benefit from screening?** In June 2022 Q7, a 72-year-old woman with diabetes remains a candidate for mammography. BRCA carrier status is unnecessary for routine screening. A 90-year-old man is beyond the age for routine PSA screening, and a 77-year-old woman with end-stage heart failure is unlikely to live long enough to benefit from colorectal screening. Both parents having had pancreatic cancer merits a pedigree and hereditary-risk assessment. The chapter recommends considering functional status, comorbidity and life expectancy when judging likely screening benefit (pp1384–1385).
> Current practice (not the book): USPSTF recommends biennial mammography at ages 40–74. Under the CAPS family-history criterion, the affected relatives must be on the same side or be first-degree relatives of each other; two unrelated parents alone do not satisfy either condition. A pathogenic variant can change surveillance eligibility. [USPSTF mammography recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/breast-cancer-screening) · [CAPS, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7295005/).

**What to discuss before androgen deprivation.** Treatment can suppress metastatic prostate cancer, relieve symptoms and lower PSA. Most patients eventually progress. Loss of androgen signaling can affect sexual function, muscle and bone, and memory. Ask about baseline cognition and follow changes in daily function (Ch 88 p1390 and Ch 90 pp1416–1417).
> Current practice (not the book): Some observational cohorts found more subsequent dementia diagnoses after treatment, a possible risk worth discussing with the man in June 2022 Q75. The association cannot predict whether he will develop dementia. [SEER–Medicare cohort, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31268539/).

## How geriatric assessment changes cancer care

ECOG measures activity and time spent confined to bed or a chair. An active patient can still fall, lose weight, forget medicines or lack anyone to call during treatment. A multidomain assessment identifies what can change: a review of sedating drugs, help with transport, nutritional support or physical therapy. Tumor stage and expected treatment benefit remain part of the same decision. Inflammatory and epigenetic markers are under study; no individual biomarker reliably substitutes for clinical assessment of functional age (pp. 1386–1388).

**Separate screening, prediction and treatment.** Tools such as G8 identify who should receive a full assessment. CARG combines age-related and treatment factors to estimate severe chemotherapy toxicity. CRASH estimates hematologic and nonhematologic toxicity separately. A score helps discuss risk; an intervention requires someone to act on the individual findings and follow the patient (pp. 1388–1389, Table 88-2).

**Two geriatric-assessment trials.** The 41-practice trial involving **718 patients** reduced serious toxicity by **20 percentage points** with assessment-guided recommendations. Hazzard reports **620 randomized patients** and a **10% reduction** in chemotherapy toxicity in the trial with multidisciplinary implementation (p1388).
> Current practice (not the book): GAP70+ provided oncologists with a geriatric assessment summary and tailored recommendations for adults aged 70 or older with advanced incurable cancer and at least one impairment. Grade 3–5 toxicity over three months occurred in 51% versus 71% with usual care. In GAIN, a single-center team implemented interventions for adults aged 65 or older starting chemotherapy for solid tumors. Usual-care oncologists received the assessment results. Among 605 eligible patients, grade 3 or higher toxicity occurred in 50.5% versus 60.6%, a difference of 10.1 percentage points. These absolute differences apply to their respective trial populations. [GAP70+, primary results](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34741815/) · [GAIN, primary results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485211/).

## Summary

### Epidemiology & Aging–Cancer Biology
- Cancer is the 2nd leading cause of death after heart disease in the US; **>60%** of newly diagnosed malignant tumors and **70%** of all cancer deaths occur in persons **65+** (p1381).
- Aging and neoplasia share the same underlying biological mechanisms (Hallmarks of Cancer / Hallmarks of Aging) but push in opposite directions: aging → reduced proliferation/senescence; neoplasia → accumulated damage → increased proliferation — "opposite sides of the same coin" (p1382).
- Telomeres protect chromosome ends. Their progressive loss can destabilize chromosomes and increase mutation risk. Page 1383 describes a functional decline from age 30 at about 1% per year.
  > Current practice (not the book): Measured leukocyte telomere shortening also occurs in infancy, and its rate varies with age. [Human leukocyte study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9576930/)
- Time from a mutated cell to a clinically detectable tumor ≈ **10–30%** of a species' maximum lifespan (p1383).
- 7 proposed mechanisms for rising cancer risk with age: longer carcinogen exposure duration, altered carcinogen susceptibility, decreased DNA repair, oncogene activation/tumor-suppressor loss, telomere shortening, cellular senescence/SASP-driven microenvironment change, decreased immune surveillance (p1383–1384).

### Screening & Presentation
- Older adults are simultaneously **underscreened** (racial/ethnic minorities, <high-school education, below poverty line) and **overscreened** (routine screening continues even with <5-year life expectancy or severe nursing-home disability, risking net harm from work-up) (p1384).
- New Mexico study of **800** patients older than 65: only **29%** were asymptomatic at diagnosis; **48%** presented within 2 months of symptom onset, but **19%** delayed ≥12 weeks and **7.4%** delayed ≥1 year (p1385).
- Biological behavior varies by tumor type and is not uniformly "more indolent" with age: **malignant melanoma** presents with more advanced, deeper local disease in older patients; **lung cancer** presents with *more* localized disease in older patients; breast cancer in older women is more often ER-positive with longer doubling time (generally better-prognosis biology) — yet overall cancer-related survival is still *lower* in older women (p1385).
- AML has a distinctly worse prognosis in older adults: poor-prognosis karyotypes (5q-, 7q-), antecedent MDS, chemo-resistance pathways, and lower apoptosis of leukemic blasts after ara-C/daunorubicin (p1385–1386).

### CGA & Chemotherapy-Toxicity Risk Tools
- ECOG performance status (0 = fully active without restriction from disease, 5 = death) alone is **not sufficient** in geriatric oncology — a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) adds substantial prognostic/functional information even in patients with a good ECOG PS (p1387–1388).
- CGA domains: functional status, comorbidities, nutrition, polypharmacy, psychological/cognitive status, socioeconomic issues, geriatric syndromes (p1388).
- **CARG** chemo-toxicity score: low risk **0–5 pts → 30%** toxicity; intermediate **6–9 pts → 52%**; high **10–19 pts → 83%** (p1388).
- **CRASH** score: low-risk patients — **7%** hematologic / **33%** nonhematologic toxicity; high-risk patients — **100%** hematologic / **93%** nonhematologic toxicity (p1388).
- Full CGA tool: median completion time **22 minutes**; **87%** of patients complete their self-reported portion without assistance (p1388).
- Geriatric assessment reduced serious treatment toxicity in the two trials summarized on p1388: **718 patients, 20-percentage-point absolute reduction** and **620 randomized patients, 10% reduction**.
- Screening tools to select who needs a full CGA (Table 88-2, p1389): **G8** (total score 17, ≤14 = geriatric risk present), **Groningen Frailty Indicator** (score 4–15 = risk present), **VES-13** (score ≥3 = vulnerability), **SAOP2**, **Abbreviated CGA (aCGA)**.

### Treatment Modalities
- **Surgery:** healing can be compromised at every phase, including inflammatory and proliferative responses and collagen remodeling. The chapter reports that geriatric comanagement can improve 90-day postoperative mortality (pp1388–1389).
  > Current practice (not the book): The retrospective cohort of cancer surgery patients aged ≥75 found an association with lower 90-day mortality. [Original surgical cohort](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32822490/).
- **Radiation**: normal-tissue radiation effects **may be** enhanced by **10–15%** in older patients; hypofractionated whole-breast RT is safe in older breast cancer; SBRT is an option when comorbidities preclude surgery for early NSCLC/pancreatic cancer; intermediate-risk prostate cancer may use **4–6 months** hormonal therapy + EBRT (p1389).
- **Chemotherapy**: dose by creatinine clearance, not merely creatinine (↓lean body mass in older adults); watch H2 blockers/antacids/PPIs with oral agents; more common/severe in older adults — neurotoxicity (taxanes, platinum, vincristine, high-dose cytarabine, bortezomib), cardiotoxicity (doxorubicin), mucositis (5-FU) (p1390).
- **Hormonal therapy**: **70–80%** of breast tumors in older patients are ER-positive. Tamoxifen ↑ thromboembolism/CVA risk — caution with venous insufficiency, HTN, AF. Aromatase inhibitors ↑ osteoporosis/fracture risk. Bone-directed therapy threshold: T-score **< −2.0 SD** alone, OR **< −1.5 SD** with one additional risk factor, OR two or more risk factors (without BMD) (p1390).
- **Targeted therapy**: mostly oral, CYP450-metabolized — high drug-interaction risk with polypharmacy; higher rate of dose reduction/discontinuation in older vs younger patients (p1390).
- **Immunotherapy (ICIs)**: one retrospective cohort observed similar frequencies of high-grade immune-related events by age. Among those with high-grade events, the older group had shorter median survival. Susceptibility to complications of steroids or hospitalization is one proposed explanation (p1391). [Original cohort, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8184608/)
- **HSCT**: historically allogeneic SCT limited to age **<50**; autologous SCT now performed in fit patients **>70**. Delirium occurs in up to **50%** of allogeneic HSCT recipients (p1391).

### Supportive & Palliative Care
- Routine symptom assessment and intervention during cancer treatment can improve overall survival by **upwards of five months** without changing cancer therapy, according to the chapter (p1391).
  > Current practice (not the book): In one trial of electronic symptom monitoring during treatment, median survival was 31.2 versus 26.0 months. A later community trial found better symptom control, function and quality of life without a survival difference. Symptom monitoring can help even when survival is unchanged. [Original trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5817466/) · [PRO-TECT](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-025-03507-y)
- **Pain**: present in **50–90%** of advanced disease, **60–70%** of patients on active treatment; HELP study — one in three patients died in severe pain (p1392).
- **Fatigue**: prevalence **40–90%** during treatment, **19–80%** after treatment completion; exercise is one of the most effective treatments despite being counter-intuitive (p1392).
- **Depression**: prevalence **17–25%** among cancer patients; SSRIs first-line, mirtazapine useful for mood/appetite/sleep, nortriptyline/desipramine third-line (p1392).
- In dying patients, the chapter says artificial nutrition and hydration do not prolong life and may increase discomfort and hasten death. It identifies **xerostomia** as the only reported discomfort attributable to dehydration near death and suggests oral swabs or ice chips (p1392).
  > Current practice (not the book): In the **last days of life** with advanced cancer, routine artificial hydration or nutrition has no established survival benefit and can add edema, pulmonary congestion or procedural burden. Ask about thirst and discomfort, offer mouth care and safe sips or ice chips as tolerated, and individualize decisions with the patient and family. [NCI, Last Days of Life](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/caregivers/planning/last-days-hp-pdq)

### Survivorship & Caregivers
- By **2030**, **70%** of cancer patients will be 65+, with incidence in this group rising **~70%** from 2010–2030. The chapter counted **16 million** US cancer survivors at the time of writing, **~60%** age 65+; by **2040**, projected **26 million** survivors, **73%** age 65+, almost **50%** age 75+ (p1392–1393).
- **63%** of home care for older cancer patients comes from informal caregivers; nearly **40%** of these caregivers have their own comorbidities and rate their own health fair/poor (p1393).

### Exam traps
- Do not equate "more indolent tumor biology" with "better survival" — older women have more ER-positive, longer-doubling-time breast cancers (favorable biology) yet still have *lower* overall cancer-related survival than younger women (p1385).
- Direction of stage-at-presentation with age is tumor-specific, not uniform: melanoma presents *more* advanced in older adults; lung cancer presents *less* advanced (more localized) in older adults — do not assume all cancers present later in older patients (p1385).
- CARG and CRASH are two different chemo-toxicity risk tools with different point scales and different endpoints (CARG = single composite risk tier; CRASH = separate hematologic/nonhematologic percentages) — don't merge their numbers (p1388).

## Recall

**1. What two summary statistics from the National Cancer Institute establish cancer as a disease of aging?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Over 60% of newly diagnosed malignant tumors and 70% of all cancer deaths occur in persons 65 years or older (p1381).

</details>

**2. How can loss of telomere function promote malignancy, and when has telomere shortening been measured?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Loss of chromosome-end protection can promote genomic instability. The chapter describes functional decline from age 30 at about 1% per year (p1383). Leukocyte measurements show that shortening is already present in early childhood and proceeds at an age-dependent rate.

</details>

**4. In the New Mexico study of 800 patients over 65, what fraction were asymptomatic at cancer detection, and what fraction delayed seeking care at least 1 year?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only 29% were asymptomatic at detection; 7.4% delayed seeking care for at least 1 year (p1385).

</details>

**5. Does malignant melanoma present at a more or less advanced stage in older patients, and how does this compare with lung cancer?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Melanoma presents with more advanced-stage, deeper-penetrating local disease in older patients; lung cancer shows the opposite pattern, with older patients more often presenting with localized disease (p1385).

</details>

**6. Why does breast cancer tend to behave more indolently in older women biologically, and does this translate into better overall survival?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Older women have a higher frequency of ER-positive tumors and longer tumor doubling times (both favorable prognostic features), but overall cancer-related survival is still lower in older women (p1385).

</details>

**8. On the CARG chemotherapy-toxicity risk model, what toxicity rates correspond to the low, intermediate, and high risk-point tiers?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Low risk (0–5 points): 30% toxicity. Intermediate (6–9 points): 52%. High (10–19 points): 83% (p1388).

</details>

**9. On the CRASH score, what hematologic and nonhematologic toxicity rates were seen in high-risk versus low-risk patients?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

High-risk: 100% hematologic, 93% nonhematologic toxicity. Low-risk: 7% hematologic, 33% nonhematologic toxicity (p1388).

</details>

**12. What T-score thresholds trigger bone-directed therapy in an older patient on aromatase-inhibitor therapy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

T-score less than −2.0 SD alone, OR less than −1.5 SD with one additional risk factor, OR two or more risk factors (without bone mineral density) (p1390).

</details>

**14. What proportion of allogeneic HSCT recipients develop delirium, and how does historical age eligibility for allogeneic versus autologous SCT differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 50% develop delirium. Allogeneic SCT was historically limited to patients under age 50, while autologous SCT has been performed in fit patients over age 70 (p1391).

</details>

**15. By 2040, what proportion of US cancer survivors are projected to be 65 or older, and 75 or older?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

73% are projected to be 65 or older, and almost 50% are projected to be 75 or older, out of a projected 26 million total survivors (p1393).

</details>
