# Chapter 13 Study: Geriatrics Around the World

## Summary

**Why geriatrics looks different across countries**
- Population aging is worldwide; in many less-developed countries it is occurring faster and within larger populations, demanding substantial social and health-system adjustment (p179).
- Longer survival brings multimorbidity, functional deficits, cognitive problems and geriatric syndromes. A disease-by-disease, acute-hospital model does not adequately capture this complexity (p179-180).
- Geriatric medicine grew from recognizing potentially reversible diagnoses and disability, using careful assessment and rehabilitation; its approach extends beyond institutions (p180).
- Funding, professional leadership, resources and perceived need shape whether geriatrics is recognized and where it is available (p180).

**Read the international survey carefully**
- The chapter surveys **63 countries** through national leaders; estimates may be inexact because definitions and health systems differ (p181).
- There were **no low-income-country respondents**. The sample included **42 high-income**, **17 upper-middle-income** and **4 lower-middle-income** countries (p181).
- Nearly **90%** reported formal training programs, but prerequisites, training length and certification differ substantially (p181).
- Specialist definitions and population denominators vary; a proposed workforce target based on consultative care cannot be directly applied to every service model (p181, p186).
- Some countries also certify geriatric specialization for nurses, therapists and social workers; training the wider team matters (p181).

**Care settings and system support**
- Acute geriatric units, postacute wards, long-term care, outpatient services and home services occur in different combinations; access across the full continuum is uncommon (p180-181).
- Hospital practice is most common, often concentrated in larger hospitals, cities and academic centers. General practitioners often provide medical care in long-term care (p186).
- Standardized assessment, including interRAI, can compare populations, service use, outcomes and quality across settings and countries (p181).
- Payment, eligibility assessments, policy mandates and national initiatives influence service development; the chapter gives country-specific examples rather than one transferable formula (p186-189).
- Workforce shortages remain widespread despite efforts to expand training, service availability and interdisciplinary geriatric care (p181, p189).
