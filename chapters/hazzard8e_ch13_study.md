# Chapter 13 Study: Geriatrics Around the World

## Summary

**Why geriatrics looks different across countries**
- Population aging is worldwide; in many less-developed countries it is occurring faster and within larger populations, demanding substantial social and health-system adjustment (p179).
- Longer survival brings multimorbidity, functional deficits, cognitive problems and geriatric syndromes. A disease-by-disease, acute-hospital model does not adequately capture this complexity (p179-180).
- Geriatric medicine grew from recognizing potentially reversible diagnoses and disability, using careful assessment and rehabilitation; its approach extends beyond institutions (p180).
- Funding, professional leadership, resources and perceived need shape whether geriatrics is recognized and where it is available (p180).

**Read the international survey carefully**
- The chapter surveys **63 countries** through national leaders; estimates may be inexact because definitions and health systems differ (p181).
- There were **no low-income-country respondents**. The sample included **42 high-income**, **17 upper-middle-income** and **4 lower-middle-income** countries (p181).
- Nearly **90%** reported formal training programs, but prerequisites, training length and certification differ substantially (p181).
- Specialist definitions and population denominators vary; a proposed workforce target based on consultative care cannot be directly applied to every service model (p181, p186).
- Some countries also certify geriatric specialization for nurses, therapists and social workers; training the wider team matters (p181).

**Care settings and system support**
- Acute geriatric units, postacute wards, long-term care, outpatient services and home services occur in different combinations; access across the full continuum is uncommon (p180-181).
- Hospital practice is most common, often concentrated in larger hospitals, cities and academic centers. General practitioners often provide medical care in long-term care (p186).
- Standardized assessment, including interRAI, can compare populations, service use, outcomes and quality across settings and countries (p181).
- Payment, eligibility assessments, policy mandates and national initiatives influence service development; the chapter gives country-specific examples rather than one transferable formula (p186-189).
- Workforce shortages remain widespread despite efforts to expand training, service availability and interdisciplinary geriatric care (p181, p189).

### Comparing systems without losing the denominator

The Royal College of Physicians estimate cited in the chapter was **2 geriatricians per 100,000 people aged 75 or older** for a consultative service. Table 13-1 reports a different denominator for national workforce estimates: people aged **65 or older**. Before comparing two figures, check both the age group and the work expected of the specialist. A consultant supporting many primary care physicians and a physician providing continuing care to nursing-home residents have different caseloads (p181, p183–186).

A common assessment instrument can serve two purposes. At the bedside, compatible assessments can communicate the person's needs across home and residential care. At the system level, they can compare service use and quality across jurisdictions. The chapter describes New Zealand's use of interRAI in home and long-term care, and national leaders' reports of different entry routes into training, including specialist training after internal or family medicine and combined programs (p181–186, p189).

### Read the population graph as counts, ages and projections

Figure 13-1 divides the population aged 65 and older into ages 65–79 and 80 or older. The vertical quantity is millions of people, not percentage of a country's population or the number needing a particular service. In the countries classified as developed in the figure, the total increases from **171 million in 2000** to a projected **327 million in 2050**. The younger segment increases from 134 to 206 million and the older segment from 37 to 121 million. The total therefore gives only part of the planning problem: the mix of ages within the older population is changing as well (p179–180).

In the developing-country group, the corresponding totals rise from **249 million** to a projected **1,175 million**. The 65–79 segment reaches 870 million and the 80-or-older segment 305 million in the 2050 bar. The figure's intermediate 2020 bars show 238 million versus 476 million aged 65 or older in the developed and developing groups. These are the source's historical estimates and projections. A projected older population of this size implies very different training and service-capacity needs from the same percentage change in a small population. It does not imply that every older person is dependent or will need a geriatrician (p179–180).

Falling fertility compounds the organizational challenge. The chapter describes fewer working-age adults for each older person, while a larger share of the population survives into very old age. State precisely which group is in the numerator before describing a dependency or support ratio. A ratio of potential workers to older adults falls under these circumstances, whereas a ratio of older adults to potential workers rises. Neither ratio measures the number actually employed, the quality of family support, the ability of older adults to work, or the extent to which publicly funded services substitute for unpaid help. These differences matter when comparing two systems facing similar demographic trends (p179).

### Why a successful acute-care model does not by itself address disability

In a disease-oriented system the usual sequence is to identify a pathology, deliver a treatment and infer that function will improve as the disease improves. That sequence can work for a single acute illness. It becomes incomplete when several conditions, cognitive impairment, poor nutrition and social constraints act together. A patient treated successfully for a fracture may still be unable to return home because of delirium, loss of mobility and the absence of someone to assist with meals. Recording the fracture outcome alone would miss both the remaining problem and the practical work required to solve it (p179–181).

The emergence of geriatric medicine is linked to the observation that people considered chronically disabled could have unrecognized treatable disease and the potential to recover function through rehabilitation. The important distinction is between a place where older people are housed and a clinical service that actively reassesses reversible problems. A team can improve mobility, nutrition or continence while several chronic diagnoses persist. The outcome then needs to include what the patient can do, where they can live and what care is required. The approach can be applied on an acute ward, in postacute rehabilitation, long-term care or at home (p180–181).

Countries did not adopt this approach through a uniform pathway. Funding, physician leadership, other professional groups, available institutions and cultural expectations shape which older people are seen and at what point in their illness. Some systems concentrate geriatric care in an acute specialty service; others direct it toward rehabilitation after acute triage. In one country a geriatrician may act as an attending physician for residential-care patients, while in another they mainly advise a primary care doctor who continues to provide ongoing care. The same specialty title can therefore describe different workloads, clinical relationships and moments at which expertise is available (p180–181).

### Ask what the international survey was designed to measure

National leaders responded through an online survey on training, clinical practice and institutional support. The chapter includes **63 countries** and explicitly leaves research activity outside its scope. Coverage differed markedly by income category. Under the classification used for the survey, **42 of 59** high-income countries, **17 of 55** upper-middle-income countries and **4 of 44** lower-middle-income countries were represented. There were no respondents among **28** low-income countries. A finding reported as a percentage of respondents therefore describes these reporting countries, not the same percentage of the global older population. Nor does a missing response establish that geriatrics is absent (p181).

A national leader can report the existence of a program without providing a comprehensive count of every training site or clinician in practice. Definitions, data sources and service organization differ; some cells are estimates, ranges or unavailable. A national “yes” about home care could mean a broadly distributed service or a program concentrated in one region. Conversely, absence of formal specialty recognition does not exclude local efforts to teach geriatric skills. Read the survey as a comparative description of how care is organized at the time reported. To judge access, coverage or quality would require additional information on the people served and the resources where they live (p181–186).

### Training, examination and workforce are different dimensions

Figure 13-2A asks whether qualification requires a defined period in a certified program; nearly nine in ten respondents said yes. Panel B divides the routes into training after internal or family medicine, combined programs beginning after medical school, no current training and other arrangements. The “other” category includes programs of differing duration and systems in transition. Panel C asks a separate question about a standardized qualifying examination; only about three-fifths reported one. A requirement for supervised training does not imply an additional standardized exam, and an exam does not tell the reader how many people enter the training pathway or where they later practice (p181–182).

Table 13-1 has separate columns for whether certified training is required, the entry route, a standard examination, number of programs, disciplines with other certification, training output and number of geriatricians per 100,000 people aged 65 or older. Do not substitute one column for another. A large national training program can operate across multiple sites. A country with many positions can report one centrally organized program. An entry referring to people at all levels of training cannot be assumed to count graduates in a single year. The table itself sometimes records this qualification in a cell that might otherwise look like an annual number (p183–186).

Several comparisons make the distinctions concrete. Australia reported one program with **250 positions at multiple sites**, three years of advanced training after internal medicine and no standard qualifying examination in the relevant column. Cuba also reported a single multisite program, but a combined medicine/geriatrics route and an examination. Finland reported **5 years after medical school**, while Germany reported **18 months** after several possible prior specialties. Japan reported two to three years after internal medicine or other specialties and **273 programs**. These are examples of how respondents defined training; the program count cannot be interpreted as a direct measure of national expertise or of the intensity of a trainee's clinical experience (p183–184).

The historical Israel row is particularly instructive because the book also describes a role in assessing functional eligibility. The table lists **2 years** after internal or family medicine or a combined internal medicine/geriatrics route, a standardized exam, **20** programs, **50** trainees per year in the reported output column and **over 100 per 100,000 aged 65 or older**. The U.S. row instead reports **1 year** following internal or family medicine, an exam, **158** programs, **262** trained annually and **14 per 100,000 aged 65 or older**. The United Kingdom row gives a combined route and a single program in its program-count column, with **750** in the training-output column. These are the chapter's survey values rather than present-day credentialing requirements. Distinct training structures, reporting conventions and workforce roles limit direct comparison (p184, p186).

The table captures interdisciplinary differences too. The Netherlands row lists a combined route including internal medicine, psychiatry and neurology and names nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy and social work among disciplines with certification. The U.S. response also lists pharmacy, psychology and psychiatry. Another country may not report formal certification for those professions but still have a multidisciplinary clinical team. Professional qualification, availability of trained staff and how those staff cooperate in care are three different questions. A workforce planning answer limited to counting specialist physicians leaves much of the rehabilitation and care-coordination work unmeasured (p181, p185–186).

### Interpret the workforce ratio in relation to the specialist's work

The U.K. Royal College of Physicians estimate in the chapter concerns a *consultative model* and uses the denominator aged **75 or older**. The table reports population aged **65 or older**. Even if both used exactly the same definition of a certified physician, the rates would differ because the populations are different sizes. An annual count of people entering the field is yet another quantity; it cannot simply replace a count of currently practicing specialists. Before inferring shortage or adequacy, ask which clinicians are counted, the age band, the year and whether the service expects consultation, primary medical responsibility, certification of eligibility or some combination (p181, p183–186).

The distribution of clinicians matters after the national ratio is known. In the surveyed countries geriatricians were often based in larger hospitals, university centers and cities. A person in a remote community or a small residential facility might not encounter one even in a country reporting a relatively large specialty workforce. If specialists support other clinicians through consultation or training, some patients benefit without a direct specialist visit. If specialists retain primary responsibility, the number of people each can follow may be smaller. These differences explain why the authors describe a widespread perception of shortage while declining to apply a single international staffing target (p180–181, p186).

### Compare sites of practice without turning them into coverage percentages

Figure 13-3A permits multiple answers. Hospital practice has the longest bar, followed by outpatient practice; residential care, home services, rehabilitation and hospice or palliative care appear in many responses. The home-service and rehabilitation bars each exceed one-half of respondents. A country's selection of “home” could include a home visit or coordination of home services. It is not the percentage of older adults receiving a visit, the fraction of a geriatrician's time spent outside hospital or the proportion of homes in the country with access. The bars cannot be added to construct a distribution of specialist working hours (p186–187).

Panel B asks about designated hospital beds, whereas panel C asks about formally established collaborative teams. A ward with dedicated beds gives a geriatric team direct responsibility for a defined group. An orthogeriatric collaboration can reach people admitted under orthopedics at the moment they need surgery and rehabilitation. The chapter also describes collaborative involvement in cancer, trauma and emergency services; “other” includes surgery, neurology, stroke care and palliative care among several possibilities. A positive answer in one panel does not establish that the other arrangement exists. A patient can receive specialty input without transfer to a geriatric ward (p186–187).

Figure 13-4A describes ambulatory services. General geriatrics is most frequently reported, followed by dementia and falls, balance or mobility clinics; incontinence clinics are less commonly reported. The category “other” includes services organized around frailty, multiple medicines, cancer, osteoporosis, perioperative assessment or movement disorders. Each clinic can concentrate expertise on a problem while primary care continues to manage other conditions. A condition-specific clinic is not by itself a substitute for following frailty, cognition, function, medicines and home support together. The relevant question is how findings return to the patient's ongoing clinicians and how recommendations can be implemented (p186, p188).

### Assessment, payment and eligibility influence which services reach patients

Comparable assessment instruments can serve different levels of decision-making. At the bedside or on transfer, compatible descriptions of function, cognition and support can help the next team understand what has changed. At a regional or national level, aggregating assessments can identify differences in case mix, service use, medication or quality. The chapter describes multinational European home- and long-term-care projects and use of large interRAI databases. Before attributing a difference in outcomes to the service itself, check whether the groups being compared have similar baseline needs. A facility taking more complex residents may otherwise appear less effective simply because of who it serves (p181).

The chapter reports implementation at different levels, from a single organization to province-wide or national use. New Zealand used home-care and long-term-care interRAI instruments; Belgium and Ontario also adopted multiple compatible instruments. Using a common vocabulary across settings can support individual continuity and comparison of caseloads, but the two uses require different outputs. A family needs a feasible plan for tonight, whereas a health ministry needs comparable information across many households. Taiwan's reported ICOPE screening approach is another example of structured assessment; a screening result and a detailed plan to deliver support are distinct steps (p181, p189).

> Current practice (not the book): WHO’s second-edition ICOPE pathway begins with basic assessment, proceeds to in-depth assessment and a personalized plan, then implementation and monitoring. Screen cognition, locomotion, vitality, vision, hearing and psychological capacity, as well as continence, social support and caregiver needs. For locomotion, inability to complete five chair rises within 14 seconds prompts further assessment. [WHO ICOPE handbook, 2024 second edition, posted 2025](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240103726).

Figure 13-4B asks how physicians are paid. Almost every respondent reported *some* salaried geriatricians, and approximately half also reported fees for individual services. The categories coexist. Salaries may come from government, hospitals or other institutions; fees can come from insurance or private patients. Panel C asks whether specialist assessment is ever required for access to a service or benefit, and somewhat fewer than half reported yes. A specialist performing an eligibility assessment is carrying out a different task from providing continuing treatment or rehabilitation. Payment for assessment can support that role without guaranteeing access to the eventual service (p186, p188).

### Read country examples as different solutions to a common problem

The book's health-policy examples reflect the period of the survey. Israel described geriatric assessment for a social-security home-service benefit in people aged **90 or older** with functional impairment. The United Kingdom described specialist participation in hip-fracture care, and Germany required geriatric involvement for hip-fracture patients aged **70 or older**. Japan offered incentives for comprehensive geriatric assessment on hospital admission and for review and reduction of polypharmacy. These are mechanisms for directing expertise toward an identified decision or patient group. They are not interchangeable age thresholds for referral in other systems (p186, p189).

Other examples concern where the specialist works. Malta emphasized postacute rehabilitation and assessment for transfer from the acute service. Norway expected geriatricians in larger acute hospitals. France reported nursing-home medical care delivered by general physicians with specific geriatric training. Turkey used an obligatory service requirement aimed at geographic areas with unmet need. Australia reported efforts to reach Indigenous older people and those in correctional settings. In each case, ask what barrier the policy addresses: expertise in acute illness, access after a hospital stay, competence of the long-term-care workforce, or distribution of clinicians beyond usual referral centers (p189).

National organization also differs outside hospitals. The chapter describes a coordinated home-care system across health and social-security sectors in Panama, community-oriented reform in Ireland, a regional population-health approach in Singapore and a national institute developing an aging plan in Mexico. Some respondents described laws, others an assessment program or changes still in development. A law, a funding mechanism, an allocated team and demonstrated patient access represent successive but distinct achievements. Read the examples against the chapter's central clinical concern: whether an older person with several illnesses and a functional problem can obtain coordinated assessment and care when and where it is needed (p186–189).

## Exam traps

- The 1,175 in the 2050 developing-country bar is millions of people aged 65 or older. It is a projection, not a percentage or an estimate of persons needing specialist care (p180).
- Nearly 90% of survey respondents reported defined training. The separate standardized-exam bar is closer to three-fifths (p181–182).
- A multisite national training program, an individual training position and an annual graduate are not equivalent units in Table 13-1 (p183–186).
- Settings and payment methods could have multiple responses. A country's hospital, home-care and salary entries do not describe three mutually exclusive groups of patients (p186–188).

- A country's reported training program does not establish how many trained specialists are available outside academic centers (p181, p186).
- A workforce figure for people aged 75 or older cannot be compared directly with a table using people aged 65 or older (p181, p183).
- The international survey describes respondents at the time of the chapter. Low-income countries were not represented (p181).
