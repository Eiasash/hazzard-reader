# Chapter 29 Study: Surgical Quality and Outcomes

## Summary

**Define success with the patient**
- Many older adults value independence and quality of life as much as, or more than, survival (p399).
- Recovery includes physical, cognitive, psychological, emotional, social and economic function, sometimes over a year or longer (p399).
- Ask what matters most: cancer control, continued independence or symptom relief may lead to different decisions, including foregoing surgery (p404).
- Short stays, complication rates and readmissions remain useful, but do not fully describe a successful outcome (p399).

**Estimate and reduce risk**
- Frailty predicts perioperative morbidity and mortality better than chronological age alone; it need not correlate with anesthetic physical-status scores (p399-400).
- Appropriately selected older adults can benefit even from high-risk procedures; age alone should not decide access (p400).
- Anticipate delirium, dehydration, falls/fractures, failure to thrive and pressure injury as geriatric surgical complications (p400).
- Geriatric-focused care and co-management are associated with shorter stays and fewer complications and deaths (p399-401).
- Include living situation, falls, mobility aids, cognition, surrogate consent and palliative needs in risk discussions (p403).

**Follow recovery beyond discharge**
- Loss of independence includes declining ADLs or mobility and increased home support or institutional care (p401-402).
- In one surgical cohort, **60%** had some loss of independence at discharge; this was not a universal prognosis (p402).
- Readmissions and intercurrent illness can delay functional recovery; reassess function after these events (p401).
- Delirium is associated with major complications, institutional discharge and mortality; the chapter reports prevention in up to **40%** with perioperative care adjustments (p402).
- Delirium and postoperative cognitive dysfunction share risks, but their relationship over time remains uncertain (p402).
- Quality of life may exceed baseline even after complications or functional decline; discuss expected trajectories and time at home (p402-403).
- Use an individualized plan spanning preoperative optimization, the operation and longitudinal recovery (p403).

### Interpreting the surgical outcomes literature

- In a U.S. outcomes comparison, adults over 75 more often needed a prolonged stay. After major gastrointestinal surgery, approximately **60%** of that group were still hospitalized after **7 days**. This figure measures length of stay, a different outcome from loss of independence at discharge (p400).
- An analysis of more than **14,000** operations linked increasing frailty to major morbidity, readmission, death and facility discharge after inpatient operations. Among outpatients the signal was unplanned admission. The same frailty history therefore informs different conversations depending on the planned setting (p400).
- In the SAGES cohort of **566** adults aged 70 or older having scheduled major noncardiac surgery, **45%** had a readmission during **18 months**. Readmissions unrelated to the original admission also delayed recovery. Assess the cumulative interruption to function, even when the operation itself went well (p401).
- Ask how much time a patient hopes to spend at home after cancer surgery. Time spent in a hospital or institution may matter more to someone prioritizing home life than an estimate of years alive; baseline frailty and the intensity of the procedure were among the factors associated with less time at home (p403).

### Decide which outcome would make an operation worthwhile

A surgeon and a patient can agree that an operation is technically feasible but mean different things by success. The surgeon may mean removal of the tumor with survival to discharge. The patient may mean being able to return to a familiar apartment, climb the stairs and remain there without daily personal care. Both are legitimate outcomes, but only one is captured by operative mortality. Ask the patient to name the ability or activity worth preserving and the burdens they would accept to achieve it. That answer changes which risks the preoperative discussion needs to quantify (p399, p403–404).

The chapter places this question in a broad US context: older adults account for **more than 40% of inpatient operations** and **33% of outpatient operations** in the estimates cited. These proportions describe the burden of surgical care, not a probability that any particular older person will need surgery. Outcomes matter across procedures of very different intensity, from a planned outpatient intervention to emergency major abdominal surgery. Always specify the setting and population before transferring a risk estimate to the person in front of you (p399–400).

Useful questions before consent include what the patient can currently do without help, who depends on them, who would help them during recovery and how they would view a prolonged need for assistance. Someone who already receives help with bathing may still value independent transfers very highly. Someone caring for a spouse may consider even a temporary period away from home a major burden. An assessment that records only whether the person lives alone cannot capture these distinctions. In the chapter's model, baseline function, support, preferences and objective risk are complementary inputs to the decision (p399, p403–404).

### Traditional risk estimates: use the right comparison

Traditional outcomes include perioperative mortality, complications, length of stay, readmission and discharge destination. They remain useful because an infectious complication or prolonged admission has real clinical consequences. Their limitation is that they often stop early in a much longer trajectory. A patient can leave hospital promptly to a facility where they spend months, or have no readmission while family members take on substantial unpaid care. A short stay cannot be interpreted as recovery without knowing where the patient went, what help they needed and whether they returned to their previous function (p399–402).

Compare like operations and distinguish the starting populations. In the chapter's major gastrointestinal surgery example, a large proportion of patients older than **75 years** remained hospitalized after **7 days**. A prolonged stay in this setting may reflect complications, reduced reserve or difficulty arranging an appropriate destination. It is not the same outcome as subsequent dependence. Likewise, an outpatient operation followed by an unplanned admission is a failure of the expected outpatient course even though the patient may have a relatively short total stay. The clinical meaning depends on what course was anticipated (p400).

Frailty measures reduced reserve across multiple systems. An anesthetic physical-status classification captures a different aspect of risk. Two people of the same age undergoing the same operation can therefore have markedly different prospects for regaining function. In one **180-patient** gastric cancer surgical study, postoperative mortality was **23% in frail patients** and **5% in fit patients**. This is evidence of a risk gradient in that cohort. It neither assigns every frail patient a one-in-four probability of death nor establishes a level of frailty at which surgery must be refused (p400).

Frailty instruments differ. A systematic review described in the chapter used **21 instruments across 23 studies** and found an association with adverse outcomes across different operations and follow-up periods. Consistency in the direction of risk does not make the instruments or the reported effect sizes interchangeable. Check what the instrument measured, whether it was applied before surgery, which outcome was studied and whether the patient resembles that cohort. In an outpatient discussion, the relevant consequence may be failure of same-day discharge; after inpatient surgery, new institutional care or mortality may be among the more pressing concerns (p400).

Geriatric complications also need explicit ascertainment. An event such as dehydration, delirium, failure to thrive, a pressure injury or a fall may disrupt recovery even when the operation itself is judged successful. In the analysis of surgical admissions discussed on p400, **2.4%** had a recorded geriatric event, varying from **1.0%** for radical prostatectomy to **10.3%** for aortic valve replacement. The proportions refer to the conditions captured in those data; actively screening a different group for delirium may give a very different result. Do not compare these numbers as if both studies used the same assessment (p400, p402).

### What co-management changes before and after surgery

In the age-friendly framework, **what matters**, medication, mentation and mobility provide different questions for the team. What matters identifies the goal. Medication review may reveal treatments that increase postoperative confusion or impede mobilization. Mentation includes baseline cognition and the risk of an acute change. Mobility affects both immediate safety and the ability to return home. Multicomplexity runs across all four. The framework becomes clinically useful when a concern identified before surgery triggers a specific perioperative action and a plan for later follow-up (p400–401, p403).

Hip-fracture co-management illustrates three different service structures. On an orthopedic ward a geriatrician may routinely consult, while the orthopedic service retains primary responsibility. On a geriatric ward the surgeon may consult on a patient under geriatric care. In an integrated shared-care model the surgeon and geriatrician share responsibility. These are arrangements for delivering care, not three different operations. When reading a comparison, ask how early input began, who implemented recommendations and which outcome improved. The chapter notes that benefits reported across studies were not identical (p400–401).

An elective cancer surgery study adds another distinction. Among patients aged **75 or older**, estimated **90-day mortality was 4.3% with geriatric co-management and 8.9% with surgical-service care alone**. Recorded adverse surgical events did not differ significantly. The co-managed group received more rehabilitation, swallowing and nutrition services. Those findings are compatible with a benefit that operates through how patients are supported after surgery, but an observational comparison cannot establish which individual service caused the survival difference (p401).

Enhanced recovery after surgery pathways span preparation, the procedure itself and the postoperative period. A procedure-specific pathway can reduce complications and length of stay while still requiring adaptation to a person with impaired cognition, frailty or an unusual home situation. A patient who cannot remember instructions without cueing needs a different plan for mobilization and medications than someone who can follow a written schedule independently. The goal of geriatric input is to make the pathway usable for this patient, not simply to add more consultations (p401, p403).

### Function as a trajectory, not a discharge checkbox

Measure a baseline that will still have meaning at follow-up. The Precipitating Events Project followed community-dwelling older people initially independent in bathing, dressing, walking and transfers. It also assessed more complex tasks, including shopping, preparing meals, taking medicines and handling finances. These domains can recover at different speeds. Someone who can walk a short distance in a supervised ward may still be unable to travel to a shop, carry groceries or manage an unfamiliar medication schedule after returning home (p401).

In that project, among **266 major surgical episodes** followed by an initial increase in disability, **65.4%** had recovered to their presurgical functional level by **6 months**, **28.6%** remained alive below baseline, and **6%** had died. The denominator is the group with initially worse function after surgery. It excludes operations followed by no initial increase in disability, and repeated operations could occur in the same participant. If asked about likelihood of recovery, name both the starting condition and the time point instead of treating these results as the prognosis of every older surgical patient (p401).

Elective surgery and absence of frailty were associated with a greater likelihood of recovery. Intervening illnesses and injuries were also strongly associated with functional decline. An older patient can improve during the first weeks, experience an unrelated admission and lose much of the gain. Asking only how the incision healed misses the pathway to disability. During follow-up, ask about emergency visits, periods of restricted activity and new assistance as well as complications the surgeon would normally record (p401).

In SAGES the impact of readmission extended beyond admissions specifically caused by surgery. Hospitalization brings immobility, interrupted sleep, nutritional problems and psychological stress. Repeated exposure can accumulate over time. A patient who misses rehabilitation because of an intercurrent illness may return to the same joint or abdominal examination but with a different ability to manage at home. Reassess functional recovery after each interruption and update the forecast rather than assuming the original discharge plan is still achievable (p401).

Loss of independence is a composite of several changes. In the geriatric surgery pilot cohort, **26%** had a decline in functional status, **30%** had declining mobility, **27.8%** needed additional home support and **18.2%** were discharged somewhere other than home. These categories overlap. A single patient who needs a walker and new assistance can meet more than one criterion. The reported **60%** with some loss of independence should not be obtained by adding the individual percentages, or described as the percentage permanently entering a nursing facility (p402).

This outcome varied with age within that study: **50% at ages 65–74**, **67% at 75–84** and **84% at 85 or older** experienced some loss of independence at discharge. The age gradient is relevant for planning, but the individual conversation still requires baseline frailty, operation type and social resources. A resident already receiving substantial support might value preservation of a single remaining independent transfer, while someone living alone needs to know whether a temporary helper could make home discharge possible. The same composite event may carry quite different consequences for them (p402–403).

### Delirium, complications and postoperative cognition

Identify the preoperative cognitive baseline and discuss it with someone who knows the patient. New fluctuating inattention after surgery calls for assessment for delirium rather than a conclusion that a long-term dementia has suddenly declared itself. The chapter emphasizes that perioperative care changes can prevent up to **40%** of delirium in some settings. This is a prevention estimate, not a forecast that forty percent of all surgical patients become delirious. A study measuring cumulative delirium incidence answers a different question (p402).

In a cohort of older patients after elective major noncardiac surgery, **24%** developed delirium and **8.3%** a major postoperative complication. Delirium was associated with longer stay, institutional discharge or readmission, and the combination of delirium with a major complication identified a particularly vulnerable group. Neither outcome is simply a synonym for the other. A patient with an uncomplicated operation can still have clinically consequential delirium, while someone with a surgical complication may remain cognitively clear. Check for both, and tell the next team if delirium affected the interpretation of current function (p402).

Longer-term postoperative cognitive change requires a baseline, an interval and a definition of decline. A test in the immediate postoperative period can be affected by delirium or transient illness. Later testing can identify persistence or improvement but must be interpreted in the context of the underlying disease, preexisting impairment and intercurrent events. The studies reviewed do not establish that an early episode of delirium and all later cognitive decline represent one continuous process in every patient. Families often want a binary forecast; explain which change has been observed and which part of the trajectory is still uncertain (p402).

### Quality of life and time at home

Quality of life is the patient's judgment across several domains. It can deteriorate immediately after surgery as pain, fatigue and dependence increase, then recover even if a measurable limitation remains. The individual may value relief of cancer symptoms or anxiety sufficiently to describe overall quality of life as better than before. Conversely, an operation with excellent technical results may leave a person dissatisfied if the expected functional gain never occurs. Measure what the person reported before the operation and use the same domains when reviewing recovery (p402–403).

A pancreatic resection comparison found physical, functional and overall quality-of-life scores fell initially in both laparoscopic and open groups and returned to baseline by **6 months**. Severe complications produced larger declines regardless of operative approach. The study does not show that a smaller incision guarantees a better long-term patient-reported outcome. The choice of approach should be discussed in relation to the likelihood of a safe operation, the early burden of recovery and the outcomes most meaningful to the patient (p403).

Time at home combines survival with where that time is spent. In the cancer-surgery comparison, the likelihood of time at home differed by procedure; breast and gastrointestinal surgery lay at different ends of the distribution. Among survivors, institutional time could increase later as well as immediately after surgery. Factors associated with less time at home included frailty, socioeconomic circumstances, rural residence and the intensity and site of surgery. An apparent difference between procedures may partly reflect these differences in the populations undergoing them (p403).

A colorectal and gastric surgery cohort illustrates why the timing of the interview changes what the patient reports. Adults aged **75 or older** were assessed before surgery and again at one, three and six months. At **1 month**, **24%** had a decline in activities of daily living; at **6 months**, **3%** were still below their prior ADL level. Group quality-of-life scores initially fell and then recovered to at least the preoperative level. This gives a different picture from a study that stops follow-up at discharge. It also leaves a subgroup with persistent loss for whom the group average offers little reassurance (p402).

The cognitive follow-up study asked another question. It compared preoperative memory, executive function and processing speed with performance **3 months** after oncologic surgery. Of **219 patients**, **12%** showed decline by the study definition. A new need for physical help is therefore neither necessary nor sufficient to identify cognitive decline. When a family reports that recovery is incomplete, ask which mental or physical task has changed, whether it was assessed before surgery and when the change first became apparent (p402).

### Put the risk estimate into a plan

The ACS NSQIP calculator described in the chapter estimated **12 outcomes at 30 days** from **21 preoperative predictors**. The geriatric pilot added six predictors: living situation, falls, mobility aid, cognitive impairment, surrogate-signed consent and palliative care on admission. Four additional outcomes were pressure injury, delirium, new mobility-aid use and functional decline. The added predictors are neither complications nor automatic reasons to avoid operating. They help estimate consequences that a patient contemplating a difficult recovery may value more than a generic morbidity percentage (p403).

Figure 29-1 places risk calculation between initial assessment and a personalized plan. Consider a person who values returning home above all but already needs a mobility aid and has been falling. Before surgery, involve the caregiver, identify what help could realistically be provided, and ask which decline would make the procedure unacceptable. During and after the operation, address medication, cognition and mobility and coordinate geriatric care where available. After discharge, follow whether independence returned and whether new illnesses interrupted it. A risk discussion is incomplete if it identifies the possibility of disability but never establishes how the team will recognize and respond to it (p403–404).

Finally, compare the expected course *with and without* surgery in terms the patient uses. For one person surgery may offer the best chance to relieve symptoms and preserve independence. Another may decline the prospect of prolonged rehabilitation despite a technically operable cancer. The chapter explicitly allows for both choices. A decision to forgo surgery is coherent when its expected burdens conflict with the person's priorities, just as advanced age alone does not disqualify a fit older patient who accepts the risks. Success is judged against the goals agreed before treatment (p399–400, p404).

## Exam traps

- Frailty and the anesthetic physical-status classification measure different aspects of risk; a reassuring score does not establish resilience (p400).
- Discharge alive and a short initial stay can coexist with a new need for mobility assistance or personal care (p399–402).
- Distinguish the proportion with postoperative delirium from the proportion of delirium episodes potentially preventable (p402).
