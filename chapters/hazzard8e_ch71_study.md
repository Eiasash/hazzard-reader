# Chapter 71 Study: Effective Communication Strategies For Patients with Serious Illness

## Summary

**Prepare and understand**
- Goals-of-care conversations are often wanted but do not happen; conduct them over time, not only during a crisis (p1089-1090).
- Arrange privacy, adequate time and the patient's chosen supports; clarify relevant medical information beforehand (p1090).
- Ask what the person understands and how much information and decision-making involvement they want (p1090-1091).
- Use ASK-TELL-ASK: establish understanding, explain clearly in small portions, then check comprehension (p1090-1091).

**Values, emotion and decisions**
- Explore hopes, worries, meaning and acceptable quality of life before matching treatments to goals (p1091).
- Respond to emotion with NURSE: name, understand, respect, support and explore (p1091).
- Explain options in relation to the person's priorities, check understanding and offer a recommendation when helpful (p1091-1092).
- Summarize the plan, next steps and goals; reassure the person that care and support will continue (p1092).
- Acknowledge prognostic uncertainty, use ranges and distinguish population estimates from an individual's course (p1093-1094).

**Adapt communication**
- For hearing loss, face the patient, reduce background noise, speak clearly and slowly, support lip-reading and use hearing aids/amplification (p1092).
- For memory difficulty, offer small, separate pieces of information and repeat as needed (p1092).
- Include supporters while continuing to address the patient directly and allowing time to answer (p1093).
- With incapacity, review prior wishes; use substituted judgment when possible and best interests when preferences cannot reasonably be established (p1093).

**Difficult situations**
- SPIKES structures difficult news: setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, empathy, then summary/strategy (p1095).
- Explain the expected course and feeding options in advanced dementia; the chapter finds tube feeding does not improve survival, aspiration or pressure-injury outcomes (p1094).
- Discuss resuscitation, intensive care and transitions in light of the patient’s goals. Table 71-6 gives examples of offering hospice or symptom-focused care at home (p1092); Table 71-8 describes how to discuss difficult news and agree on next steps (p1095).
- Document and revisit preferences as illness and circumstances change (p1095).

### What to say when the conversation stalls

- In studies summarized by the chapter, **61% to 91%** of older participants wanted to discuss end-of-life care with their clinicians. Patients saw a chance to spare loved ones uncertainty later. Ask whom to include, whether the patient wants detailed prognostic information, and when to return to the discussion (p1089–1091).
- When someone reacts to bad news with silence or anger, pause the explanation. Identify the emotion, acknowledge it and ask what is most frightening. Once that concern is understood, check whether the person is ready for more information or wants help with a decision (p1091, p1095).
- Anchor surrogate decisions in the patient's prior words, values or advance directive. Asking what the patient would choose can ease the sense that a relative must make a personal choice about someone else's life. If there is no reasonable basis to infer a choice, discuss benefits and burdens for this patient (p1092–1093).
- In one study of **323** proxies for people with advanced dementia, **40%** recalled making at least one medical decision. Feeding and infections were common subjects. Explain in advance that feeding difficulty may worsen as part of dementia, and discuss what careful hand feeding can offer (p1092–1094).

### Open a discussion while the patient can shape it

In chronic heart or lung disease and dementia, decline may be punctuated by temporary improvement after an admission. Waiting for a clearly identifiable terminal phase can leave little time to learn the patient's priorities before the next crisis. A discussion can begin when there is no immediate procedure to accept or refuse. It can establish whom the person wants present, what outcomes they consider acceptable and how they would like decisions made if they become less able to participate. Later discussions can update that picture as function, symptoms and available support change (p1089–1090).

The chapter contrasts willingness with actual experience. In the review cited, **61%–91%** of older adults wanted to discuss end-of-life care. Across other studies only an estimated **2%–29%** of frail older adults had discussed such plans with a health professional. These figures come from different studies and are not a single before-and-after comparison. In **60 recorded discussions** about advance directives, physicians spoke for **70%** of the time and explored values and goals in detail less than one-third of the time. Completing a document or speaking for a long time may therefore coexist with knowing little about the outcome the patient most values (p1089).

Table 71-1 matches barriers to specific changes in how a visit is conducted. When time is short, reserve a visit for the discussion and address a manageable part today. When clinicians fear causing offense, introduce the topic as a routine part of planning and ask permission. When prognosis is uncertain, acknowledge what is unknown and prepare to discuss a best, worst and most likely course. When the obstacle is clinician discomfort with the conversation itself, training and practice can help. A prognostic calculator may inform a range, but neither the calculator nor a form establishes how much the patient wants to know (p1090).

### Prepare for the conversation and learn how the patient sees the illness

Before entering the room, know the relevant medical facts and why the conversation matters now. If several clinicians are involved, clarify what has been observed and where opinions remain uncertain. Arrange seats where faces are visible, reduce interruption and ask the patient whom to include. A relative may provide comfort or practical knowledge, but their presence should reflect the patient's wishes. If the visit occurs in hospital, privacy and a working hearing aid may matter more for understanding than adding another clinician. Acknowledge when time is limited and agree what can be addressed today and what will be revisited (p1090, p1092–1093).

Table 71-2 begins with the patient's understanding. An open invitation to describe what has changed and what other clinicians have said often reveals the important gap. One person may understand that the illness is serious but believe the next treatment will restore baseline function; another may think a change in medication means all disease treatment is ending. Ask what the patient thinks has caused the recent deterioration. Reflect the account back before correcting it, so that the patient can clarify a misunderstanding and the team does not spend the limited visit explaining facts the person already knows (p1090–1091).

Table 71-3 asks two separate questions. How much information does this patient want, and how much responsibility do they want for the medical choice? A person may want explicit prognostic information yet prefer a recommendation. Another may wish a family member to receive detailed figures while still stating which outcomes they would find acceptable. Check the intended arrangement with the patient rather than assuming that accepting family help amounts to giving up a role in the decision. A preference for less detail can also change: offer another opportunity to talk as the illness or the choice becomes more immediate (p1090–1091, p1093).

### Use Ask-Tell-Ask to find the next piece of information

The first “ask” identifies the person's starting point and the information they want. The “tell” should be short enough for them to respond to and should address the question they actually asked. The second “ask” explores what they took from the explanation and what remains confusing. For example, if a patient hopes another hospitalization will cure a progressive illness, explain what admission might improve and which part of the disease is likely to continue. Pause and ask how that fits with their understanding. A correction embedded in a long technical lecture makes it hard to discover whether the central point was heard (p1090–1091).

If an important misunderstanding persists, shorten the explanation and change the language rather than repeating the same jargon. A patient may use “better” to mean less short of breath, returning home or restoring a lost ability. Ask which meaning they have in mind. Then explain a proposed treatment in those terms. Asking the patient to describe the plan in their own words tests how well the clinician conveyed it; framing the check as the clinician's responsibility avoids making a vulnerable person feel examined. An unresolved question can become the purpose of the next visit rather than being silently treated as agreement (p1090–1092).

### Elicit a goal that can guide an actual choice

Table 71-4 divides the exploration into the lived burden of illness, what gives life meaning, and hopes and fears about the future. A question about the hardest part of the last admission may elicit inability to recognize familiar people, separation from a partner or loss of mobility. Follow the answer far enough to learn what would make a burdensome treatment worthwhile. “Independence” can mean walking to the bathroom alone for one patient and participating in family decisions despite needing hands-on assistance for another. An account in the patient's own words is more actionable than an isolated label such as quality of life (p1091).

Explore more than one possible outcome. The patient may hope to live longer and also fear another intensive care stay. Neither statement, in isolation, explains how to choose when a treatment might prolong life but requires hospital care. Discuss the expected benefit, burden and uncertainty, then ask which consequences weigh most heavily. A person may reasonably accept treatment despite a small chance of recovering a valued ability, or decline it because the foreseeable burden matters more. The clinician should be able to explain the reasoning behind the plan when discussing it with a colleague who was not present (p1091–1092).

### Respond to emotion before trying to settle the decision

A change in facial expression, a long silence or a relative answering abruptly may indicate that information has become difficult to absorb. Table 71-5 offers several responses. Naming tentatively identifies an emotion and leaves room for correction. Understanding acknowledges why it makes sense in the situation. Respect can recognize the work of caring for a spouse or the difficulty of weighing two bad outcomes. Support commits the clinician or team to ongoing care. Exploration invites the person to say which part worries them most. Use the response that fits the moment rather than reciting the mnemonic as a script (p1091).

For example, when a daughter repeatedly asks about another intervention after learning that her father's condition is worsening, first establish what she fears will happen without it. If she believes declining the procedure would mean leaving him in distress, a description of ongoing symptom treatment is relevant. If the worry is that she would be responsible for his death, returning to his own prior preferences is more helpful than another list of procedural complications. A quiet pause is often necessary before the concern can be named. After acknowledging it, ask whether the family wants more information now or would prefer time to process it (p1091–1093).

### Make a recommendation that follows from what the patient said

Table 71-6 begins by restating the main priority and checking its accuracy. Offer a recommendation only after learning whether the patient wants that role from the clinician. If the person prioritizes a chance of returning to current function and would accept another hospital stay to achieve it, explain which treatment could plausibly support that aim. If the main priority is relief of distress at home, explain how a home-based symptom plan or, when appropriate, hospice could serve it. A recommendation should include what care will be provided, how it serves the patient and what happens next. Avoid presenting a list of procedures as though the patient had been asked to choose from a menu (p1091–1092).

Recheck understanding at the end of the meeting. Summarize the clinical situation, the goal, the decision made and any question still open. Ask what the patient or supporter will tell the rest of the family. Identify who will follow up and how to obtain help if symptoms worsen. A recommendation against a burdensome intervention should be accompanied by an affirmative account of ongoing care. Reassurance that the team will continue to care for the person is especially important after bad news, a decision to stay at home or a decision about resuscitation (p1092, p1095).

### Make participation possible when hearing or memory is impaired

If the patient repeatedly gives an unrelated answer, check hearing before interpreting it as disorientation or unwillingness to engage. Face the person, control background conversation, support use of a hearing aid or amplifier and allow time for lip-reading. A clinician speaking toward a computer while a family member talks simultaneously has made a meaningful choice difficult to hear. For memory difficulty, put one idea in each brief statement, repeat key points and recheck comprehension. Adaptation can allow the patient to express consistent values and preferences even when they cannot retain every technical detail discussed (p1092–1093).

Supporters may describe what the patient can do at home, explain established priorities, help remember the plan or participate by invitation. Make eye contact with the patient, direct questions to them and allow enough time for their answer before asking the relative. If the relative repeatedly talks over the person, redirect respectfully. Separate inability to hear, difficulty recalling a long explanation and inability to make the particular decision. The last of these may require a surrogate; the first two first call for improving communication. Reassess participation as the situation changes, for example when an acute delirium improves (p1092–1093).

When the person lacks capacity for the decision, look first for applicable previously expressed wishes, including an advance directive. If the specific situation was never described, invite the surrogate to infer a choice from the patient's values and prior decisions. This is substituted judgment. Ask what the patient valued enough to tolerate hardship for and what outcomes they had previously said they would find unacceptable. If there is no reasonable basis for inferring a choice, turn to the person's best interests, weighing benefits and burdens for them. The surrogate's own preferences for their personal medical care are a different question (p1092–1093).

### Give prognosis without false precision

Before discussing time, establish whether the person wants an estimate and what they need it for. Someone arranging a family visit may need a different level of detail from someone choosing between another operation and returning home. Table 71-7 allows a clinician to describe uncertainty openly and to give ranges such as weeks to months when that is the precision the evidence supports. Explicitly identify an estimate derived from groups with similar illness. A group median does not assign an exact date or even a single most important outcome to one person with several interacting conditions (p1093–1094).

A best-case, worst-case and most likely description can connect the medical trajectory to function: where the patient might live, what help could be needed and whether another acute event is foreseeable. Ask how the person feels hearing that there is uncertainty. An effort to narrow the range beyond the evidence may miss the actual issue, such as whether the patient can make a plan now. After addressing distress, identify one action that is useful across possible futures, such as telling a surrogate what would matter most or organizing additional support at home. Give an opportunity to revisit the estimate when the course becomes clearer (p1090, p1093–1094).

### SPIKES: distinguish giving the news from planning the response

Table 71-8 organizes difficult news, including progressive disease, a poor treatment outcome or loss of a valued activity. Setup includes preparation, privacy and attention to the clinician's own reaction. Perception and invitation establish what the patient understands and how they want to receive further information. Knowledge is conveyed plainly, in a short statement, followed by a pause. The table suggests **10–15 seconds** to let the information register. Continuing to explain through the first silence can prevent the patient from showing what they understood or what emotion needs attention (p1094–1095).

The empathy step makes room for that reaction before the team discusses another intervention. Acknowledge disappointment or sadness without promising an unlikely recovery. Then summarize and strategize: check what the news means to the patient, explain the next concrete step and ensure access to continuing support. In the studies cited, **75%–90%** wanted difficult news, but the appropriate method and amount of detail still depend on the individual. A wish to hear information does not imply that the person is ready to choose immediately after receiving it (p1094–1095).

### Apply these skills to feeding and escalation in advanced dementia

Feeding difficulty in advanced dementia may include refusal, dysphagia or repeated aspiration. Explain that it can reflect progression of the disease and may worsen. Ask what the family hopes a tube would accomplish. They may hope for longer survival, prevention of aspiration, closure of a pressure injury or relief from the distress of seeing a relative eat less. Each hope leads to a specific discussion of evidence and an alternative plan. The chapter compares careful hand feeding with tube feeding in *advanced dementia*, generally involving profound functional impairment and limited speech. Do not apply that comparison to every patient with dysphagia from any cause (p1094–1095).

In the evidence reviewed, tube feeding offered no improvement in survival, aspiration pneumonia, function or comfort compared with careful hand feeding. It was associated with burdens such as agitation, restraints, emergency visits, hospitalization and pressure ulcers. Describe how hand feeding can continue in a way attentive to the patient's comfort and cues. A family hearing only that a tube will not be inserted may understandably interpret the plan as withholding all food or support. Asking them to explain the proposed care in their own words can uncover that misunderstanding immediately (p1094–1095).

In the proxy study, feeding decisions were recalled by **27%** and infection decisions by **21%**. Discuss predictable problems early enough for supporters to think about a future episode outside an emergency. In the nursing-home study described, understanding the prognosis and likely complications of advanced dementia was associated with fewer burdensome interventions near death. This association supports honest education; its purpose is to facilitate a choice consistent with the patient, not to secure a preselected answer. At the end of life, document preferences about resuscitation, intensive care or transfer together with the reasoning and positive care plan, and revisit them if health or preferences change (p1092–1095).

Ask about the preferred place of care or death separately from a decision about resuscitation. A person may still want hospital treatment for a reversible problem while preferring to be at home near death. Invite the chosen surrogate into this discussion when possible. Hearing the patient's reasoning may preserve details that a written directive cannot capture, particularly if a later situation differs from the one originally imagined (p1095).

## Exam traps

- A preference about how much information to receive and a preference about who recommends or decides are separate questions (p1090–1091).
- Use a prognostic range with the individual's goals and illness pattern. A population median cannot supply a precise date for one patient (p1093–1094).
- In the advanced-dementia comparison, careful hand feeding was the active comparator; the alternative to a tube was not withdrawal of care (p1094–1095).

- Ask how much prognosis the patient wants to hear before quoting a survival estimate (p1090–1091, p1093–1094).
- A family member's presence should facilitate communication with a patient who can participate (p1093).
- Discuss careful hand feeding as active care when weighing feeding tubes in advanced dementia (p1094).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Review the medical issues, arrange a quiet and private setting, allow time and invite the supporters the patient wants (p1090).</details>

**3. What should precede a detailed explanation of prognosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask what the patient already understands and how much more they wish to know (p1090–1091, p1093–1094).</details>

**4. What does Ask-Tell-Ask accomplish?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Establish current understanding, give a small amount of clear information, then ask what the patient understood or wants clarified (p1090–1091).</details>

**5. Which questions reveal a person's priorities before a treatment decision?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask what they hope for, what they fear and what degree of function or quality of life matters (p1091).</details>

**6. How can the clinician respond to strong emotion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use NURSE: name the emotion, show understanding and respect, offer support and explore its source (p1091).</details>

**7. What makes a recommendation patient-centered?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>With permission, explain how the option serves the patient's expressed goals and check their response (p1091–1092).</details>

**8. How can hearing impairment be accommodated?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Face the patient, reduce background noise, speak slowly and clearly and use available hearing aids or amplification (p1092).</details>

**9. What helps someone with memory impairment follow a discussion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Present one idea at a time, use short sentences, repeat important points and check understanding (p1092).</details>

**10. What is substituted judgment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The surrogate uses the patient's own prior preferences and values to infer what that person would choose (p1093).</details>

**11. When is a best-interests decision appropriate?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When prior wishes and values provide no reasonable basis for inferring the patient's choice (p1093).</details>

**12. How should an uncertain prognosis be expressed?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>State the uncertainty, give a range when useful and explain that group averages cannot predict one person's exact course (p1093–1094).</details>

**13. What outcomes should be discussed when a family asks about a feeding tube in advanced dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Careful hand feeding yields similar or sometimes better survival, aspiration, function and comfort outcomes in the evidence discussed (p1094).</details>

**14. What does SPIKES provide during disclosure of bad news?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A sequence for setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, empathy and a shared summary or strategy (p1095).</details>

**15. What should the patient leave the meeting knowing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The agreed goals, next steps, who will follow up, and that care and support will continue (p1092, p1095).</details>
