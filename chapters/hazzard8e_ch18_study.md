# Chapter 18 Study: Transitions of Care

## Summary

**Why transitions fail**
- Transitions move responsibility between teams or settings; fragmentation, resource differences and poor communication create safety risks (p249-252).
- Medication discrepancies/errors occur in **30–50%** of patients after hospitalization; pending tests and changes in function or diet can also be lost (p250).
- Match the receiving setting to clinical, functional and social needs; postacute services differ in staffing, capability and payment (p251-253).
- Involve patients and caregivers early, especially when cognition limits learning or recall. Elicit goals, preferences and practical capacity (p255).

**Make the handoff usable**
- Prepare patients/caregivers to manage medications, attend follow-up, recognize worsening symptoms and know whom to contact (p256).
- Reinforce simple, tailored education before and after discharge. Involve caregivers and observe the patient performing the task over time to check understanding (Table 18-5, p256).
- Communicate promptly with the receiving team. Include diagnoses, treatments, pending results, medication changes, functional needs, goals and follow-up responsibilities (p253, p256-257).
- Reconcile actual current use with intended prescriptions: obtain lists, compare, resolve discrepancies, then communicate the final plan (p257).
- Use the patient, caregivers, prior records, prescribers and pharmacy; include nonprescription products and adherence problems (p257).

**Models and vulnerable groups**
- CTI and TCM combine home follow-up with self-management support; the chapter reports about one-third fewer rehospitalizations, with effects lasting up to **180 days** (p258).
- Home-visit programs depend on willingness to accept home support and geographic reach; alternatives include telephone-based coordination (p258).
- Project RED reduced emergency visits but did not significantly reduce rehospitalizations in its trial; its younger study population limits extrapolation (p258).
- INTERACT supports nursing-home recognition, assessment and communication of clinical change; implementation and measured effects vary (p259).
- Cognitive impairment increases vulnerability during transitions. Communicate baseline cognition and behavioral symptoms to the receiving team and weigh the benefit of transfer against the harm of an unfamiliar setting (p253, p257, p259–260).
- Discuss goals and advance planning near end of life; earlier planning may reduce burdensome transitions. Adapt education and caregiver support to dementia severity (p259-260).

### Make the receiving team's first day safe

- A discharge order is useful only if it can be carried out. A skilled nursing facility may lack an on-site pharmacy or centralized equipment supply. Tell staff promptly about a last-minute change, reconcile conflicting versions of the summary and check that essential medicines and equipment will be available (p256–257).
- Discrepancy can arise when the patient returns to an old supply at home, follows an earlier list or cannot obtain the new prescription. Elicit what they actually take, include over-the-counter products, resolve differences with the prescriber, and explain the final list in language the patient or caregiver can use (p256–257).
- Care transitions involve learning as well as sending information. Have the patient or caregiver demonstrate the task, identify symptoms that should prompt action and explain how to contact the responsible clinician. Reinforce the same plan after discharge (p256).
- Project RED strengthened hospital discharge processes and reduced **30-day** emergency visits in its trial, without a significant rehospitalization effect. Participants averaged **50 years** of age. C-TraC instead uses a nurse case manager and intensive postdischarge telephone contact; its early rehospitalization results required more rigorous testing (p258).

### Trace the change in responsibility, including within a hospital

Table 18-3 includes emergency department to ICU, ICU to ward, hospital to home or a skilled nursing facility, and transfers from nursing facilities to clinics, hospital or hospice. A change of building is not required: moving from closely monitored intensive care to a ward changes available nursing attention and who interprets the next change in condition. Conversely, a patient returning to the same home may now require another person to supervise medicines or assist transfers. Describe the change in monitoring and responsibility as well as the change in address (p249–254).

Figure 18-1 follows a hip-fracture patient from home through hospital and skilled rehabilitation, then to primary care and home therapy. Diabetes, heart failure and anticoagulation services continue to make decisions in parallel. Each clinician may see only one part of the sequence. A dose adjusted in one clinic can appear to be a mistake to the next clinician if the rationale and monitoring plan do not travel with it. The patient or family can then become the only link between teams precisely when illness, unfamiliar surroundings or cognitive impairment make that role least realistic (p252–254).

The chapter gives two examples of what is lost. Approximately one-third of patients left with pending tests that were not communicated to the next setting in the evidence, and at least **10%** of those could have led to a change in care. Listing “culture pending” is only the beginning: specify who will see the final result, what clinical choice depends on it and how the receiving clinician can be reached. A correct list of medicines can likewise become unsafe if it does not say which drugs were stopped, when the next dose is due or who should monitor the change (p250, p253, p256–257).

### Match the destination to what has to happen between clinical visits

Table 18-1 compares U.S. postacute settings by the services actually available. A long-term care hospital has daily physician input and a multidisciplinary staff for persistent medical complexity. An inpatient rehabilitation facility provides intensive rehabilitation; the table describes **3 hours per day, 5 days per week** as the usual qualifying intensity and an average stay of **12 days**. Someone with a potentially reversible major functional loss may benefit if able to sustain that schedule. A patient who still needs acute management that exceeds available monitoring or who cannot participate at that intensity needs a different plan, even when the family prefers an institution called “rehabilitation” (p251–253).

A skilled nursing facility can combine ongoing nursing with therapy for a patient who needs more help with daily activities. Staffing and physician availability differ from an acute hospital. Table 18-1 illustrates this with physician visits at least every **30 days** during the first **90 days**, then every **60 days** in its U.S. framework. In many facilities the average stay was **14–25 days**, although the individual course varies. Orders must therefore work between medical visits, and staff must have a way to reach someone who can respond to deterioration. A skilled stay and long-term custodial residence can occur in similar buildings but answer different functional and medical needs (p251–253).

Home health can provide intermittent visits for a homebound patient; it does not supply a caregiver around the clock. Assisted living offers a residential setting with variable services and often limited licensed nursing staff. Hospice can provide symptom-focused care in the home or in a designated inpatient setting when appropriate, but its availability does not automatically solve a family's need for continuous custodial help at home. In the book's U.S. table, hospice eligibility includes an estimated prognosis under **6 months**. Determine who can actually transfer the patient at night, prepare food, supervise medicines and recognize a problem between visits before using any setting's name as assurance of safety (p251–253, p255).

> Current practice (not the book): US Medicare hospice eligibility uses an expected prognosis of six months or less if the illness follows its normal course. This is a clinical certification; survival beyond six months does not itself end eligibility if the patient still meets the criteria. [CMS hospice manual, 2026](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/r13664bp.pdf).

The chapter describes how payment rules and incentives can reshape services. Shorter acute stays increase the number of patients whose treatment or rehabilitation must continue elsewhere. Readmission-related incentives and payment for coordinated transitional care can encourage stronger links across teams, but reimbursement definitions do not guarantee that an individual facility has the staff, medicine or equipment needed today. Table 18-1 illustrates the historical U.S. fee-for-service skilled benefit after a qualifying inpatient stay, with a maximum **100 days** per qualifying episode; it does not promise a funded 100-day stay for every admission. Establish the actual clinical need, local availability and the patient's coverage when arranging care (p250–252).

### Ask the receiving nurse what is needed to deliver care on arrival

Table 18-2 uses the receiving team's perspective. Supply contact details for the sending unit, responsible clinician, relevant specialists, nurse familiar with the patient, family or support person and authorized surrogate where applicable. Include the reason for admission, significant hospital events and code status. A short explanation of why a drug was started, stopped or changed is more actionable than a long list of inpatient events. For a time-sensitive medicine, include the last administration and when the next is expected. Communicate drug, food and other relevant allergies or intolerances rather than leaving the nurse to interpret an unexplained historical entry (p253, p256–257).

Describe what the patient needs another person to do. “Mobilize as tolerated” does not tell the receiving team whether rising from bed requires a device, one assistant or a more intensive transfer. Specify mobility equipment, fall risk, assistance with activities, sensory aids and whether dentures or hearing aids are accompanying the patient. Include the type of wound dressing and schedule, line care, continence or catheter status, last bowel movement, swallowing or dietary instructions and devices used at meals. The destination may have to arrange special supplies in advance; a last-minute discharge revision is medically important if the revised plan cannot be implemented until the next working day (p253, p256–257).

Effective handover of cognition goes beyond a diagnosis. Describe the usual baseline, any acute change, what the person can understand, behaviors that affected care, and calming or reorientation strategies that actually worked. A patient who accepts a dressing change after an explanation from a familiar relative may be recorded simply as “resistant” if no one passes on that detail. Tell receiving staff whether an attendant was needed and for which circumstance. Such information affects staffing and can help prevent a change of environment from being mistaken for a new psychiatric or medical diagnosis (p253, p257, p259–260).

### Use patient and caregiver experience to test the plan

Table 18-4 describes people who received instructions written for clinicians, caregivers surprised by changes or costs, families willing to help at home but unaware of available services, and patients unsure what question to ask. A person may say there are no questions because they cannot yet anticipate what will happen when the pharmacy is closed, not because the plan is understood. Ask them to describe the first evening after discharge, including how they will obtain medicines, eat, get to the bathroom and summon help. A difficulty that emerges in this account may change the destination or timing of discharge (p254–256).

Distinguish willingness from capacity when inviting relatives to help. Wanting the patient home does not establish that the caregiver can perform a transfer, administer a new medicine, take time off work or attend a distant clinic. Ask what tasks they performed before the illness and what is newly required. Clarify the patient's own preferences before assuming the family will receive information or make decisions. If the patient has communication or memory difficulties, adapt the explanation and include the supporter who will actually be present; participation and surrogate decision-making are different roles (p253–256, p259–260).

Table 18-5 contrasts a last-day binder or a brief general telephone call with teaching spread across the admission and early recovery. Work on the tasks that the person will perform: identify medicines and their purpose, recognize a worsening symptom, describe the next appointment and identify whom to call and how. Let the person demonstrate a skill and use that demonstration to revise the teaching. A patient can repeat an appointment date but still lack transportation, and a relative can name the new prescription but be unable to obtain it. Reinforcement after discharge checks whether knowledge translated into something workable at home (p255–256).

### Reconcile actual use, the intended plan and the list others receive

Table 18-6 separates five steps. Establish the previous regimen, establish the intended prescriptions, compare the two, make a clinical decision about each discrepancy and communicate the resulting regimen to the patient and next caregivers. Printing two lists side by side achieves only the comparison. A chronic medicine held because of hypotension, renal injury or an interaction may be reconsidered later. Document who will reassess it and under what circumstances. Equally, identify short courses with a planned end date so the receiving team does not continue a temporary hospital treatment indefinitely (p253, p257).

Table 18-7 establishes how to build the first list. Ask what is actually taken and when, who prescribes each medicine, which pharmacies are used, what adverse reactions occurred and how the regimen is organized. Deliberately ask about inhalers, drops, patches, creams, suppositories, nonprescription products, vitamins and supplements. Clarify whether a caregiver fills a pillbox or whether the patient selects medicines directly from containers. A pharmacy record confirms dispensing, not ingestion; a copied clinic list may include drugs stopped elsewhere. If accounts differ, seek corroboration rather than automatically reinstating the longest list (p257).

Consider the hip-fracture example in Figure 18-1. Anticoagulation dosing may change during acute illness and again after rehabilitation. The relevant handoff is a chain: record the regimen, most recent result, who is managing the next test, and how the next dose instruction reaches the patient and everyone administering it. If the patient also attends a diabetes clinic or has home therapy, determine which clinician will integrate changes across services. This chapter teaches ownership of prescribing and monitoring rather than a standard drug dose that can be carried forward across all transitions (p253–254, p257).

### Why some transitional care interventions work in one setting and not another

Figure 18-2 shows a transitional care nurse working with a patient and family together. The chapter's home-visit models, Coleman's Care Transitions Intervention and Naylor's Transitional Care Model, connect hospital preparation with in-home reinforcement. They emphasize medication management, follow-up, early signs of deterioration and access to clinicians. The reduction in rehospitalization of roughly one-third in the studies reviewed persisted for as long as **180 days** despite a much shorter active intervention. This supports the importance of teaching usable self-management skills; it does not establish that an isolated courtesy call will reproduce the effect (p257–258).

A patient who refuses a home visit, lives outside the service area or lacks practical access to the team may still benefit from a different model. In C-TraC, a nurse case manager combines structured in-hospital contacts with postdischarge calls and links to the clinicians on either side of the transfer. Preliminary results suggested about one-third fewer **30-day** rehospitalizations among participants. The authors call for more rigorous evaluation. Project RED emphasizes inpatient preparation, instructions and education. Its trial reduced emergency visits without a significant rehospitalization reduction. Distinguish the outcome measured, the age and risk of the sample, and how much contact continued after discharge (p258).

INTERACT addresses a different transition: a nursing-home resident becoming acutely unwell and potentially going to hospital. Its tools support early recognition of change, structured assessment and communication, and review of transfers. Effective use requires that staff recognize the change and have timely access to a clinician and appropriate treatment on site. One site with on-site nurse practitioners implementing key components reported a **30% reduction** in all-cause hospitalization. Results have varied with implementation. A lower transfer rate alone cannot tell whether appropriate acute treatment was delivered; the relevant question is which transfers can safely be avoided and what happens to residents who stay (p259).

### A dementia diagnosis is not a discharge instruction

Table 18-9 illustrates several failures acting together. An older woman with Alzheimer disease developed swallowing difficulty during treatment for pneumonia and needed a modified diet. Her family had also described increasing difficulty at home after bereavement. The change in diet was absent from both discharge orders and summary. One clinician discussed antibiotics and assumed someone else would explain the diet. Another assumed that a patient with Alzheimer disease could not use the information. The receiving nurses could not reach the hospital team, and her community clinician did not yet know she had been admitted. A regular diet was started; the patient later returned with recurrent aspiration pneumonia (p260).

The **23/30** cognitive score in the vignette is a clue to what the team assumed, not a standalone test of decision-making capacity. Ask what the patient can understand and retain with support. Explain the reason and practical meaning of dietary changes to the patient and the person helping with meals. Ensure the actual diet order and feeding instructions reach the facility, specify who can answer questions and communicate directly if the order is incomplete. This case teaches reliable transmission and individual assessment; it does not establish that every person with dementia or pneumonia needs a thickened-liquid diet (p253, p256–257, p259–260).

A nursing-home resident with cognitive impairment may be sent to an emergency department without someone able to report the usual mental or functional state. Communicate baseline, recent change, goals, medication and treatment already given. Weigh the value of diagnostic or therapeutic resources available in hospital against delirium, disorientation or disruption of established support. Conversely, insufficient on-site assessment or support can make transfer necessary. Sources of collateral information and a reliable return handoff matter in either direction, including when an emergency visit does not result in overnight admission (p252–254, p259–260).

### Make end-of-life transitions purposeful

The chapter reports that **over 80%** experienced at least one transition in the last six months of life, and about one-third experienced four or more. Multiple transitions can be burdensome even when clinicians in each setting provide technically competent care. Ask what the patient wants to achieve by going to hospital, which symptoms could be treated in place and which services the family can actually obtain at night or during a crisis. Earlier conversations with a person who can express their wishes give surrogates a basis for later decisions when dementia or acute illness limits participation (p259–260).

Table 18-8 describes resources with different purposes: caregiver discharge-planning questions, comparison of nursing-home services and resident rights, advocacy for long-term care, and accessible materials to help patients prepare for visits. Choose the resource that addresses the immediate decision. For a family comparing facilities, explain how to ask about actual nursing and rehabilitation capacity and the plan for return home. For one caring for a dying relative at home, a clear contact for symptom escalation may be more valuable than a directory. A handout supports a conversation; successful transfer still depends on a feasible care plan, communication between teams and follow-through after responsibility changes (p255–260).

## Exam traps

- A readmission, an emergency visit and a discrepancy in medicines are distinct outcomes. Keep the intervention, denominator and follow-up period with each reported effect (p250, p258–259).
- Home health and home hospice can provide intermittent skilled or symptom support; neither automatically provides continuous custodial help (p251).
- A preadmission dispensing record, an inpatient administration record and a final home prescription serve different purposes in medication reconciliation (p253, p257).
- In Table 18-9, assuming another team member explained the changed diet was as consequential as omitting the formal order (p260).
- Fewer emergency visits did not amount to a significant reduction in rehospitalization in the Project RED trial (p258).
- Transfer to a higher level of care can itself harm a person with dementia; communicate baseline function and weigh the goals of transfer (p259–260).
