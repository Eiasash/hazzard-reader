# Chapter 32 Study: Oral Health

## Summary

**Examine and prevent**
- Oral health supports eating, speech, sensation and social interaction; include oral screening in the general assessment (p453-454).
- Inspect palate, uvula, tongue, cheeks, lips, floor of mouth, gums and teeth with good lighting and retraction (p465).
- Report infection/abscess promptly; document loose, fractured, decayed or missing teeth and abnormal tissue in dental referrals (p465).
- Fluoride, effective brushing and interdental cleaning help prevent caries; adapt brush handles or use electric brushes when disability limits care (p456-457, p466).
- Periodontitis involves inflammation, infection and loss of the tooth’s attachment apparatus. Destruction may cause bone loss, tooth mobility, pain with eating and tooth loss (p456).

**Dry mouth, senses and mucosa**
- Salivary hypofunction is not an inevitable consequence of healthy aging. Review medicines, systemic disease and head/neck treatment (p457-458).
- Address causative medicines where possible; consider dose modification, alternatives, sugar-free stimulation and saliva substitutes (p458).
- Pilocarpine **5 mg TID and qhs** or cevimeline **30 mg TID** can help when functional salivary tissue remains (p458).
- Oral moisture can be supported with appropriate sugar-free fluids and gels; keep removable dentures clean and out during sleep (p458).
- Taste changes in healthy aging are generally modest; medicines and impaired smell can contribute to food complaints (p458-459).
- Consider malignancy in persistent lesions: biopsy a lesion not healed within **2 to 3 weeks** after removal of suspected causes (p461).
- Before cancer therapy, arrange comprehensive dental assessment and treatment of infection; discuss treatment-related oral complications (p461).

**Function and dependent care**
- Dentition, disease and neuromuscular impairment affect mastication and swallowing; assess function as well as visible pathology (p461-462).
- Ask about pain and difficulty biting or chewing; financial and access barriers may prevent needed dental care (p463-464).
- In long-term care, provide reliable assisted hygiene, regular dental review and adaptations for disability (p465-466).
- Near life's end, priorities may shift toward comfort, safety, dignity and preserving function (p465).

### Interpreting the findings at the chairside

- Localize the damage. Inflamed, bleeding gums alone indicate gingivitis. Periodontitis also destroys the tissues attaching the tooth, including the ligament and alveolar bone. A loose tooth can therefore signal loss of support even when the crown appears intact (p454, p456).
- Review nifedipine and other implicated calcium-channel blockers, phenytoin and cyclosporine if the gums enlarge. The overgrowth can make cleaning harder and predispose to caries and further periodontal destruction (p456).
- Remove a partial or full denture before inspecting the mucosa. Local trauma from an ill-fitting plate can cause tissue overgrowth; candidiasis can involve the denture-bearing surface and may coexist with fissures at the corners of the mouth (p459).
- Ask whether enough salivary function remains to stimulate. If little or none remains, a substitute, rinse or moisturizing gel can still improve comfort. Include topical fluoride and daily oral hygiene alongside treatment for dryness (p458).

## Exam traps

- Healthy aging can change gland structure while resting salivary output remains largely preserved (p457).
- An edentulous patient still needs inspection of the mucosa beneath dentures (p459).
- A plausible mechanical cause does not close the assessment of an ulcer that persists after the cause has been removed (p461).

## Recall drill

**1. Which oral functions can influence health beyond the appearance of the teeth?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chewing and swallowing, speech, sensation and the ability to eat and interact socially (p453–454).</details>

**2. Which structures should be inspected when screening a patient who wears dentures?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Remove the denture and inspect the gums, palate, tongue, cheeks, floor of mouth and lips as well as any remaining teeth (p459, p465).</details>

**3. How do gingivitis and periodontitis differ on examination?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Gingivitis affects the gums. In periodontitis, inflammation is accompanied by loss of the tissues supporting the tooth (p456).</details>

**4. Which medicines can produce gingival enlargement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examples include nifedipine, phenytoin and cyclosporine (p456).</details>

**5. Which findings belong in a dental referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Describe abnormal tissue and the location of painful, loose, fractured, carious or missing teeth. Suspected abscess or infection warrants prompt attention (p465).</details>

**6. What should a new complaint of dry mouth prompt you to review?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Medicines, systemic disease and previous head or neck treatment. Healthy aging alone usually has little effect on salivary output (p457–458).</details>

**7. When might sugar-free gum or a salivary stimulant help?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When residual secretory tissue can respond. Chewing or gustatory stimulation can supplement medication review (p458).</details>

**8. What drug doses does the chapter describe for salivary stimulation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Pilocarpine <strong>5 mg three times daily and at bedtime</strong>, or cevimeline <strong>30 mg three times daily</strong>, when secretory capacity remains (p458).</details>

**9. What can help when little or no salivary function remains?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A substitute, rinse or moisturizing gel; sugar-free fluids and lip lubrication can also improve comfort (p458).</details>

**10. Why examine beneath a poorly fitting denture?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Mechanical trauma can cause hyperplasia, and candidiasis may be visible on the covered mucosa (p459).</details>

**11. When should a persistent oral lesion be biopsied?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When it has not healed within <strong>2 to 3 weeks</strong> after suspected causes have been removed (p461).</details>

**12. Which assessment should precede cancer therapy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Comprehensive dental assessment with treatment of oral infection and discussion of anticipated oral complications (p461).</details>

**13. What can make food seem tasteless despite modest age-related taste changes?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Impaired smell and medications can contribute; ask about both when reviewing intake (p458–459).</details>

**14. What adaptations can preserve oral hygiene when grip is poor?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A modified handle or electric brush and reliable caregiver assistance when needed (p466).</details>

**15. How might the aims of dental care change near the end of life?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Focus on freedom from pain, comfort, dignity, safety and remaining oral function (p465).</details>
