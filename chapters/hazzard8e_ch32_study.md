# Chapter 32 Study: Oral Health

## Summary

**Examine and prevent**
- Oral health supports eating, speech, sensation and social interaction; include oral screening in the general assessment (p453-454).
- Inspect palate, uvula, tongue, cheeks, lips, floor of mouth, gums and teeth with good lighting and retraction (p465).
- Report infection/abscess promptly; document loose, fractured, decayed or missing teeth and abnormal tissue in dental referrals (p465).
- Fluoride, effective brushing and interdental cleaning help prevent caries; adapt brush handles or use electric brushes when disability limits care (p456-457, p466).
- Periodontitis involves inflammation, infection and loss of the tooth’s attachment apparatus. Destruction may cause bone loss, tooth mobility, pain with eating and tooth loss (p456).

**Dry mouth, senses and mucosa**
- Salivary hypofunction is not an inevitable consequence of healthy aging. Review medicines, systemic disease and head/neck treatment (p457-458).
- Address causative medicines where possible; consider dose modification, alternatives, sugar-free stimulation and saliva substitutes (p458).
- Pilocarpine **5 mg TID and qhs** or cevimeline **30 mg TID** can help when functional salivary tissue remains (p458).
- Oral moisture can be supported with appropriate sugar-free fluids and gels; keep removable dentures clean and out during sleep (p458).
- Taste changes in healthy aging are generally modest; medicines and impaired smell can contribute to food complaints (p458-459).
- Consider malignancy in persistent lesions: biopsy a lesion not healed within **2 to 3 weeks** after removal of suspected causes (p461).
- Before cancer therapy, arrange comprehensive dental assessment and treatment of infection; discuss treatment-related oral complications (p461).

**Function and dependent care**
- Dentition, disease and neuromuscular impairment affect mastication and swallowing; assess function as well as visible pathology (p461-462).
- Ask about pain and difficulty biting or chewing; financial and access barriers may prevent needed dental care (p463-464).
- In long-term care, provide reliable assisted hygiene, regular dental review and adaptations for disability (p465-466).
- Near life's end, priorities may shift toward comfort, safety, dignity and preserving function (p465).

### Interpreting the findings at the chairside

- Localize the damage. Inflamed, bleeding gums alone indicate gingivitis. Periodontitis also destroys the tissues attaching the tooth, including the ligament and alveolar bone. A loose tooth can therefore signal loss of support even when the crown appears intact (p454, p456).
- Review nifedipine and other implicated calcium-channel blockers, phenytoin and cyclosporine if the gums enlarge. The overgrowth can make cleaning harder and predispose to caries and further periodontal destruction (p456).
- Remove a partial or full denture before inspecting the mucosa. Local trauma from an ill-fitting plate can cause tissue overgrowth; candidiasis can involve the denture-bearing surface and may coexist with fissures at the corners of the mouth (p459).
- Ask whether enough salivary function remains to stimulate. If little or none remains, a substitute, rinse or moisturizing gel can still improve comfort. Include topical fluoride and daily oral hygiene alongside treatment for dryness (p458).

### Connect oral symptoms to the task the patient can no longer perform

The mouth has to receive food, identify it, reduce it to manageable particles, form a cohesive bolus and transfer it for swallowing. Teeth and their supporting tissues bear the forces of biting. The tongue positions and mixes food; saliva lubricates and dissolves tastants; lips and cheeks maintain the oral seal. The same tissues support speech, facial expression and social interaction. Difficulty holding food in the mouth is therefore a different problem from pain on biting, inability to propel a bolus or difficulty initiating a swallow. Tables 32-1 and 32-2 help localize the failed task before assigning a cause (p453–454).

Ask what has changed at meals. Someone who avoids apples or meat may have unstable teeth, dry mucosa, prosthesis pain, weak chewing muscles or fear of choking. These explanations can coexist. A person may report eating normally because the family now prepares only soft foods or because they have stopped eating with others. Ask about meals avoided, time needed, the need to sip liquid, and whether dentures stay in place. Examine the mouth and observe function where appropriate. An adequate calorie count does not establish comfort, enjoyment or a varied diet (p453–454, p461–463).

Table 32-3 summarizes local oral infections (p454). Table 32-5 covers systemic illness and treatments associated with mucosal changes (pp458–460). New mucosal discomfort can follow a change in medicine, dry mouth, an ill-fitting prosthesis, infection or a systemic disease. Loss of function after stroke may reduce oral clearance despite an intact dentition. Conversely, dental pain can impair eating in the absence of pharyngeal dysfunction. Separate what the patient feels, what can be seen and which task is affected; a person's account of “trouble swallowing” may first require examination of teeth, tongue mobility, saliva or a removable appliance (p453–454, p461–462).

### Why keeping more teeth changes the pattern of disease

The enamel covering a crown provides a hard protective surface. Dentin makes up most of the tooth; cementum covers the root and has less mineral content than enamel. As gingiva recedes, the root becomes exposed to plaque and oral fluids. This makes root caries a particular concern in older people who retain natural teeth, even if they have few untreated cavities on the crowns. Examine the gum line, including tooth surfaces made harder to reach by bridges or partial dentures. A patient can retain many teeth while having only a few that are painless, well supported and opposed by a tooth in the other arch (p454–456).

Dental caries results when the balance between demineralization and remineralization is repeatedly unfavorable. Frequent exposure to fermentable carbohydrates, adherent plaque and diminished salivary protection make a vulnerable surface more likely to decay. Reduced manual dexterity or inability to remember the sequence of brushing can change risk without a change in diet. Review sweetened medicines, drinks or candies used for a dry mouth. A sweet lozenge may briefly encourage fluid in the mouth while repeatedly exposing the root surface to sugar. Managing pain, improving hygiene access and choosing sugar-free stimulation address different components of the cycle (p455–458).

Fluoride can support remineralization on tooth surfaces exposed to repeated challenges. The chapter discusses fluoridated water and dentifrices and the role of professional topical fluoride according to caries risk. Plaque removal and attention to diet are still necessary. Treatment ranges from restoring a damaged surface to replacing missing teeth with bridges, partial or complete dentures or implants. Decide what the patient can use and maintain. A removable prosthesis that cannot be inserted comfortably or cleaned reliably may not restore the function the patient hoped for, while preserving a small number of strategically useful, healthy teeth may improve chewing (p455–456, p461–462).

### Gingival inflammation, attachment loss and pneumonia risk

The periodontal ligament, cementum and alveolar bone hold the tooth in place. Gingivitis is confined to the gum; periodontitis involves loss of supporting attachment as well as inflammation. Look for recession, bleeding, pockets, mobility, pain on chewing or altered tooth position. The chapter describes episodic periods of activity rather than uniform continuous loss throughout adult life. In healthy adults, attachment loss need not accelerate solely because of age. Smoking, medical illness, changes in medication and reduced access to effective cleaning can explain why oral disease is more prevalent in a particular older patient (p456–457).

Gum enlargement around retained teeth can be drug associated. Calcium-channel blockers such as nifedipine, phenytoin and cyclosporine are examples in the book. Overgrowth creates niches that trap plaque and make home care harder. A prescriber may be able to substitute or modify a medicine, while a dentist addresses accumulated deposits and local disease; stopping a necessary drug without addressing the tooth surfaces would be an incomplete response. A loose crown or tooth in someone with weak oral control can also become an aspiration hazard, an issue distinct from the gradual loss of periodontal support (p456, p465).

Aspiration pneumonia involves more than dental plaque alone. Oral or denture biofilm can harbor organisms that enter the lower airway when swallowing or airway protection is impaired. Dependence for mouth care and feeding, dysphagia and medical illness can coincide in long-term care. The association gives daily cleaning a plausible clinical role; it does not prove that a single rinse will prevent pneumonia or that mouth care corrects a failed swallow. Address oral disease and the patient's ability to handle and clear secretions together, and communicate swallowing concerns to the appropriate team (p456, p461–462, p465–466).

Mechanical cleaning remains central. The chapter describes brushing for **2 minutes twice daily** and interdental cleaning. Scaling, root planing and other professional interventions address deposits and disease that brushing alone cannot remove. Selected adjuncts listed include chlorhexidine **0.12%**, subgingival minocycline **1 mg** and low-dose doxycycline **20 mg**. The text also mentions clindamycin **300 mg four times daily** or metronidazole **400 mg three times daily for 7–10 days** as examples used in periodontal treatment. These are dental treatment examples, not a reason to prescribe systemic antibiotics for any bleeding gum. The treatment plan depends on severity, tooth prognosis and whether the patient can sustain daily care (p457).

### Dry mouth: symptom, secretion and cause

Xerostomia describes the experience of dryness, while hypofunction refers to reduced secretion. A patient can report dryness while measured output is relatively preserved; a patient unable to communicate may instead present with dry mucosa, altered eating or recurrent caries. The parotid, submandibular and sublingual glands and numerous minor glands contribute secretions with different properties. The chapter reports loss of approximately **30% of glandular parenchyma** over the adult lifespan, yet secretion in healthy older adults is largely maintained. A structural observation about aging cannot by itself explain a new symptom (p457).

Take a medication history that includes antidepressants, anticholinergic drugs, sedatives and other agents that can contribute to dryness. Ask about dehydration, autoimmune disease and head or neck radiotherapy. Determine when discomfort began and whether the person wakes at night needing water or cannot swallow dry food. On examination, note whether there is a pool of saliva, whether mucosa adheres to an instrument, and whether chapped lips, new root-surface lesions, candidiasis or denture friction accompany the complaint. Review reversible causes and medication burden before escalating to a secretagogue (p457–459).

The choice of stimulation versus substitution depends on residual gland function. Sugar-free gum or other noncariogenic stimulation may help when saliva can still be produced. The printed chapter lists pilocarpine **5 mg three times daily and at bedtime** or cevimeline **30 mg three times daily** as pharmacological examples. Assess whether a systemic cholinergic agent is suitable for the individual. When little tissue can respond, lubrication with rinses, substitutes or gels directly addresses dryness. Protect teeth with fluoride, clean the mouth regularly and ask the patient to remove and clean dentures overnight. A monthly prescription alone will not address caries or infection already present (p458).

### Taste, smell and oral motor control

Ask the patient to describe whether the problem is detecting basic tastes, identifying familiar foods, or finding food enjoyable. Some experimental thresholds change with age, yet ordinary eating involves olfaction, texture, temperature, memory and oral comfort as well as taste buds. The chapter describes relatively modest changes in taste with healthy aging compared with more consistent changes in odor detection and recognition. A medication change, dry mouth, illness, depression or diminished smell can be more informative than the person's age. Loss of pleasure in food can lead to restricted intake even with preserved ability to swallow (p458–459).

Chewing requires teeth or stable prostheses, adequate saliva, coordinated tongue and jaw movements, muscle strength and intact sensation. A slipping denture may prolong chewing without producing overt choking. Neuropathy, stroke or a neurodegenerative disease may instead impair bolus control or the timing of transfer. Someone with fewer working teeth may compensate for years until new weakness or a change in food preparation exceeds that reserve. Ask about coughing, pocketing food, oral residue and changing speech, as well as dental pain. Combine dental assessment with swallowing or communication evaluation where findings indicate a motor problem (p461–462).

Temporomandibular findings and symptoms need not coincide. A radiograph showing degenerative change does not locate every episode of jaw or facial pain. Examine movement, pain with chewing, muscles, joints, teeth and mucosa, and consider neuropathic or referred pain. Speech also depends on teeth, tongue, saliva and the fit of appliances, so a new speech complaint after a denture adjustment deserves direct examination. Conversely, a new neurologic deficit affecting speech cannot safely be dismissed as an appliance problem. The time course and accompanying findings guide the interpretation (p462).

### Oral mucosal lesions: identify what can heal and what needs tissue

Remove dentures and inspect both the appliance and the underlying mucosa. Local pressure or an old ill-fitting denture can cause an ulcer or an area of hyperplasia with a shape that matches the appliance. In dry mucosa, even modest friction can injure tissue. Reassess after removing the suspected source of trauma. Denture stomatitis, angular cheilitis and oral candidiasis can coexist, so inspect the palate, tongue, commissures and surfaces under the denture. A sore spot recurring at the same place calls for attention to fit, mucosal integrity and microbial disease rather than repeated courses of empiric medication (p459–461).

Location, persistence and appearance matter. A removable plaque, a persistent red patch, an indurated ulcer and widespread painful erosions have different implications. Table 32-5 includes infections, inflammatory or autoimmune disease, trauma, prosthetic injury, salivary hypofunction and malignancy. Ask about lesions elsewhere, recent treatment and whether oral pain limits nutrition or basic hygiene. An apparently obvious explanation such as denture trauma does not close the assessment if the lesion fails to heal. The book calls for biopsy of a lesion persisting **2–3 weeks** after removal of plausible causes; arrange earlier evaluation when the initial findings themselves are concerning (p459–461).

Table 32-6 addresses selected diagnosed vesiculobullous or erosive disorders, not an unexplained solitary ulcer. Topical options listed include fluocinonide **0.05%**, triamcinolone acetonide **0.1%** and clobetasol **0.05%**, applied to involved regions three times daily and at bedtime in the table. The rinse examples are dexamethasone **0.5 mg/5 mL** or diphenhydramine **12.5 mg/5 mL**, **10 mL** rinsed and spat rather than swallowed, four times daily for five minutes. Oral candidiasis can complicate therapy. For selected diagnosed disease requiring systemic prednisone, Table 32-6 lists **5-mg tablets**. It prints the regimen as “12 tabs QOD × 2 d, decreasing by 2 tabs every other day.” The initial “× 2 d” is ambiguous with alternate-day administration. Do not infer a second dose on the following day. It lists azathioprine **50 mg twice daily** as a steroid-sparing option. These are specialist regimens: dose and duration depend on severity and comorbid illness, and oral candidiasis may require treatment. Reexamine a nonhealing lesion rather than simply intensifying steroid treatment (p461).

Tobacco and alcohol are important risk exposures for oral cancer. Inspect high-risk surfaces that are easily missed, including the lateral and undersurfaces of the tongue, floor of mouth, soft palate and retromolar areas. Figure 32-6 illustrates carcinomas in sites that might be concealed by the tongue, cheek or an appliance. Early lesions do not necessarily produce severe pain. The chapter's US figures give five-year survival of **85% for localized**, **67% for regionally spread** and **40% for metastatic** oral and pharyngeal disease; compare stage-specific groups rather than assuming these are the prospects of an individual patient. Definitive assessment of a persistent suspicious lesion requires tissue (p460–461).

A cancer diagnosis raises a second dental question before treatment starts. Identify active caries, periodontal infection, abscesses and mucosal lesions before radiotherapy, chemotherapy or surgery limits the ability to deal with them. Head and neck surgery can alter appearance, sensation and oral function. Radiotherapy can injure mucosa and reduce salivary function; a patient who has managed well with dentures may need new strategies for friction, dryness, hygiene and feeding. Counsel the patient about these possibilities while they can discuss priorities and arrange follow-up, rather than waiting until mucositis or difficulty eating interrupts treatment (p461).

Ask separately about pain and denture use after transition to a facility. A staff report that a resident has dentures does not establish whether the correct appliances are present, comfortable, used at meals or cleaned after use. A resident who repeatedly removes a plate may be communicating discomfort or instability rather than refusing care. Check for fractures, pressure injuries and mucosal disease, provide symptom relief, and coordinate assessment of the appliance. If the person is at risk of aspirating a loose component, document the risk and ensure the team knows what is safe to offer until the problem is addressed (p459, p465–466).

Use the same functional approach when choosing how extensive a periodontal plan should be. Healing may take longer in an older patient, but favorable long-term results are possible. The condition of the supporting tissues, medical illness, smoking, capacity for daily cleaning and the value of the tooth in chewing matter more than a chronological cutoff. Discuss whether retaining or extracting a tooth would improve the specific problem the patient raised, and how the surviving or replacement teeth will be maintained after treatment (p456–457).

### Applying the examination in community and institutional care

Begin with good illumination and removal of an appliance. Inspect the lips and corners of the mouth, retract the cheeks, and examine gums, remaining teeth and visible roots. Ask the patient to protrude and move the tongue so that the sides, undersurface and floor can be seen. Inspect the hard and soft palate and uvula. Record the site, approximate extent, color, tenderness and relation to dental or prosthetic surfaces of a lesion for referral. Note a broken or loose tooth, purulent focus, swelling or abscess. Communicate urgent concerns directly and state what the patient can or cannot eat or tolerate while waiting for definitive care (p464–465).

Access changes the disease that clinicians see. The chapter's US analyses distinguish income, dental coverage, demographic group and receipt of care. A patient who rarely attends a dental practice can have untreated disease because of transport, cost or care dependency despite valuing their oral health. Ask whether preventive and restorative treatment are feasible and whether help is needed for travel, consent or daily maintenance. Population averages on tooth loss and care use describe inequalities in exposure and access; they do not justify assuming that a particular person will decline treatment. Prevention before functional decline can reduce the burden when dependence develops (p463–465).

In long-term care, find out who is responsible for mouth care and whether they can perform it. A resident unable to grasp a conventional brush may benefit from an enlarged handle, an electric brush or hand-over-hand assistance. A resident with dementia may need simple cues, a familiar routine and repeated opportunities. Inspect and clean dentures as well as residual teeth. Record what the resident tolerates, their own preferences, who can help, and how pain or new oral findings will be reported. Specify access to routine dental assessment and emergency care rather than relying on the patient to request an appointment (p465–466).

The priorities in Table 32-7 include freedom from oral pain and infection, safe teeth and appliances, ability to eat, early detection and dependable daily hygiene. As life expectancy shortens, the balance can shift toward moisture, comfort, dignity and removal of an immediately hazardous appliance. This does not mean abandoning examination; a painful fractured tooth, ill-fitting denture or treatable infection can be a major source of distress even if extensive restoration is inappropriate. Listen to the resident and caregivers about which activities matter most, then set a plan that can actually be delivered at the bedside (p465–466).

## Exam traps

- Healthy aging can change gland structure while resting salivary output remains largely preserved (p457).
- An edentulous patient still needs inspection of the mucosa beneath dentures (p459).
- A plausible mechanical cause does not close the assessment of an ulcer that persists after the cause has been removed (p461).

## Recall drill

**1. Which oral functions can influence health beyond the appearance of the teeth?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Chewing and swallowing, speech, sensation and the ability to eat and interact socially (p453–454).</details>

**2. Which structures should be inspected when screening a patient who wears dentures?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Remove the denture and inspect the gums, palate, tongue, cheeks, floor of mouth and lips as well as any remaining teeth (p459, p465).</details>

**3. How do gingivitis and periodontitis differ on examination?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Gingivitis affects the gums. In periodontitis, inflammation is accompanied by loss of the tissues supporting the tooth (p456).</details>

**4. Which medicines can produce gingival enlargement?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examples include nifedipine, phenytoin and cyclosporine (p456).</details>

**5. Which findings belong in a dental referral?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Describe abnormal tissue and the location of painful, loose, fractured, carious or missing teeth. Suspected abscess or infection warrants prompt attention (p465).</details>

**6. What should a new complaint of dry mouth prompt you to review?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Medicines, systemic disease and previous head or neck treatment. Healthy aging alone usually has little effect on salivary output (p457–458).</details>

**7. When might sugar-free gum or a salivary stimulant help?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When residual secretory tissue can respond. Chewing or gustatory stimulation can supplement medication review (p458).</details>

**8. What drug doses does the chapter describe for salivary stimulation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Pilocarpine <strong>5 mg three times daily and at bedtime</strong>, or cevimeline <strong>30 mg three times daily</strong>, when secretory capacity remains (p458).</details>

**9. What can help when little or no salivary function remains?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A substitute, rinse or moisturizing gel; sugar-free fluids and lip lubrication can also improve comfort (p458).</details>

**10. Why examine beneath a poorly fitting denture?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Mechanical trauma can cause hyperplasia, and candidiasis may be visible on the covered mucosa (p459).</details>

**11. When should a persistent oral lesion be biopsied?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When it has not healed within <strong>2 to 3 weeks</strong> after suspected causes have been removed (p461).</details>

**12. Which assessment should precede cancer therapy?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Comprehensive dental assessment with treatment of oral infection and discussion of anticipated oral complications (p461).</details>

**13. What can make food seem tasteless despite modest age-related taste changes?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Impaired smell and medications can contribute; ask about both when reviewing intake (p458–459).</details>

**14. What adaptations can preserve oral hygiene when grip is poor?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A modified handle or electric brush and reliable caregiver assistance when needed (p466).</details>

**15. How might the aims of dental care change near the end of life?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Focus on freedom from pain, comfort, dignity, safety and remaining oral function (p465).</details>
