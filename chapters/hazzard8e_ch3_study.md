# Chapter 3 Study: Immunology and Inflammation

> Excluded from the IMA required reading list (P005-2026). Cited in the 2020-22 past papers (Hazzard 7e, linked by chapter).

## What the exams asked

- Which immune function declines with age? Distinguish neutrophil chemotaxis from phagocytosis, baseline cytokine production and the proportion of memory T cells. [December 2021 Q20](?chapter=3#asked-chapter).

## Neutrophil recruitment and clearance

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-464ec512c4048c136965e84e"></span>

**Chemotaxis** is directed movement toward a chemical signal. Older neutrophils follow this gradient less effectively, so fewer may reach a focus of infection or a healing wound promptly. This was the change tested in December 2021. Once a neutrophil reaches a bacterium, engulfing it by **phagocytosis** and killing it inside the cell are separate functions. Both also decline. [Neutrophil function, p50](?chapter=3#ch3-neutrophil-chemotaxis).

Resolution requires dying neutrophils to be cleared by macrophages. In the human skin model described in the chapter, clearance of apoptotic neutrophils was slower in older adults. Thus inflammation can persist at a site even when recruitment and bacterial killing are less effective. [Resolution of inflammation, p51](?chapter=3#ch3-neutrophil-clearance).

**Two other choices in the same question refer to different compartments.** Circulating inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF tend to rise at rest. A dendritic cell may produce less additional cytokine in response to a new stimulus despite this higher baseline. In the adaptive compartment, the involuting thymus releases fewer naïve T cells and the proportion of antigen-experienced memory T cells rises. The ability to respond to a new antigen may therefore decline even while memory cells become more numerous (p47, p50–52, p55–57).

## Hematopoiesis and innate immune signaling

- Stem-cell count and stem-cell performance measure different things. Hematopoietic stem cells can increase in number while their ability to repopulate blood cell lineages declines. Output shifts toward the myeloid lineage, leaving fewer lymphoid progenitors for T- and B-cell development. Transplantation experiments show the functional loss most clearly in mice (p47–48).
- The first response to infection includes epithelial barriers, complement, phagocytes and natural killer cells. Antigen-presenting cells also connect this early response to specific T-cell immunity. Pattern recognition receptors detect conserved microbial structures or signals from damaged tissue. Dendritic cells then provide both a peptide presented on MHC and a costimulatory signal to the T cell (p48–50, Table 3-1).
- Persistent infection, damaged cell components and inflammatory proteins released by senescent cells are possible contributors to inflammaging. Higher inflammatory markers are associated with weaker muscle and greater mortality in older cohorts. A raised marker by itself cannot identify which mechanism is active in an individual patient (p47, p50).
- Innate responses vary by cell type and setting. Some monocytes and dendritic cells produce fewer cytokines after stimulation, yet particular inflammatory monocyte populations produce more. Although a greater fraction of natural killer cells has a cytotoxic phenotype in older adults, killing by each cell is less efficient (p51–52, Table 3-2).

## How T cells and B cells acquire specificity and memory

- Recombination gives each developing lymphocyte its own antigen receptor. A B cell recognizes antigen directly. A T cell recognizes a peptide bound to MHC and, for full activation, receives costimulation. Thymic selection preserves cells able to recognize host MHC and removes cells with dangerous affinity for self. CD4 cells recognize class II MHC and CD8 cells recognize class I (p52–56).
- Less thymic output narrows the pool of naïve T cells available for a new challenge. Repeated stimulation, especially with latent cytomegalovirus, can expand a few memory clones at the expense of overall repertoire diversity. Many older CD8 cells have lost CD28, which normally supplies a costimulatory signal. Blood counts alone can therefore miss defects in diversity or activation (p55–58, Table 3-3).
- Helper T-cell lineages support intracellular pathogen defense, antibody production in follicles and other specialized responses. Regulatory T cells restrain activation. Tissue-resident memory T cells can provide a rapid local response. Aging affects these populations differently, and peripheral blood findings need not describe their behavior in tissue (p57–58).
- B cells mature in marrow. Class switching changes the antibody isotype while retaining its target specificity. Somatic hypermutation can improve affinity. Both require activation-induced cytidine deaminase. In older humans, marrow B-cell production is relatively preserved, but circulating B-cell numbers, class-switched memory cells and antibody-secreting plasmablast responses fall. Autoantibody titers can rise despite a weaker response to vaccination (p53–54, p58–59).

## Vaccine responses and investigational treatments

- **Influenza.** With less antibody production after standard vaccination, increasing the antigen dose or adding an adjuvant can improve the response. In the trial described, high-dose vaccine gave a 24% **relative** reduction in influenza-like illness compared with standard dose. Antibody titers and clinical illness are different outcomes (p59–60).
- **Zoster.** Loss of cell-mediated control allows latent varicella zoster virus to reactivate. Protection with the older live vaccine fell markedly with age. In separate trials, the adjuvanted recombinant vaccine reduced shingles by 97% in adults aged 50 or older and by 90% in those aged 70 or older. The older cohort also had fewer cases of postherpetic neuralgia (p60–61).
- **Pneumococcus.** Capsular polysaccharide triggers chiefly a direct B-cell response. Attaching it to a carrier protein recruits T-cell help and allows immune memory to form. CAPiTA found fewer cases of vaccine-type pneumonia and invasive disease with the 13-valent conjugate vaccine, in a population studied before widespread use of conjugate vaccine in Dutch children. Childhood vaccination can change older adults’ exposure to those serotypes (p61).
- **COVID-19.** The chapter describes stronger inflammatory responses in severe illness and substantial variation across clinical studies. The original mRNA vaccine trials found protection in older participants, with neutralizing antibodies and T-cell responses. Their initial estimates covered the strains and follow-up periods studied (p61–62).
- **Immune metabolism.** TORC1 integrates nutrient signals that affect protein synthesis. In older volunteers, low-dose inhibition of this pathway improved the antibody response to influenza vaccination. Metformin activates AMP kinase and can inhibit TORC1. Senolytics aim to reduce the burden of senescent cells. The chapter presents these as experimental approaches to immune aging. Genome-wide, single-cell, metabolic and microbiome studies are exploring why older people with similar ages respond differently (p60, p62).

## Summary

**Immune aging is not uniform suppression**
- Immunosenescence combines low-grade baseline inflammation with impaired responses to acute stimuli; responses to new antigens are often more impaired than memory responses (p47).
- Hematopoietic stem-cell numbers may increase while differentiation and self-renewal deteriorate; output shifts toward myeloid cells at the expense of lymphoid progenitors (p47-48).
- Innate cells recognize pathogens and activate antigen presentation. Aging alters neutrophil, monocyte, dendritic-cell and natural-killer-cell functions, with both impaired responses and persistent inflammation (p48-52).
- Inflammaging involves raised inflammatory mediators and associations with mortality and age-related illness; associations do not prove that every mediator is a causal treatment target (p47, p50).

**Adaptive immunity**
- Antigen-receptor rearrangement creates diverse B/T repertoires; antigen selection supports specificity and memory (p52-54, p58).
- Thymic involution reduces new T-cell output. Fewer naive cells, repertoire contraction and accumulated differentiated/memory cells impair responses to new challenges (p55-57).
- Human marrow B-cell production appears generally preserved, unlike prominent mouse changes; circulating B-cell numbers, antibody responses and repertoire diversity decline (p58-59).

**Vaccines and investigational approaches in the book**
- Influenza high-dose/adjuvanted formulations can improve responses. One trial found **24% relative efficacy** for high-dose versus standard-dose vaccine against influenza-like illness (p60).
- Recombinant zoster vaccine reduced shingles by **97%** in a trial of adults **50 or older**, and **90%** in another of adults **70 or older**; the latter also reduced postherpetic neuralgia (p61).
- Pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine is largely T-independent; conjugation enables T-cell help and memory. CAPiTA's Dutch results may not transfer directly to US settings with childhood conjugate vaccination (p61).
- Coronavirus disease disproportionately affected older adults; inflammatory and immune-response changes are under investigation, with heterogeneous clinical studies (p61-62).
- Low-dose rapamycin/mTOR inhibition, senolytics and metformin are discussed as research approaches; the chapter does not establish routine anti-aging prescriptions (p60, p62).
