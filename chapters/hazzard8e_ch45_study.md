## After the meal or after standing?

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-66fec28ab46cb543ad7d9cf3 mcq-4c5e3d0b0dc72c3a12baa7a7" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**After lunch, measure pressure against the pre-meal baseline.** In the nursing-home case, systolic pressure falls from 142 to 118 mm Hg at 90 minutes. A fall of at least 20 mm Hg within two hours of starting a meal meets the chapter definition of postprandial hypotension. More blood remains in the splanchnic bed, so less returns to the heart. The normal increases in sympathetic activity, heart rate and resistance may be too small in an older adult with diabetes or Parkinson disease. Insulin and some gut peptides may add vasodilation. The earlier sitting asked only where the blood pools. Neither question requires a change in posture. Postprandial and orthostatic hypotension can coexist (p. 668).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-6391efa1061274125cfe4d1b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**On standing, the baroreflex normally restores pressure.** Pooling in the legs and splanchnic circulation reduces ventricular filling. Less stretch at the carotid sinus and aortic arch triggers sympathetic activation and vagal withdrawal. With age, baroreflex sensitivity falls and the heart and resistance vessels respond less effectively. A patient can be volume depleted without a large rise in pulse. Renal sodium conservation and thirst can also diminish. Long-standing hypertension can shift the cerebral autoregulatory range upward, so an abrupt drop can cause symptoms even at a pressure that appears acceptable (pp. 666–668).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-10be7c951b2f4755be8289f0" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Match the treatment to the measured change.** In the woman who fell on rising to use the bathroom, seated pressure was 135/90 and standing pressure was 110/80. Review the regular regimen and adjust drugs that could lower pressure or impair balance. Ask about diuretics, nitrates, alpha blockers, rate-limiting medicines and psychoactive drugs. Determine why each drug is prescribed and reassess the symptoms and standing pressure after adjustment. Physical therapy can then address weakness or instability. Stable atrial fibrillation alone does not explain this reproducible trigger (pp. 668, 672, 675).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b83da14732e3306ed9970cce" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Either pressure component can meet the threshold.** A fall from 130/80 to 125/65 includes a diastolic decline of 15 mm Hg despite little change in systolic pressure. Prefer a resting supine baseline followed by pressure and pulse at one and three minutes standing, with assistance when needed. Ask when symptoms occur. A fall of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic supports classical OH. Heat and straining can aggravate it. The September paper's listed intervention was head-up sleeping at 30–45 degrees (pp. 667, 672, 675).

**How to interpret the angle.** In patients with severe autonomic impairment and supine hypertension, overnight pressure diuresis can worsen morning OH. The American Heart Association notes that the steep whole-bed tilt used in small studies can be impractical or unsafe. The exam angle should not be prescribed routinely to a frail patient transferring out of bed at night. Individualize fluid, sodium and compression measures, especially with cardiac or renal disease. [American Heart Association scientific statement, nocturnal hypertension and Figure 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11067441/).

## A faint, a seizure, bleeding or an arrhythmia?

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-7e29198920ca95a14795d359" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Ask the witness what happened before, during and after the collapse.** Syncope reflects transient global cerebral hypoperfusion, with loss of tone and spontaneous recovery. Brief myoclonic movements can accompany it. Dimming vision followed by a few jerks and rapid orientation favors a faint. Prolonged postictal confusion and aching muscles favor a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Muscle soreness alone cannot settle the diagnosis. Older patients may have amnesia for the event and report only an unexplained fall (pp. 665, 669–670). The June 2023 comparison comes from Harrison 21e, pp. 3315–3316, Table 425-7.

**Shock can present as a fall.** In the June 2022 case, blood pressure was 92/58, heart rate was 110, hemoglobin was 10.2 g/dL and BUN was 42 mg/dL with creatinine 0.9 mg/dL. Prior ibuprofen exposure raises concern for gastrointestinal bleeding. Examine for melena or blood while stabilizing perfusion and investigating the cause. A reassuring rectal examination cannot exclude a bleed. The shower may have aggravated hypotension, but does not account for anemia. Persistent hemodynamic abnormalities, evidence of bleeding, serious ECG findings, exertional or supine syncope, or suspected structural heart disease warrant urgent evaluation (pp. 667–668, 671–673).

**Read the waveform and the medication list together.** The December 2021 strip shows rapid wide complexes twisting in amplitude after levofloxacin was added to amiodarone and high-dose escitalopram. This is the pattern of torsades in a patient exposed to several QT-prolonging drugs. Rivaroxaban inhibits factor Xa and was the exception. A single rhythm strip of tachycardia does not measure the preceding QT interval. The chapter identifies prolonged QT and ventricular arrhythmia as high-risk findings (pp. 667, 673). [Rivaroxaban thorough-QT study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18095747/).

**Use tests that could capture the suspected mechanism.** Start with history, an eyewitness account when available, medication review, examination, an ECG and postural measurements. A new murmur may justify echocardiography. Match rhythm-monitor duration to event frequency. A short Holter has low yield with infrequent spells, whereas a loop recorder may capture the symptom and rhythm together. Consider tilt testing for suspected reflex syncope after an inconclusive initial assessment. EEG or brain imaging has low yield in uncomplicated syncope without neurologic clues (pp. 671–673).

**A neck trigger suggests a different reflex.** Head turning or a tight collar can precipitate bradycardia, hypotension or both. In a suitable patient, a trained clinician uses monitored carotid sinus massage after screening for contraindications. An abnormal hemodynamic response can also occur in asymptomatic older adults. Reproducing the patient's usual symptoms helps distinguish carotid sinus syndrome from hypersensitivity. Dual-chamber pacing is an option in selected patients over 40 with severe recurrent unpredictable syncope and a documented cardioinhibitory response. A vasodepressor component can persist (pp. 669, 672, 675). [European Society of Cardiology pacing guideline, §5.4](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehab364).

## Vertigo by timing and trigger

**Rolling over in bed and standing up test different systems.** Repeated, brief attacks on rolling in bed, looking up or lying back suggest BPPV. In posterior-canal disease, Dix–Hallpike testing elicits a characteristic transient upbeating, torsional nystagmus. The chapter describes a short latency and fatigue with repetition. Epley repositioning then uses gravity to guide particles from the posterior canal back to the utricle. Routine post-maneuver positional restrictions and routine vestibular suppressants are discouraged (pp. 670, 673–674, 676, Figures 45-1 and 45-2). [AAO-HNSF BPPV guideline, statements 1a, 4a–4b and 6](https://www.entnet.org/quality-practice/quality-products/clinical-practice-guidelines/bppv/).

**Auditory features change the differential.** Ménière disease causes spontaneous episodes with fluctuating hearing, tinnitus or fullness in the affected ear. Progressive unilateral hearing loss and imbalance suggest a vestibular schwannoma. Recurrent vestibulopathy classically causes discrete attacks without associated auditory or neurologic features. A history of dizziness for a year says little about the duration or trigger of each attack (pp. 670, 673–674).

**Continuous acute vertigo needs a stroke assessment.** An adult in the emergency department with new persistent continuous dizziness and spontaneous nystagmus has a different presentation from brief positional spells. In this setting a clinician trained in HINTS examines head impulse, nystagmus and skew and checks hearing. Central or equivocal findings warrant MRI/MRA. Without nystagmus, assess gait severity. A normal head CT cannot reliably exclude posterior circulation ischemia. Brief position-triggered attacks call for positional testing. [Society for Academic Emergency Medicine, GRACE-3, recommendations 2–7 and 11–13](https://www.saem.org/publications/grace/grace-3).

**Chronic disequilibrium often has several treatable contributors.** Look at vision, hearing, proprioception, strength, gait, mood and the medicines that affect alertness or blood pressure. Correct sensory deficits and reversible systemic illness. For persistent vestibular symptoms, supervised exercises use coordinated head, eye and body movements to retrain balance. Continuing sedating suppressants such as meclizine or diazepam can delay compensation and increase falls (pp. 670–671, 673–676).

## Summary

**Definitions**
- Syncope: transient loss of consciousness 2° cerebral hypoperfusion, with unresponsiveness/loss of postural tone and spontaneous, complete recovery (p665)
- Dizziness: abnormal perception of the body's relationship to space; 4 subtypes — vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, and other (p665, p670)

**Epidemiology**
- Syncope lifetime prevalence up to **47%** in healthy adults; **3%** of ED visits (p665)
- Incidence **0.6%/yr**, rising to **2% to 6%/yr** in older adults (p665)
- 1-yr outcomes after ED syncope visit: **7%** mortality, **16%** recurrence needing hospitalization, **6.0%** device insertion (p665)
- Syncope = **7th** most common reason for ED admission in patients >65; **1%** of all hospital admissions; costs the US **$2.4 billion**/yr; up to **40%** remain unexplained despite inpatient workup (p666)
- Young adults: peak incidence age **10–30**, mostly vasovagal. Older adults: more often cardiovascular (p666)
- Dizziness prevalence **4% to 30%** in persons ≥65, higher in females (p666)
- Cardiac causes responsible for about **one-third** of syncope cases in older patients (p667)
- **Table 45-1** causes of syncope: cardiac = structural heart disease, myocardial dysfunction, or arrhythmia (**bradyarrhythmia incl. AV block**, or tachyarrhythmia); noncardiac = neurologic, neurally mediated, respiratory, or vascular (p667)

**Pathophysiology**
- Standing pools **500 to 1000 cc** of blood in legs/splanchnic bed → ↓ venous return → baroreflex response (increased sympathetic outflow, decreased vagal tone) limits the BP fall (p666)
- Aging blunts this defense: ↓ baroreflex sensitivity, defective β-adrenergic signal transduction → diminished HR response to standing/exercise (p666)
- Older adults also lose blood volume: ↓ renin/aldosterone, ↑ atrial natriuretic peptide, ↓ thirst response, often plus diuretics (p666)

**Orthostatic and postprandial hypotension**
- OH definition: **≥20 mmHg** systolic and/or **≥10 mmHg** diastolic drop on standing, usually measured immediately and after a short delay (eg, at **1 and 3 minutes**) (p667)
- Drugs that can contribute to OH include antipsychotics, antidepressants such as MAOIs and TCAs, diuretics, beta-blockers, nitrates and alpha-blockers. Combined alpha and beta blockade further impairs compensation on standing (p668)
- PPH definition: **≥20 mmHg** systolic drop within **2 hours** of starting a meal (p668)
- PPH prevalence: **24% to 36%** of nursing home residents; **23%** of older adults admitted to a geriatric hospital with syncope/falls; found in **50%** of older adults with unexplained syncope (p668)

**Carotid sinus hypersensitivity / carotid sinus syndrome (CSS)**
- CSH definition: asystole **≥3 sec** (cardioinhibitory) and/or systolic BP fall **>50 mmHg** (vasodepressor) during carotid sinus massage (p669)
- Massage technique: done sequentially on each side; recommended duration **5 to 10 sec**; maximal HR fall occurs within **5 sec** of onset (p669)
- CSS requires an abnormal response that reproduces the patient’s usual spontaneous symptoms in a compatible clinical history; vasovagal syncope or OH coexists in **one-third** of CSH patients; about **half** of CSS patients sustain an injury/fracture during episodes (p669)

**Evaluation of syncope**
- Presence of heart disease independently predicts cardiac syncope: sensitivity **95%**, specificity **45%** (p671)
- BNP **≥300 pg/mL** or NT-proBNP **>156 pg/mL** independently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) after ED syncope evaluation (p671)
- Cardiac arrhythmias evident in up to **50%** of patients with EF <40%; atrial fibrillation in **1 in 5** men older than 80 (p671)
- 24-hour ambulatory ECG/Holter: diagnostic yield only **1% to 2%** in unselected populations (p671–672)
- In a review of 2106 patients ≥65 admitted after syncope, postural BP was measured in only **38%** of episodes, yet had the highest diagnostic yield of any test: affected diagnosis **18%–26%**, management **25%–30%**, and determined etiology **15%–21%** (p672)
- ~**10%** of ED syncope patients have a serious outcome within **7 to 30 days** of the visit (p673)
- Dix-Hallpike maneuver (Figure 45-1) BPPV criteria: latency **1 to 5 sec** between maneuver completion and onset, nystagmus/vertigo lasting **10–20 sec**, and fatigability (decreasing intensity) with repeated testing (p673–674)
- **Episode duration is a clue.** BPPV typically causes positional attacks lasting **less than 1 minute**. Spontaneous attacks can overlap in TIA, vestibular migraine and Ménière disease. Definite Ménière disease includes attacks lasting **20 minutes–12 hours** with characteristic auditory findings; probable episodes can last up to **24 hours** (p670; [Bárány Society criteria](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25882471/)).

**Management**
- Vestibular rehabilitation: improvement usually seen after **6 to 8 weeks** (p676)
- Epley canalith repositioning maneuver (Figure 45-2): each of the 5 head positions held **10 to 15 sec** or until vertigo subsides (p676)
- Cardioinhibitory CSS: dual-chamber cardiac pacing abolishes syncope in **85% to 90%** of patients (p675)
- Syncope caused by sustained VT warrants electrophysiologic assessment for an ICD, including ventricular function and reversible causes. After a previous MI, **unexplained syncope with inducible sustained VT on electrophysiologic study** is an ICD indication. Previous MI alone does not justify implantation (p675; [ESC syncope guideline](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehy037)).
- For severe symptomatic **OH**, specialist options include midodrine **2.5–10 mg up to three times daily** while awake (avoid near bedtime) or fludrocortisone **0.05–0.2 mg daily** with potassium, edema and supine BP monitoring. Avoid fludrocortisone in heart failure. For **postprandial hypotension**, the chapter discusses caffeine **250 mg** in the morning and a small study of subcutaneous octreotide **50 micrograms** before a glucose load (p675; [OH review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10024337/), [AHA statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11067441/)).
  > Octreotide was studied in **micrograms**. The textbook’s milligram unit overstates the trial dose by a factor of 1,000 ([Jansen et al., 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2646315/)).

## Recall

**1. What is the definition of syncope, and how does it differ from a seizure or TIA?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Transient loss of consciousness from cerebral hypoperfusion, with loss of postural tone and spontaneous, complete recovery. Other transient causes of LOC (eg, vertebrobasilar ischemia, hypoxemia, hypoglycemia) are technically not syncope if they involve focal neurologic abnormalities or do not recover spontaneously without intervention (eg, oxygen or glucose). Brief myoclonic jerks can occur during syncope from hypoperfusion. They do not, by themselves, establish an epileptic seizure (p665).

</details>

**2. What are the 4 subtypes of dizziness, and how do their typical time courses differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, and other. Vertigo and presyncope are typically acute/episodic; disequilibrium and "other" are usually chronic and sustained (p665).

</details>

**3. What proportion of syncope cases remain unexplained despite extensive inpatient evaluation, and what does syncope cost the US annually?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to 40% remain unexplained; syncope-related hospitalizations have been estimated to cost the US $2.4 billion annually (p666).

</details>

**4. What blood pressure criteria define orthostatic hypotension (OH), and at what time points should BP be measured?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A decline of ≥20 mmHg systolic and/or ≥10 mmHg diastolic when moving from supine/sitting to standing, measured immediately and again after a short delay (typically 1 and 3 minutes) (p667).

</details>

**5. What defines postprandial hypotension (PPH), and what fraction of nursing home residents have it?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

A decline of ≥20 mmHg systolic BP within 2 hours of starting a meal. Prevalence is 24% to 36% in nursing home residents (p668).

</details>

**6. What are the objective criteria for carotid sinus hypersensitivity (CSH) on carotid sinus massage?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Asystole ≥3 seconds (cardioinhibitory) and/or a fall in systolic BP >50 mmHg (vasodepressor), or a combination of both (mixed), during carotid sinus massage (p669).

</details>

**7. How long should carotid sinus massage be performed, and when does the maximal heart-rate fall occur?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

5 to 10 seconds (massage is done sequentially on each side); the maximal fall in heart rate usually occurs within 5 seconds of the onset of massage (p669).

</details>

**8. In a large review of hospitalized syncope patients, how often were postural BP measurements actually performed, and how useful were they when done?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only in 38% of episodes — yet they had the highest diagnostic yield of any test, affecting diagnosis in 18%–26%, management in 25%–30%, and determining etiology in 15%–21% of cases (p672).

</details>

**9. What are the diagnostic criteria for BPPV on the Dix-Hallpike maneuver?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Vertigo with rotatory nystagmus; (2) latency of 1–5 seconds between completing the maneuver and onset of symptoms; (3) paroxysmal nystagmus/vertigo lasting 10–20 seconds; and (4) fatigability — decreasing intensity with repeated testing (p673–674).

</details>

**10. How does the time course of episodic dizziness help distinguish BPPV, TIA/migraine, and Meniere disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

BPPV commonly causes positional spells under 1 minute. TIA and vestibular migraine can overlap in duration. Definite Ménière attacks last 20 minutes–12 hours with characteristic auditory findings, and probable attacks can last up to 24 hours (p670; Bárány Society criteria, PMID 25882471).

</details>

**11. What sensitivity and specificity does the presence of heart disease carry for predicting a cardiac cause of syncope?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Sensitivity 95%, specificity 45% (p671).

</details>

**12. What is the diagnostic yield of a 24-hour ambulatory Holter monitor in an unselected syncope population, and when can longer monitoring help?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only 1% to 2% diagnostic yield in unselected populations; a Holter is useful when symptoms are frequent. An implantable loop recorder can capture an infrequent recurrent episode when arrhythmia remains a concern after the initial assessment (p671–672).

</details>

**15. What proportion of ED syncope patients suffer a serious outcome within 7–30 days, and name three high-risk features that warrant hospital admission.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 10%. High-risk features include persistently abnormal vital signs, GI bleed or acute change in hematocrit to <30, volume depletion, acute coronary syndrome, implantable cardiac device dysfunction, valvular heart disease, new undiagnosed murmur, family history of sudden death, new dyspnea/CHF features, prolonged QT or other conduction disease, slow AF (<40 bpm), persistent bradycardia (<40 bpm), Brugada pattern on ECG, or syncope during exertion/supine/sitting (p673 — any three of these).

</details>
