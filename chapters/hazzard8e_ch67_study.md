# Chapter 67 Study: Palliative Care and Special Management Issues

**Study summary only - read the full chapter in the book, pp 1045-1054.**

Fast build from the book; not lane-reviewed.

## Summary

### Definition & scope
- Palliative care addresses serious-illness symptoms, stress and quality of life for patients and families; it can accompany curative treatment at any stage. Primary palliative care includes symptom care, coordination, communication and caregiver support. (p. 1045)
- US Medicare hospice: attending physician and hospice medical director certify expected prognosis **<6 months** if the disease follows its usual course; coverage determinations guide prognosis, rather than impose admission requirements. (p. 1046)
<a id="p1047"></a>
- Trials support quality-of-life and family benefits; noncancer evidence includes less emergency use, hospitalization and symptom burden. Dementia trials have been least promising. (p. 1047)

### Assessment & planning
- Prognostication involves estimating, communicating and interpreting prognosis; compare time to benefit with life expectancy. (p. 1050)
- Clinicians tend to overestimate survival by **3-5-fold**. Combine clinical information, cognition, function and prognostic indices; acknowledge uncertainty. (p. 1051)
- ACP is an ongoing conversation. In the US framework, a proxy designates a decision maker, a living will states future wishes, and POLST records current treatment orders. (p. 1052)

### Management & drugs
<a id="p1048"></a>
- Advanced dementia: offer assisted oral feeding; feeding tubes have not improved survival, quality of life, aspiration prevention or pressure-ulcer healing. (p. 1048)
- Pain may drive behavioral symptoms: acetaminophen was the first step for most patients in a stepwise trial; opioids may be tried for nonresponders. Antipsychotics are not first-line behavioral treatment. (p. 1048)
- Aspiration-pneumonia antibiotics in advanced dementia were associated with longer survival, not better comfort; intravenous treatment/hospitalization caused greatest discomfort. Align infection treatment with goals. (p. 1048)
- Frailty care: consider reversibility, treatment burden and patient goals; a therapeutic trial can clarify potential, with renewed goals discussion if function does not improve. (p. 1049)

### Exam traps
- Palliative care does not require stopping curative therapy. (p. 1045)
- Frailty's exclusion as a principal US hospice billing diagnosis does not itself exclude hospice eligibility. (p. 1049)
- ACP is more than a completed form; preferences may change. (p. 1052)

## Drill

**1. Must a patient stop curative treatment to receive palliative care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No; palliative care can accompany curative treatment at any stage of serious illness. (p. 1045)</details>

**2. What distinguishes primary from specialist palliative care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>All clinicians can provide core symptom management, coordination, goals discussions and caregiver support; specialists contribute an interprofessional team with palliative expertise. (p. 1045)</details>

**3. What prognostic certification does the chapter describe for US Medicare hospice?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The attending physician and hospice medical director certify terminal illness with expected prognosis <strong>&lt;6 months</strong> if the disease follows its usual course. (p. 1046)</details>

**4. Are local coverage determinations strict hospice admission requirements?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. They are guidelines that help physicians determine prognosis. (p. 1046)</details>

**5. What did the cited noncancer palliative-care meta-analysis associate with palliative care?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Less emergency department use, hospitalization and symptom burden; dementia trials had been least promising. (p. 1047)</details>

**6. What should precede medication for dementia-related behavioral symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Identify the behavior and severity, identify and remove physiologic/environmental triggers when possible, and attempt nonpharmacologic interventions. (p. 1047)</details>

**7. What pain-treatment approach reduced behavioral symptoms in a nursing-home trial?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>An empiric stepwise protocol; most patients received acetaminophen at the first step. Opioids may be tried for nonresponders, and PAINAD may help assessment. (p. 1048)</details>

**8. What feeding approach is recommended in advanced dementia with dysphagia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assisted oral feeding rather than percutaneous feeding tubes; tubes have not improved survival, quality of life, aspiration prevention or pressure-ulcer healing. (p. 1048)</details>

**9. How did aspiration-pneumonia antibiotics affect survival and comfort in advanced dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>They were associated with improved survival, not improved comfort; intravenous treatment and hospitalization caused greatest discomfort, with similar survival benefit across administration routes. (p. 1048)</details>

**10. Does absence of a single terminal disease automatically exclude a frail patient from hospice?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>No. Severity and irreversibility of frailty plus multimorbidity can support terminal prognostic certification; the principal-diagnosis billing restriction is a separate issue. (p. 1049)</details>

**11. What domains structure care for multimorbidity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Patient preferences, interpretation of evidence, prognosis, clinical feasibility, and optimization of therapies/care plans. (p. 1049)</details>

**12. Why compare prognosis with time to benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>When life expectancy is shorter than an intervention's time to benefit, immediate harms may occur without an opportunity to receive its benefits. (p. 1050)</details>

**13. In which direction is clinicians' survival estimate commonly biased?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Toward overestimation, by a factor of <strong>3-5</strong>; prognostic indices may improve accuracy over judgment alone. (p. 1051)</details>

**14. How can a clinician express prognostic uncertainty?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Give a time range or best-case, worst-case and most common scenarios; support emotional reactions with empathy and therapeutic silence. (p. 1052)</details>

**15. In the chapter's US framework, how do proxy, living will and POLST differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A health care proxy names a future decision maker; a living will records future treatment wishes; POLST is a standing medical order for current care that accompanies the patient across settings. (p. 1052)</details>
