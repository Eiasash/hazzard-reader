# Falls

Reconstruct the fall, then assess mobility, medicines, vision and the environment. The intervention depends on the risk factor and the setting.

## Risk factors

### Which risk factors carry the most weight?

<a href="?chapter=43#book-table-43-1">Table 43-1</a> grades how consistently a factor predicted falls in prospective studies. Dementia, stroke, advanced age, reduced peripheral sensation, depression, psychoactive medication and use of four or more drugs have strong evidence. Living alone has moderate evidence and may partly reflect age and sex. Orthostatic hypotension has a weaker association in community cohorts. A symptomatic postural pressure drop still deserves attention in an individual who falls when standing (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p635">635</a>).

Demographic: advancing age and female gender in the community; in hospital/institutional settings risk is similar or even higher in men (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>).

Medical: stroke, Parkinson disease, MS, dementia, depression, dizziness, incontinence, arthritis. Incontinence and cognitive impairment matter more in institutions; orthostatic hypotension and vestibular disorders aren't strong *community* risk factors but matter in specific populations (e.g., orthostatic hypotension in Parkinson disease) (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>).

<a href="?chapter=43#book-table-43-1">Table 43-1</a> rates each risk factor's association with falls on a \*/\*\*/\*\*\* scale; **use of ≥4 medications** carries the same top rating (\*\*\*) as psychoactive-drug use, and living alone/inactivity are rated \*\* (<a href="?chapter=43#book-table-43-1">Table 43-1</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#p635">p635</a>).

Consider a man with dementia, diabetes and alcohol use who takes two medicines. Dementia itself belongs in the strongest group. Diabetes becomes especially relevant when examination reveals reduced peripheral sensation. Cohort studies did not show a consistent association with alcohol consumption, although underreporting and selection could obscure an effect. A patient may have peripheral neuropathy, depression, five medicines including duloxetine, and a carotid bruit. The first three correspond to strongly supported risks. A bruit suggests arterial disease and does not identify the cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome that can cause syncope (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p635">635</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#p641">641</a>).

Alcohol has NOT been shown to be a fall-risk factor in large cohort studies (despite causing osteoporosis/cerebellar atrophy/neuropathy) — likely underreporting/selection bias (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>).

Looking at the feet substitutes for position information lost with advanced neuropathy. Looking away removes that visual compensation and the patient loses balance. A motor stroke would more often produce an asymmetric deficit. Vitamin B12 deficiency can also disturb position sense (<a href="?chapter=43#p635">p635</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p636">636</a>, <a href="?chapter=45#p670">670</a>).

### Medication history after a fall

Ask what the patient actually took and when anything changed. Escitalopram is the antidepressant among aspirin, a longstanding angiotensin receptor blocker and mirabegron. Antidepressants, antipsychotics and benzodiazepines are the classes consistently associated with falls in the chapter. The first day after an antihypertensive is started or its dose changed is a distinct risk period. A chronic stable prescription carries a different history (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p636">636</a>).

Medications: psychoactive drugs (benzodiazepine, antidepressant, antipsychotic) → **2–3x** fall risk, **~2x** hip-fracture risk (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>). NSAIDs: most recent systematic review found no significant increase on adjusted analysis (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>). Evidence across antihypertensive classes is inconsistent; some studies suggest lower fall risk with renin–angiotensin system blockers; fall risk is significantly increased within the **24 hours** after starting an antihypertensive or changing its dose (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>).

Environmental: it has been estimated that extrinsic factors are involved in **35–45%** of injurious falls; **1/4 to 1/2** of falls occur away from home; a stairs fall carries **~2x** the injury risk of other falls (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p637">637</a>).

An older man fell while getting up to use the bathroom. Review sedative exposure, pulse-slowing drugs, diuresis, nocturia and orthostatic symptoms. Apixaban chiefly changes the consequences of trauma through bleeding. A medication review can identify a reversible contributor even though trials of withdrawing fall-risk drugs alone did not consistently prevent later falls (<a href="?chapter=43#p634">p634</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p636">636</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#p639">639</a>).

Balance/gait: slower speed, shorter steps, wider step width, more double-support time predict falls; poor trunk/head control while walking impairs gaze stability (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>).

Neuropsychological: poor dual-task performance (e.g., talking while walking), poor executive function/attention, fear of falling and depression → inactivity/isolation (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>).

### Vision, cataract and the lower part of a multifocal lens

Contrast sensitivity is the ability to see an edge when its brightness differs little from the background. A person may read high-contrast letters yet miss a shallow step. Among visual deficits, reduced contrast sensitivity has the strongest evidence. Impaired depth perception can also cause misjudgment of an obstacle (<a href="?chapter=43#p635">p635</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p636">636</a>).

Sensory/neuromuscular: reduced contrast sensitivity/depth perception and reduced knee-extension/ankle-dorsiflexor strength (especially in RACF) are risk factors (<a href="?chapter=43#p636">p636</a>).

A patient with cataract may repeatedly trip on stairs. The first-eye trial found 34% fewer falls with expedited surgery. A similar estimate after second-eye surgery was statistically uncertain. The demonstrated benefit applied to outdoor-active habitual multifocal wearers who received single-lens distance glasses and counseling. Bifocal lower segments can blur the ground just where a stair edge must be located. General prescription updates had inconsistent results (<a href="?chapter=43#p638">pp638</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#p641">641</a>; trial details <a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a> and <a href="?chapter=43#book-figure-43-3">Figures 43-3</a> and <a href="?chapter=43#book-figure-43-4">43-4</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#p640">p640</a>).

Fall = event resulting in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground/floor/lower level (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

Community-dwelling 65+: fall incidence **32–40%**; beyond 75: fall rates **40–50%** (prospective studies; no time frame stated) (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

Hospital falls: **2%** (general hospitals) up to **27%** (acute geriatric wards) (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

Falls = **14%** of ED admissions and **4%** of all hospital admissions in 65+ (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

Falls without injury still matter: fear of falling → reduced mobility/frailty and a key driver of institutionalization (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

## Screening and mobility assessment

Watch the person rise, walk three metres at their usual pace with their usual aid, turn, return and sit. Record the quality of the transfers and gait as well as the time. Distinguish this fall-risk screen from scales for ADL assistance, rehabilitation function and frailty. A slow rise and uncertainty about previous falls warrant further assessment. A review of 25 community studies found limited prediction and could not recommend a time cutoff for the TUG (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#book-table-43-2">Table 43-2</a>).

Ask about falls in the last 12 months + assess balance/gait — previous falls is one of the strongest predictors of a future fall (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>).

TUG test: rise from chair, walk **3 meters**, turn, return, sit; a 25-study systematic review found limited predictive value in community-dwelling older adults and no cut-point for impaired performance could be recommended (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>).

The Alternate Step Test requires rapid weight transfer while the other foot moves onto an 18-cm step. More than ten seconds for eight alternating placements indicates impairment. A time of 17 seconds therefore identifies a specific balance problem. A T-score of −2.7 describes skeletal fragility and changes the likely injury from a fall. Five chair rises in more than 12 seconds indicate impaired performance on a different test. The Short Physical Performance Battery combines standing balance, chair rises and gait, up to 12 points (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>, <a href="?chapter=43#book-table-43-2">Table 43-2</a>).

Alternatives with good predictive accuracy: Alternate Step Test, Sit-to-Stand Test, Short Physical Performance Battery (each domain scored 0–4, max total **12**) (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>).

Hospital (UK NICE): automatically assess all patients **65+**, or **50–64 with a risk condition** (e.g., recent stroke) (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>).

> **Current practice: Inpatient fall assessment.** NICE recommends comprehensive falls assessment for the defined inpatient groups and advises against falls prediction tools. Vitamin D should not be offered solely to prevent falls; address other indications, including deficiency. [NICE NG249, 2025](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng249).

RACF: CaHFRiS screen items — **MMSE < 17**, impulsivity, reduced standing balance, walking-frame use, a fall in the past year, antidepressant/hypnotic-anxiolytic use; absolute 6-month fall risk ranges **0% (no factors) → 100% (≥6 factors)** (<a href="?chapter=43#p637">p637</a>).

## Preventing falls

For a community-dwelling patient, choose an intervention shown to prevent falls. Exercises that challenge balance train the tasks that avert a fall, including shifting weight, turning and taking a recovery step. Across 64 randomized trials there were 23% fewer falls with well-designed programs. Higher balance challenge and at least three prescribed hours per week were associated with larger effects. Individual and group delivery can both work. Routine vitamin D for community fall prevention and a blanket switch to multifocals do not share this evidence (<a href="?chapter=43#p638">p638</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p639">639</a>).

**Medications:** withdrawing centrally-acting drugs did NOT significantly reduce falls in the most recent systematic review (5 trials, **1309** participants; RaR **0.98**, 95% CI 0.63–1.51) — better to prevent uptake than deprescribe later (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). Avoid prescribing benzodiazepines/z-drugs/other psychotropics for insomnia unless there is a clear pattern of addiction or inability to complete a withdrawal program; consider nonpharmacological approaches (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). Vitamin D: no benefit in community-dwelling older adults (even if deficient); may reduce falls in RACF residents (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>); Required reading: see [AGS Beers 2023 required-article card](?chapter=laws&lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023) for anticholinergics with prior falls or fractures (<a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">Table 3</a>, <a href="?chapter=laws&amp;lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023">p2066</a>)

Cochrane review of **43** community trials: multifactorial interventions → **23%** fewer falls overall (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>).

A woman who trips on a rug at night and fractures her wrist needs a home hazard assessment. Remove obstacles such as low furniture and loose rugs. An occupational therapist observes how the person moves through the home, agrees priorities with them and follows up the adaptations. Safer movement matters outside the home too. Intensive programs reduced falls by 21% overall and by 39% in high-risk groups. Advice alone misses implementation and practice (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p640">640</a>).

**Vision:** first-eye cataract surgery → **34%** fewer falls; second-eye surgery similar (~**32%**) but not statistically significant; one prospective cohort study associated cataract surgery with fewer falls provided post-op spectacle lens power updates were cautious (**less than ±0.75 diopter** change) (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). Updating glasses to correct prescription: evidence less clear — one RCT found no significant reduction, and another (vision assessment + treatment) **increased** falls by **~50%** (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). Single-lens distance glasses plus counseling about ground-level hazards for habitual multifocal wearers: **40%** fewer falls in the outdoor-active subgroup (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). Home-safety modification for severe uncorrectable low vision: **41%** fewer falls (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>).

**Home safety:** OT-delivered intensive interventions cut fall risk **21%** overall, **39%** in high-risk groups (<a href="?chapter=43#p640">p640</a>); a cluster RCT of **842 households** found home modifications cut fall-injury rate **26%** overall and **39%** for intervention-specific injuries (<a href="?chapter=43#p640">p640</a>).

**Footwear/podiatry:** multifaceted podiatry → **23%** fewer falls; multifactorial interventions incorporating podiatry → **27%** fewer falls (community) (<a href="?chapter=43#p640">p640</a>); a RACF podiatry pilot was protective at 3 months but the effect was gone by 6 months (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>).

A program that works for an active person in the community may expose a recently discharged patient to tasks beyond current ability. One home program after discharge increased falls, while a supervised program combining resistance and balance training reduced them in long-term care. Decide which deficits to train, what supervision is feasible and how the patient manages between sessions (<a href="?chapter=43#p638">p638</a>–<a href="?chapter=43#p639">639</a>).

**Exercise:** **23%** fewer falls across 64 RCTs (community-dwelling); best results with high balance challenge + **≥3 hrs/week** of prescribed exercise, effective even in 75+ (<a href="?chapter=43#p638">p638</a>). Rapid-stepping training: **52%** fewer falls (7-RCT meta-analysis) (<a href="?chapter=43#p638">p638</a>). CAUTION — not a universal win: not effective in long-term stroke survivors or people just discharged from hospital; one home-based program **increased** falls by **43%** in the recently-discharged (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>). In contrast: RACF resistance + balance training (25 wks) cut falls **55%** (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>); a multifaceted RACF program (staff training/feedback + resident education + environmental adaptations + hip protectors + 2x/week group exercise) cut falls **45%** (<a href="?chapter=43#p639">p639</a>).

**Psychological:** most CBT programs used alone have not prevented falls in people with fear of falling; adding CBT to multifactorial/exercise programs may help (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>).

**Cardiovascular:** consider pacing for symptomatic cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome with recurrent unexplained syncope or falls; test positivity alone does not establish an indication (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>).

RACF: single-component interventions generally ineffective; multidisciplinary multifactorial programs with staff education work (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>).

Hazzard reports no significant reduction in **fractures (n = 5,451)** or **serious fall injuries (n = 9,803)** in two primary-care trials (<a href="?chapter=43#p641">p641</a>). *[sic — the primary reports give the reverse enrollment figures: PreFIT, 9,803 participants (fractures); STRIDE, 5,451 (serious fall injuries).]* Direct delivery and uptake of the intervention may matter when implementing prevention through existing services. [PreFIT](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2001500) · [STRIDE](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2002183).

## Key numbers

- Residential aged care (RACF) residents: fall incidence **30–56%** (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).

- **37–56%** of falls cause minor injury; **10–15%** cause major injury (<a href="?chapter=43#p633">p633</a>).
