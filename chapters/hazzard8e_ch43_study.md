## Which risk factors carry the most weight?

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ea5d2390c7896a41cfa7e3a6 mcq-172d42c25d100b9c49976bb4" data-practice-ids="mcq-5324b8a6225029bff2dabcee mcq-8bb3225a8c4eb0919527c5c1" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Table 43-1 grades how consistently a factor predicted falls in prospective studies. Dementia, stroke, advanced age, reduced peripheral sensation, depression, psychoactive medication and use of four or more drugs have strong evidence. Living alone has moderate evidence and may partly reflect age and sex. Orthostatic hypotension has a weaker association in community cohorts. A symptomatic postural pressure drop still deserves attention in an individual who falls when standing (p634–635).

June 2023 Q3 gives a man with dementia, diabetes and alcohol use who takes two medicines. Dementia itself belongs in the strongest group. Diabetes becomes especially relevant when examination reveals reduced peripheral sensation. Cohort studies did not show a consistent association with alcohol consumption, although underreporting and selection could obscure an effect. May 2024 Q93 gives peripheral neuropathy, depression, five medicines including duloxetine, and a carotid bruit. The first three correspond to strongly supported risks. A bruit suggests arterial disease and does not identify the cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome that can cause syncope (p634–635, 641).

In December 2021 Q61, looking at the feet substitutes for position information lost with advanced neuropathy. Looking away removes that visual compensation and the patient loses balance. A motor stroke would more often produce an asymmetric deficit. Vitamin B12 deficiency can also disturb position sense, which explains its inclusion alongside advanced age in 2020 Q30 (p635–636, 670).

## Medication history after a fall

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-3929e741ee0d01c333a58865" data-practice-ids="mcq-4cd9324a98a56ce5a20d1d9d" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Ask what the patient actually took and when anything changed. In June 2025 Q42, escitalopram is the antidepressant among aspirin, a longstanding angiotensin receptor blocker and mirabegron. Antidepressants, antipsychotics and benzodiazepines are the classes consistently associated with falls in the chapter. The first day after an antihypertensive is started or its dose changed is a distinct risk period. A chronic stable prescription carries a different history (p634–636).

The man in 2020 Q99 fell while getting up to use the bathroom. Review sedative exposure, pulse-slowing drugs, diuresis, nocturia and orthostatic symptoms. Apixaban chiefly changes the consequences of trauma through bleeding. A medication review can identify a reversible contributor even though trials of withdrawing fall-risk drugs alone did not consistently prevent later falls (p634–636, 639).

## What the mobility tests can tell you

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-041747d2188be93271d452d3 mcq-a95e9c5ca09aca36f108c948 mcq-2fdd846e83242323d98210d5" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Watch the person rise, walk three metres at their usual pace with their usual aid, turn, return and sit. Record the quality of the transfers and gait as well as the time. June 2023 Q69 distinguishes this fall-risk screen from scales for ADL assistance, rehabilitation function and frailty. In June 2026 Q2 a slow rise and uncertainty about previous falls warrant further assessment. A review of 25 community studies found limited prediction and could not recommend a time cutoff for the TUG (p637, Table 43-2).

The Alternate Step Test requires rapid weight transfer while the other foot moves onto an 18-cm step. More than ten seconds for eight alternating placements indicates impairment. The 17 seconds in September 2024 Q19 therefore identifies a specific balance problem. A T-score of −2.7 describes skeletal fragility and changes the likely injury from a fall. Five chair rises in more than 12 seconds indicate impaired performance on a different test. The Short Physical Performance Battery combines standing balance, chair rises and gait, up to 12 points (p637, Table 43-2).

## Vision, cataract and the lower part of a multifocal lens

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-1075f6c0fed2b86a8c672639 mcq-ebb48fc2c1941aee8ce92d21" data-practice-ids="mcq-57c4cc79fa07a7e570f0a73b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Contrast sensitivity is the ability to see an edge when its brightness differs little from the background. A person may read high-contrast letters yet miss a shallow step. Of the visual functions offered in June 2023 Q6, reduced contrast sensitivity has the strongest evidence. Impaired depth perception can also cause misjudgment of an obstacle (p635–636).

December 2021 Q63 describes a patient with cataract who repeatedly trips on stairs. The first-eye trial found 34% fewer falls with expedited surgery. A similar estimate after second-eye surgery was statistically uncertain. June 2025 Q17 also awards credit for spectacle fitting. The demonstrated benefit applied to outdoor-active habitual multifocal wearers who received single-lens distance glasses and counseling. Bifocal lower segments can blur the ground just where a stair edge must be located. General prescription updates had inconsistent results (published Q17 pages 638 and 641, trial details p639 and Figures 43-3 and 43-4, p640).

## Choosing an intervention for the person and setting

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e71329f82512960352dd33a0 mcq-ff953c4f5cde2966a8ac98cc" data-practice-ids="mcq-c24f583e2f83fe771a93990b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

June 2023 Q49 asks which single intervention has the best support in community dwellers. Exercises that challenge balance train the tasks that avert a fall, including shifting weight, turning and taking a recovery step. Across 64 randomized trials there were 23% fewer falls with well-designed programs. Higher balance challenge and at least three prescribed hours per week were associated with larger effects. Individual and group delivery can both work. Routine vitamin D for community fall prevention and a blanket switch to multifocals do not share this evidence (p638–639).

June 2022 Q35 describes a woman who tripped on a rug at night and fractured her wrist. September 2024 Q1 asks about removing obstacles such as low furniture and rugs. An occupational therapist observes how the person moves through the home, agrees priorities with them and follows up the adaptations. Safer movement matters outside the home too. Intensive programs reduced falls by 21% overall and by 39% in high-risk groups. Advice alone misses implementation and practice (p639–640).

A program that works for an active person in the community may expose a recently discharged patient to tasks beyond current ability. One home program after discharge increased falls, while a supervised program combining resistance and balance training reduced them in long-term care. Decide which deficits to train, what supervision is feasible and how the patient manages between sessions (p638–639).

## Summary

### Epidemiology
- Fall = event resulting in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground/floor/lower level (p633).
- Community-dwelling 65+: fall incidence **32–40%**; beyond 75: fall rates **40–50%** (prospective studies; no time frame stated) (p633).
- Residential aged care (RACF) residents: fall incidence **30–56%** (p633).
- Hospital falls: **2%** (general hospitals) up to **27%** (acute geriatric wards) (p633).
- **37–56%** of falls cause minor injury; **10–15%** cause major injury (p633).
- Falls = **14%** of ED admissions and **4%** of all hospital admissions in 65+ (p633).
- Falls without injury still matter: fear of falling → reduced mobility/frailty and a key driver of institutionalization (p633).

### Risk Factors
- Demographic: advancing age and female gender in the community; in hospital/institutional settings risk is similar or even higher in men (p634).
- Alcohol has NOT been shown to be a fall-risk factor in large cohort studies (despite causing osteoporosis/cerebellar atrophy/neuropathy) — likely underreporting/selection bias (p634).
- Medical: stroke, Parkinson disease, MS, dementia, depression, dizziness, incontinence, arthritis. Incontinence and cognitive impairment matter more in institutions; orthostatic hypotension and vestibular disorders aren't strong *community* risk factors but matter in specific populations (e.g., orthostatic hypotension in Parkinson disease) (p634).
- Medications: psychoactive drugs (benzodiazepine, antidepressant, antipsychotic) → **2–3x** fall risk, **~2x** hip-fracture risk (p634). NSAIDs: most recent systematic review found no significant increase on adjusted analysis (p636). Some antihypertensive classes (angiotensin system blocking medications) are protective; fall risk is significantly increased within the **24 hours** after starting an antihypertensive or changing its dose (p636).
- Balance/gait: slower speed, shorter steps, wider step width, more double-support time predict falls; poor trunk/head control while walking impairs gaze stability (p636).
- Sensory/neuromuscular: reduced contrast sensitivity/depth perception and reduced knee-extension/ankle-dorsiflexor strength (especially in RACF) are risk factors (p636).
- Neuropsychological: poor dual-task performance (e.g., talking while walking), poor executive function/attention, fear of falling and depression → inactivity/isolation (p636).
- Environmental: it has been estimated that extrinsic factors are involved in **35–45%** of injurious falls; **1/4 to 1/2** of falls occur away from home; a stairs fall carries **~2x** the injury risk of other falls (p636–637).
- Table 43-1 rates each risk factor's association with falls on a \*/\*\*/\*\*\* scale; **use of ≥4 medications** carries the same top rating (\*\*\*) as psychoactive-drug use, and living alone/inactivity are rated \*\* (Table 43-1, p635).

### Screening & Assessment
- Ask about falls in the last 12 months + assess balance/gait — previous falls is one of the strongest predictors of a future fall (p637).
- TUG test: rise from chair, walk **3 meters**, turn, return, sit; a 25-study systematic review found limited predictive value in community-dwelling older adults and no cut-point for impaired performance could be recommended (p637).
- Alternatives with good predictive accuracy: Alternate Step Test, Sit-to-Stand Test, Short Physical Performance Battery (each domain scored 0–4, max total **12**) (p637).
- Hospital (UK NICE): automatically assess all patients **65+**, or **50–64 with a risk condition** (e.g., recent stroke) (p637).
- RACF: CaHFRiS screen items — **MMSE < 17**, impulsivity, reduced standing balance, walking-frame use, a fall in the past year, antidepressant/hypnotic-anxiolytic use; absolute 6-month fall risk ranges **0% (no factors) → 100% (≥6 factors)** (p637).

### Prevention — Single Interventions
- **Exercise:** **23%** fewer falls across 64 RCTs (community-dwelling); best results with high balance challenge + **≥3 hrs/week** of prescribed exercise, effective even in 75+ (p638). Rapid-stepping training: **52%** fewer falls (7-RCT meta-analysis) (p638). CAUTION — not a universal win: not effective in long-term stroke survivors or people just discharged from hospital; one home-based program **increased** falls by **43%** in the recently-discharged (p639). In contrast: RACF resistance + balance training (25 wks) cut falls **55%** (p639); a multifaceted RACF program (staff training/feedback + resident education + environmental adaptations + hip protectors + 2x/week group exercise) cut falls **45%** (p639).
- **Vision:** first-eye cataract surgery → **34%** fewer falls; second-eye surgery similar (~**32%**) but not statistically significant; one prospective cohort study associated cataract surgery with fewer falls provided post-op spectacle lens power updates were cautious (**less than ±0.75 diopter** change) (p639). Updating glasses to correct prescription: evidence less clear — one RCT found no significant reduction, and another (vision assessment + treatment) **increased** falls by **~50%** (p639). Single-lens distance glasses plus counseling about ground-level hazards for habitual multifocal wearers: **40%** fewer falls in the outdoor-active subgroup (p639). Home-safety modification for severe uncorrectable low vision: **41%** fewer falls (p639).
- **Medications:** withdrawing centrally-acting drugs did NOT significantly reduce falls in the most recent systematic review (5 trials, **1309** participants; RaR **0.98**, 95% CI 0.63–1.51) — better to prevent uptake than deprescribe later (p639). Avoid prescribing benzodiazepines/z-drugs/other psychotropics for insomnia unless there is a clear pattern of addiction or inability to complete a withdrawal program; consider nonpharmacological approaches (p639). Vitamin D: no benefit in community-dwelling older adults (even if deficient); may reduce falls in RACF residents (p639); Required reading: see [AGS Beers 2023 required-article card](?chapter=laws&lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023) for anticholinergics with prior falls or fractures (Table 3, p2066)
- **Home safety:** OT-delivered intensive interventions cut fall risk **21%** overall, **39%** in high-risk groups (p640); a cluster RCT of **842 households** found home modifications cut fall-injury rate **26%** overall and **39%** for intervention-specific injuries (p640).
- **Footwear/podiatry:** multifaceted podiatry → **23%** fewer falls; multifactorial interventions incorporating podiatry → **27%** fewer falls (community) (p640); a RACF podiatry pilot was protective at 3 months but the effect was gone by 6 months (p641).
- **Psychological:** most CBT programs used alone have not prevented falls in people with fear of falling; adding CBT to multifactorial/exercise programs may help (p641).
- **Cardiovascular:** pacemaker implantation for cardioinhibitory carotid sinus hypersensitivity reduces drop attacks, syncope, and fall frequency (p641).

### Prevention — Multifactorial
- Cochrane review of **43** community trials: multifactorial interventions → **23%** fewer falls overall (p641).
- RACF: single-component interventions generally ineffective; multidisciplinary multifactorial programs with staff education work (p641).
- Two large primary-care trials found no significant reduction in their respective primary outcomes: **fractures in PreFIT (9,803 participants)** and **serious fall injuries in STRIDE (5,451 participants)**. Direct delivery and uptake of the intervention may matter when implementing prevention through existing services (p641; [PreFIT](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2001500), [STRIDE](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2002183)).
