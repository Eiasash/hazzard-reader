# Chapter 76 Study: Heart Failure

## Summary

### Definition & Epidemiology
- HF is a syndrome (not a single disease) resulting from any structural/functional cardiac disorder impairing ventricular filling or ejection (p1165).
- **~6.5 million** Americans affected; projected to exceed **8 million by 2030**; **>1 million** new cases/year. Prevalence **doubles every decade after 40**, exceeding **10%** in both men and women over 80 (p1166).
- **1 million** primary-HF hospital admissions in 2014: **71%** in patients >65y, **53%** ≥75y, **25%** occurred in the **2%** of the population ≥85y. Almost **2 million** ambulatory visits in 2016 — HF ranks 2nd only to hypertension among CV outpatient reasons (p1166).
- Annual cost **>$35 billion**; projected **$69.8 billion by 2030** (≈**$244**/US adult) (p1166).

### Aging Physiology (4 principal effects)
- (1) Reduced β-adrenergic responsiveness — receptor **desensitization**, not reduced receptor density (p1167).
- (2) Increased arterial stiffness → increased afterload/impedance to LV ejection (p1168).
- (3) Altered diastolic filling — impaired early relaxation, increased reliance on atrial "kick": up to **30–40%** of LV end-diastolic volume from atrial contraction in older adults (vs a small fraction in youth) (p1168–1169).
- (4) Altered mitochondrial energy metabolism — normal at rest, can't meet demand under stress (p1170).
- Restrictive Doppler filling pattern (A-wave <**50%** of E-wave amplitude) = advanced disease with poor prognosis, not attributable to aging alone (p1169).

### Etiology & Precipitants
- HTN + CAD account for **>70%** of HF causes (p1170).
- ATTRwt (wild-type transthyretin) cardiac amyloidosis found in **13%** of older adults hospitalized for HFPEF with LV wall thickness **>12 mm**; the ATTRv variant is present in up to **4%** of African-Americans (p1171).
- High-output failure causes: chronic anemia, hyperthyroidism, thiamine deficiency, AV shunting (dialysis fistula/AVM), morbid obesity — often overlooked (p1172).
- Most common precipitant: medication/diet nonadherence, up to **two-thirds** of exacerbations (p1172).
- **30–50%** of older HF adults take ≥1 medication that can cause/worsen HF: NSAIDs, corticosteroids, estrogens, TZDs (rosi-/pioglitazone), minoxidil, clonidine, excess β-blocker/CCB, class Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics (p1173).

### Clinical Features & Diagnosis
- Classic signs: pulmonary rales, elevated JVP, abdominojugular reflux, S3 gallop, pitting edema — but often unreliable in older adults (p1173).
- **Highly specific** in older adults: pulsus alternans, S3 gallop, JVD at rest or with abdominojugular reflux maneuver (p1173).
- BNP **>500 pg/mL** in the right clinical context is highly suggestive of active HF; a normal value (**<100 pg/mL**) in a nonobese older adult makes HF much less likely — but natriuretic-peptide specificity **declines with age** and is lower with higher BMI (p1174).
- Diagnostic workup Class I (most patients): CBC, chemistries, TSH, BNP/NT-proBNP, UA, CXR, ECG, 2D echo with Doppler (Table 76-6, p1174).

### HFREF vs HFPEF
- HFREF "usually defined as ejection fraction **less than 40% to 50%**" (p1175) — the chapter then narrows this to a specific working cutoff for the rest of the discussion: **EF <45%** for HFREF and **EF ≥45%** for HFPEF (p1175). Know both numbers (see Exam traps below).
- No single clinical feature reliably distinguishes HFREF from HFPEF; echo/MRI/radionuclide angiography needed (p1175).
- **>50%** of older HF patients have HFPEF (p1184).
- **70–80%** of older HFPEF patients have hypertension (p1186).

### Nonpharmacologic Management
- Nonadherence/behavioral factors drive readmission: **64%** of exacerbations tied to medication/diet nonadherence in one study, **26%** to emotional/environmental factors; in a 140-patient cohort (age ≥70), **47%** were readmitted within 90 days (p1176).
- Meta-analysis of 33 disease-management trials: HF readmissions **↓42%**, all-cause readmissions **↓24%**, mortality **↓20%** (p1176).
- Sodium target **1500–3000 mg/d** — expert consensus, not trial-derived (p1176).
- PICNIC trial (120 malnourished patients, mean age 79): intensive dietary counseling → death/HF hospitalization at 1y **27% vs 61%** (*p*<0.001) (p1177).
- HF-ACTION (2331 patients, EF ≤35%): exercise → **7%** reduction in death/hospitalization, unadjusted (ns, *p*=0.13); **11%** reduction after adjustment (*p*=0.03) (p1177–1178).

### Drugs for HFREF (name — dose — key caution)
- **Carvedilol** — start 3.125–6.25 mg BID → target 25–50 mg BID (p1179).
- **Metoprolol succinate** — start 12.5–25 mg daily → target 100–200 mg daily; (tartrate 6.25 mg BID–QID is a titration alternative) (p1179).
- **Bisoprolol** — start 1.25–2.5 mg daily → target 10 mg daily (p1179). Only these 3 β-blockers have proven HF mortality benefit.
- **Captopril** — start 6.25–12.5 mg TID; **enalapril** start 2.5–5 mg BID; monitor BP/renal function/K+ during titration (p1179).
- **Sacubitril/valsartan (ARNi)** — start low, e.g. 24/26 mg PO BID in older adults; needs ≥36h washout from ACEi to avoid angioedema (p1181).
- **Spironolactone** — 12.5–50 mg daily; **eplerenone** — 25–50 mg daily; both contraindicated if creatinine **≥2.5 mg/dL** or hyperkalemia (p1181).
- **Hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate** — older-adult start 12.5–25 mg TID–QID / 10 mg TID–QID (p1182).
- **Loop diuretics** — furosemide 20–160 mg, bumetanide 0.5–5 mg, torsemide 5–100 mg/day; add **metolazone 2.5–10 mg daily** if refractory (p1182).
- **Digoxin** — 0.125 mg daily in older adults with normal renal function; target serum level **0.5–0.9 ng/mL**; toxicity can occur even at "therapeutic" levels (p1182–1183).
- **Refractory HF** — high-dose oral furosemide 200 mg BID or bumetanide 10 mg daily; or continuous IV furosemide 5–40 mg/h or bumetanide 0.5–1 mg/h (p1189).
- **Tafamidis** (ATTR cardiac amyloidosis) — meglumine 80 mg daily or free-salt 61 mg daily (not per-mg substitutable); ATTR-ACT: mortality **29.5% vs 42.9%** placebo; cost **~$225,000/year** (p1188).

### Device Therapy
- ICD: NYHA II–III HFREF, EF **≤35%** (primary prevention) or post-arrest VT/VF (secondary); benefit declines with age; patients **≥80y** are **twice as likely** as younger patients to have major implant complications (p1184).
- CRT: NYHA II–IV HFREF, EF **≤35%**, QRS **≥150 ms**; improves symptoms/survival even in octogenarians, unlike ICD (p1184).
- MitraClip (COAPT trial, **n=614**, HFREF + moderate-severe secondary MR despite max GDMT): lower HF hospitalization and lower all-cause mortality vs medical therapy alone (p1184).

### HFPEF-Specific Therapy
- Only **SGLT2 inhibitors** show consistent benefit in HFPEF trials to date.
- EMPEROR-Preserved (**n=5988**, EF **>40%**): death/HF hospitalization HR **0.79** (95% CI 0.69–0.90, *p*<0.001), driven by fewer HF hospitalizations; effect consistent regardless of diabetes status (p1188).
- Digoxin **not recommended** in HFPEF except as an adjunct for rate control in AF (p1188).

### Prognosis & End-of-Life
- 5-year survival among older adults with established HF is **<50%**; in patients **≥80y** hospitalized for HF, **fewer than 25%** survive >5 years (p1190).
- Death causes: **40–50%** progressive HF, **40%** arrhythmias, **10–20%** other (p1190).
- Overall HF survival is lower than for most cancers (p1190).

### Prevention
- Hypertension treatment reduces HF incidence by **>50%** across multiple trials (p1176).
- HYVET: treating HTN in patients ≥80y → **64%** reduction in incident HF. SPRINT intensive arm (<120 mmHg) → **36%** lower rate of acute decompensated HF vs standard (140 mmHg) arm (p1191).

### Exam traps / book contradictions
- **Two EF numbers for HFREF, not a contradiction — a general range narrowed to a working cutoff**: the chapter first gives the general literature range ("less than 40% to 50%"), then explicitly adopts **EF <45%** as its working definition for HFREF (**≥45%** for HFPEF) for the rest of the discussion (p1175). A question could test either number.
- β-blockers: only carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol are proven in HFREF trials — a distractor option naming any other β-blocker (e.g., atenolol, metoprolol tartrate as primary agent) is wrong even though metoprolol tartrate is an acceptable titration-phase alternative (p1179).
- Digoxin lowers HF hospitalizations but has **no mortality benefit** in either HFREF (DIG trial) or HFPEF (DIG ancillary) — a trap for assuming any drug that reduces hospitalizations also reduces mortality (p1182, p1188).

## Drill

**1. Roughly how many Americans are affected by heart failure today, and what is the projected total by 2030?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 6.5 million today; projected to exceed 8 million by 2030 (p1166).

</details>

**2. Of 2014 primary-HF hospital admissions, what fraction occurred in patients 75 years or older, and what fraction in the (much smaller) population aged 85 and up?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

53% were in patients ≥75y; 25% occurred in the 2% of the population aged ≥85y (p1166).

</details>

**3. Name the four principal age-related changes in the cardiovascular system that predispose to heart failure.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Reduced β-adrenergic responsiveness (receptor desensitization), (2) increased arterial stiffness/afterload, (3) altered left ventricular diastolic filling with increased reliance on atrial contraction, (4) altered mitochondrial energy metabolism under stress (p1166–1170).

</details>

**4. In older adults, what proportion of left ventricular end-diastolic volume can be attributable to atrial contraction (the atrial "kick")?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

As much as 30–40% (p1168–1169).

</details>

**5. What proportion of older adults hospitalized for HFPEF with increased LV wall thickness (>12 mm) were found to have wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

13% (p1171).

</details>

**6. What is the single most common precipitant of an acute heart failure exacerbation in a patient with preexisting HF?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nonadherence to medications and/or diet — contributing to up to two-thirds of exacerbations (p1172).

</details>

**7. What BNP cutoffs make heart failure highly likely versus much less likely in a nonobese older adult, and why does BNP become a less reliable rule-in test with age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

>500 pg/mL is highly suggestive of active HF; <100 pg/mL makes HF much less likely. Natriuretic peptide levels rise modestly with normal aging (especially in women) and with declining renal function, so specificity for HF declines with age (p1174).

</details>

**8. The chapter gives a general EF range for HFREF, then a narrower working cutoff. What are the two numbers?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

General range: "less than 40% to 50%." Working cutoff adopted for the rest of the chapter's discussion: EF <45% for HFREF (HFPEF is ≥45%) (p1175).

</details>

**9. Which three β-blockers are proven, evidence-based agents for HFREF, and what are their target maintenance doses?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Carvedilol 25–50 mg BID, metoprolol succinate 100–200 mg daily, bisoprolol 10 mg daily (p1179).

</details>

**10. Why must sacubitril/valsartan (ARNi) initiation be timed carefully relative to a prior ACE inhibitor?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

At least a 36-hour washout from ACE inhibitors is required to mitigate the risk of angioedema (p1181).

</details>

**11. What creatinine threshold contraindicates mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone/eplerenone), and what is spironolactone's typical dose range?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Creatinine ≥2.5 mg/dL (or preexisting hyperkalemia); spironolactone 12.5–50 mg once daily (p1181).

</details>

**12. What target serum digoxin concentration is associated with improved survival in HFREF, and does digoxin reduce mortality?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0.5–0.9 ng/mL; no — digoxin reduced HF hospitalizations in the DIG trial but did not reduce mortality (34.8% vs 35.1%, not different from placebo) (p1182–1183).

</details>

**13. At what ejection fraction and QRS duration is cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) indicated, and how does its benefit differ from an ICD's?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

EF ≤35% and QRS ≥150 ms in NYHA II–IV HFREF. Unlike an ICD (which reduces sudden death but does not improve quality of life), CRT improves symptoms, exercise tolerance, quality of life, and survival — including in octogenarians (p1184).

</details>

**14. Which drug class is the only one with consistent trial benefit in HFPEF, and what was the key outcome in its largest preserved-EF trial?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

SGLT2 inhibitors. In EMPEROR-Preserved (n=5988, EF >40%), empagliflozin reduced death or HF hospitalization (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.90, p<0.001), driven mainly by fewer HF hospitalizations, regardless of diabetes status (p1188).

</details>

**15. What is the 5-year survival for older adults with established heart failure, and for patients 80 years or older specifically hospitalized with heart failure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Less than 50% for older adults generally; fewer than 25% for patients ≥80y hospitalized with heart failure survive more than 5 years (p1190).

</details>
