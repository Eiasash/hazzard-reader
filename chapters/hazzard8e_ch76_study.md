## Reading the examination and the BNP together

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-551b3101ea16a79edb40dc20 mcq-1464838102c98ff3a34184c7" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

When a patient wakes breathless or coughs up frothy sputum, the first question is whether left-sided filling pressure has risen. A fourth heart sound just before S1 reflects late filling against a stiff ventricle. A hypertensive patient with an S4 can develop pulmonary edema without raised neck veins or swollen legs. In contrast, a displaced apex and an S3 favor a dilated, poorly contracting ventricle. These findings shift probability rather than measure EF. An echocardiogram assesses ventricular size, EF, wall thickness, filling and valves (pp1173–1175, Table 76-7).

The aging ventricle relaxes more slowly and relies more on atrial contraction. Rapid AF shortens diastole and removes the atrial contribution. A small change in rate or volume can therefore precipitate symptoms even when the EF was preserved at the patient's last examination. Reduced beta-adrenergic reserve limits the compensatory heart-rate response, and arterial stiffness increases the load against which the ventricle ejects (pp1167–1170).

BNP reflects myocardial stretch. An older person with CKD can have a high result without a new episode of pulmonary edema. Age, particularly in women, and anemia also push levels upward, while higher BMI pushes them downward. In a nonobese older patient a value below 100 pg/mL weighs against active HF, whereas a value above 500 pg/mL supports it when the history and examination agree. Neither value distinguishes HFrEF from HFpEF. Consider alternative pulmonary causes and use chest imaging, ECG and Doppler echocardiography to answer the next question (pp1173–1175).

## Finding the precipitant and the underlying disease

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0081f896ab2a7645732697e6 mcq-6109da3d3d42107aafeb13c6 mcq-3b316d09c1a5d91bc326475c mcq-38f357fdf35289d175b07a3e" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

In established HF, ask what changed before escalating the maintenance dose. Missing tablets, being unable to obtain refills, urinary urgency after the morning diuretic, or dependence on salty prepared food can all produce recurrent congestion. In the series summarized in the chapter, difficulty following medication or diet accounted for up to two-thirds of exacerbations. Among cardiac triggers, ischemia and atrial arrhythmias are especially important. Infection, anemia, thyroid disease, acute kidney dysfunction and newly prescribed drugs can further narrow an older patient's limited reserve (pp1172–1173, Table 76-4).

In the June 2023 Basic case a woman with diastolic HF becomes hypoxemic soon after receiving IV fluid for a febrile urinary infection. The bilateral opacities, timing and limited capacity to accommodate volume favor hydrostatic edema. Support oxygenation, reassess further fluids, treat the congestion and continue treating the infection. The printed range begins with the precipitant on p1172. The [diuretic paragraph](?chapter=76#exam-p1182-mcq-6109da3d3d42107aafeb13c6) explains the treatment separately (pp1172–1175, 1182).

With systemic NSAIDs, reduced prostaglandin activity can both damage the upper gastrointestinal mucosa and impair renal sodium excretion. A proton-pump inhibitor lowers the likelihood of upper gastrointestinal ulcer bleeding but does not prevent kidney injury, higher pressure or congestion. Review over-the-counter analgesics in someone whose diuretics appear to have stopped working. May 2024 Q14 draws the gastrointestinal teaching from Chapter 52 and the HF exacerbation from Chapter 76 (pp787–788, 1173).

Thiamine deficiency, severe chronic anemia, hyperthyroidism and arteriovenous shunting can cause high-output failure. A high cardiac output still falls short of the body's requirements. Wet beriberi is the classic exam association. Look for limited food intake or prolonged diuretic exposure when deficiency is plausible. Replace deficiency without assuming that all patients taking furosemide benefit from routine supplementation (pp1171–1172, Table 76-3).

Amyloid infiltration produces stiff, thickened ventricles. Prior carpal tunnel syndrome, especially bilateral disease, is a clue when HF coincides with otherwise unexplained thickening or atrial arrhythmia. An EF of 40% has many possible causes, and normal BNP does not identify cardiac amyloid. All four options received credit in June 2022 Q55. In a selected group of older patients hospitalized with HFpEF and LV wall thickness over 12 mm, 13% had wild-type ATTR cardiac amyloidosis. That denominator matters when considering testing (p1171, ACC cardiac amyloidosis consensus 2023, Table 1).

## Choosing treatment by the outcome it changes

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d034cd9715f2f2dd845cca98 mcq-cb7d90148e8999fdf9097145 mcq-65f1b2348078e99a77093d23 mcq-d6f42ab8d1be3a2ddc5790b8 mcq-777d91eb7840ca302219d066" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

For stable HFrEF, distinguish the four disease-modifying treatment classes from diuretics for symptom relief. An ARNI or suitable alternative modifies renin–angiotensin signaling, evidence-based beta blockade reduces harmful sympathetic stimulation, an MRA blocks aldosterone and an SGLT2 inhibitor lowers HF events even without diabetes. Start and titrate around blood pressure, pulse, kidney function, potassium and what matters to the patient. With persistent edema, a small increase in creatinine alone is insufficient reason to stop decongestion. If the patient instead becomes dry and orthostatic, reduce the diuretic intensity and reassess (pp1179–1183).

Carvedilol, sustained-release metoprolol succinate and bisoprolol have HFrEF outcome evidence. Atenolol and propranolol cannot inherit those outcomes simply by belonging to the same class. SENIORS also found that nebivolol reduced death or cardiovascular hospitalization in a population aged 70 or older. These findings support starting an appropriate drug after stabilization. Severe decompensation, marked bradycardia and advanced heart block change the immediate decision (p1179).

Compared with enalapril in PARADIGM-HF, sacubitril/valsartan reduced cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization, with more hypotension but less renal impairment and severe hyperkalemia. The risk is lower, not absent. Check pressure, creatinine and potassium and allow at least 36 hours after an ACE inhibitor to reduce angioedema risk. If first-line renin–angiotensin agents cannot be tolerated, hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate offers combined arterial and venous dilation. The evidence is for the combination, and multiple daily doses matter when planning support at home (pp1180–1182).

Eplerenone does not directly slow the heart. Bisoprolol acts through beta blockade, ivabradine slows the sinus pacemaker, and digoxin slows AV nodal conduction. In a euvolemic older patient with controlled AF, CKD and a digoxin level of 1.4 ng/mL, reconsider the dose before attributing exertional symptoms to inadequate diuresis. The chapter's low HF concentration range is 0.5–0.9 ng/mL. Declining renal function and hypokalemia increase toxicity risk even when a laboratory labels the concentration therapeutic (pp1179, 1181–1183).

The June 2022 post-discharge question credits two options. For someone taking propranolol, a switch to carvedilol after stabilization addresses HFrEF outcomes. Adding spironolactone can also be appropriate if eGFR is above 30 mL/min/1.73 m² and potassium is below 5.0 mEq/L, with both rechecked after starting. The fluid advice bundled with each option must be considered separately. Table 76-8 warns against excess intake, especially with hyponatremia. Four cups per day is not a general HF prescription, and avoiding all exertion can accelerate deconditioning (pp1176–1179, 1181, AHA/ACC/HFSA 2022, §7.3.3).

Influenza can trigger decompensation, so vaccination belongs in the management plan. The chapter describes an association with lower mortality. The subsequent randomized IVVE trial found a mortality reduction during peak influenza circulation but no significant mortality difference over the full observation period. Loop diuretics relieve breathlessness and edema without an established survival benefit. The distinction between a cohort association, a trial endpoint and symptomatic benefit is what makes June 2023 Q74 difficult (pp1176, 1179–1182, IVVE, PMID 36400089).

## HFpEF trials and adverse effects

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-edd8a3bd4e1d30138e1e61de mcq-538cb2dab0c6ea72540a81e9" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

For HFpEF, use a diuretic for pulmonary or systemic congestion and titrate it carefully. A stiff ventricle still depends on adequate preload. Excessive diuresis can lower stroke volume, cause postural symptoms and reduce renal perfusion. Treat hypertension and associated ischemia or AF, and individualize blood pressure targets for a frail patient at risk of falls. Preserved EF does not justify routine use of a beta blocker for prognosis (pp1186–1187).

In CHARM-Preserved, candesartan reduced HF hospitalizations without a demonstrated mortality reduction. In EMPEROR-Preserved, empagliflozin reduced the combined risk of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization, chiefly through fewer admissions. The separate mortality component was not significantly reduced. In the ancillary DIG trial for preserved EF, an early reduction in HF admissions did not persist as a significant difference across complete follow-up. Digoxin is generally reserved for another indication, such as adjunctive rate control in AF. These are distinct endpoints and distinct reasons for prescribing (pp1185–1188).

SGLT2 inhibition can cause glycosuria, genital fungal infection, volume depletion and, rarely, ketoacidosis. An early fall in eGFR can reflect the expected hemodynamic change, whereas hypotension with poor intake calls for assessment for true volume depletion or kidney injury. Long-term renal protection is compatible with short-term volume sensitivity. In June 2026 Basic Q147, hypomagnesemia belongs more naturally with loop or thiazide diuresis (pp1181–1182, 1578).

## Devices, prognosis and the treatment decision

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-61c1dacfc6182349c975f40d mcq-78efef2ee60d19f031350bbc" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

CRT treats delayed ventricular activation. A patient with class III HFrEF despite tolerated therapy, EF at or below 35%, sinus rhythm, LBBB and QRS at least 150 ms fits the strongest indication. NYHA class III is only one component. An EF of 20% with a narrow QRS does not supply an electrical delay to correct. In contrast, an ICD treats lethal ventricular arrhythmia and need not improve exertional symptoms. Discuss expected survival, competing illness, procedural risks and the possibility of future shock deactivation (pp1184, 1190).

Repeated admissions, severe activity limitation and end-organ dysfunction despite optimized treatment prompt an advanced-HF assessment. A durable LVAD can improve survival and function in a selected older person with refractory systolic failure. Screen for comorbidity, support, ability to manage the device and the patient's goals. Chronic inotrope infusion can palliate symptoms or bridge to advanced therapy but has no established long-term survival benefit. A mitral clip targets significant secondary regurgitation rather than mild regurgitation incidentally seen on echo. Palliative consultation is valuable before a decision about implantation (pp1184, 1189–1190).

At a population level, outcomes are poorer in men, in ischemic HF, and with elevated BNP, renal impairment or severe functional limitation. Female sex is the favorable association among the December 2021 Q59 choices. At the bedside, the patient's trajectory and goals matter more than any one characteristic. For advanced disease, revisit the patient's views on hospitalization, resuscitation and future device management with the patient and the people they wish to involve (p1190).

## Right heart failure and care across the course of illness

Isolated right-sided failure produces systemic venous congestion with less prominent lung findings. Consider pulmonary hypertension from lung disease or chronic thromboembolism, tricuspid disease, right ventricular infarction and acute pulmonary embolism. Assess the cause before treating edema alone. Advanced biventricular failure can also produce marked right-sided signs. New AF, infection, ischemia, drug effects or excess sodium and fluid can mimic progression to truly refractory HF (pp1172–1173, 1189).

When frailty, malnutrition or poor access makes the treatment plan difficult to carry out, ask what the patient can manage at home. Arrange medication reconciliation, accessible follow-up and help with food and activity. After stabilization, graded aerobic and resistance exercise can restore function. For a patient taking a rate-slowing drug, perceived exertion may be more helpful than an age-predicted heart-rate target. The following sections retain the chapter's trial populations, doses and quantitative findings for reference (pp1176–1179).

## Summary

### Definition & Epidemiology
- HF is a syndrome (not a single disease) resulting from any structural/functional cardiac disorder impairing ventricular filling or ejection (p1165).
- **~6.5 million** Americans affected; projected to exceed **8 million by 2030**; **>1 million** new cases/year. Prevalence **doubles every decade after 40**, exceeding **10%** in both men and women over 80 (p1166).
- Rising HF incidence/prevalence reflects more than population aging: improved survival from CAD, stroke, renal disease, cancer, and infections, plus growing obesity/diabetes, expand the pool of patients who live long enough to develop HF (Table 76-1, p1166).
- **1 million** primary-HF hospital admissions in 2014: **71%** in patients >65y, **53%** ≥75y, **25%** occurred in the **2%** of the population ≥85y. Almost **2 million** ambulatory visits in 2016 — HF ranks 2nd only to hypertension among CV outpatient reasons (p1166).
- Annual cost **>$35 billion**; projected **$69.8 billion by 2030** (≈**$244**/US adult) (p1166).

### Aging Physiology (4 principal effects)
- (1) Reduced β-adrenergic responsiveness — receptor **desensitization**, not reduced receptor density (p1167).
- (2) Increased arterial stiffness → increased afterload/impedance to LV ejection (p1168).
- (3) Altered diastolic filling — impaired early relaxation, increased reliance on atrial "kick": up to **30–40%** of LV end-diastolic volume from atrial contraction in older adults (vs a small fraction in youth) (p1168–1169).
- (4) Altered mitochondrial energy metabolism — normal at rest, can't meet demand under stress (p1170).
- Restrictive Doppler filling pattern (A-wave <**50%** of E-wave amplitude) = advanced disease with poor prognosis, not attributable to aging alone (p1169).

### Etiology & Precipitants
- HTN + CAD account for **>70%** of HF causes (p1170).
- ATTRwt (wild-type transthyretin) cardiac amyloidosis found in **13%** of older adults hospitalized for HFPEF with LV wall thickness **>12 mm**; the ATTRv variant is present in up to **4%** of African-Americans (p1171).
- High-output failure causes: chronic anemia, hyperthyroidism, thiamine deficiency, AV shunting (dialysis fistula/AVM), morbid obesity — often overlooked (p1172).
- Most common precipitant: medication/diet nonadherence, up to **two-thirds** of exacerbations (p1172).
- **30–50%** of older HF adults take ≥1 medication that can cause/worsen HF: NSAIDs, corticosteroids, estrogens, TZDs (rosi-/pioglitazone), minoxidil, clonidine, excess β-blocker/CCB, class Ia/Ic antiarrhythmics (p1173).

### Clinical Features & Diagnosis
- Classic signs: pulmonary rales, elevated JVP, abdominojugular reflux, S3 gallop, pitting edema — but often unreliable in older adults (p1173).
- **Highly specific** in older adults: pulsus alternans, S3 gallop, JVD at rest or with abdominojugular reflux maneuver (p1173).
- BNP **>500 pg/mL** in the right clinical context is highly suggestive of active HF; a normal value (**<100 pg/mL**) in a nonobese older adult makes HF much less likely — but natriuretic-peptide specificity **declines with age**, and levels are generally lower with higher BMI (p1174).
- Diagnostic workup Class I (most patients): CBC, chemistries, TSH, BNP/NT-proBNP, UA, CXR, ECG, 2D echo with Doppler, **and cardiac catheterization/coronary angiography if angina or significant ischemia** (unless not a revascularization candidate) (Table 76-6, p1174).
- HFREF vs HFPEF exam clues: HFREF trends younger/male with prior MI, displaced PMI, S3 gallop, Q waves; HFPEF trends older/female with hypertension/diabetes/obesity/aortic stenosis, preserved PMI, S4 gallop (Table 76-7, p1175).

### HFREF vs HFPEF
- HFREF "usually defined as ejection fraction **less than 40% to 50%**" (p1175) — the chapter then narrows this to a specific working cutoff for the rest of the discussion: **EF <45%** for HFREF and **EF ≥45%** for HFPEF (p1175). Know both numbers (see Exam traps below).
- No single clinical feature reliably distinguishes HFREF from HFPEF; echo/MRI/radionuclide angiography needed (p1175).
- **>50%** of older HF patients have HFPEF (p1184).
- **70–80%** of older HFPEF patients have hypertension (p1186).

### Nonpharmacologic Management
- Nonadherence/behavioral factors drive readmission: **64%** of exacerbations tied to medication/diet nonadherence in one study, and emotional/environmental factors contributed to **26%** of readmissions; in a 140-patient cohort (age ≥70), **47%** were readmitted within 90 days (p1176).
- Meta-analysis of 33 disease-management trials: HF readmissions **↓42%**, all-cause readmissions **↓24%**, mortality **↓20%** (p1176).
- Sodium target **1500–3000 mg/d** — expert consensus, not trial-derived (p1176).
- Dietary consultation also covers avoidance of excess fluid intake (**>2 L/d**, especially if Na **<130**) alongside avoidance of excess sodium (**>2–3 g/d**), plus low-fat/low-cholesterol and adequate-calorie guidance (Table 76-8, p1177).
- Exercise is contraindicated with decompensated NYHA IV HF, severe aortic stenosis/hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, recent MI/unstable angina (within 2wk), life-threatening arrhythmias not adequately treated, or any acute serious illness (eg, pneumonia) (Table 76-9, p1178).
- PICNIC trial (120 malnourished patients, mean age 79): intensive dietary counseling → death/HF hospitalization at 1y **27% vs 61%** (*p*<0.001) (p1177).
- HF-ACTION (2331 patients, EF ≤35%): exercise → **7%** reduction in death/hospitalization, unadjusted (ns, *p*=0.13); **11%** reduction after adjustment (*p*=0.03) (p1177–1178).

### Drugs for HFREF (name — dose — key caution)
- **Carvedilol** — start 3.125–6.25 mg BID → target 25–50 mg BID (p1179).
- **Metoprolol succinate** — start 12.5–25 mg daily → target 100–200 mg daily; (tartrate 6.25 mg BID or QID is a titration alternative) (p1179).
- **Bisoprolol** — start 1.25–2.5 mg daily → target 10 mg daily (p1179). The book calls these "the only three drugs that were studied and subsequently demonstrated benefit" — yet the same page reports **SENIORS** (age ≥70, n=2128): **nebivolol** significantly lowered death or CV hospitalization (p1179).
- **Captopril** — start 6.25–12.5 mg TID; **enalapril** start 2.5–5 mg BID; monitor BP/renal function/K+ during titration (p1179).
- **Sacubitril/valsartan (ARNi)** — start low, e.g. 24/26 mg PO BID in older adults; needs ≥36h washout from ACEi to avoid angioedema (p1180–1181).
- **Steroidal MRAs in HFrEF.** The book gives spironolactone **12.5–50 mg once daily** and eplerenone **25–50 mg once daily**. It lists **creatinine ≥2.5 mg/dL** or **preexisting hyperkalemia** as contraindications and calls for close renal-function and potassium monitoring in older patients (p1181).
  > Current practice (not the book): <span id="current-practice-1" data-reader-metadata="navigation"></span>For symptomatic HFrEF, AHA/ACC/HFSA recommends starting an MRA if eGFR is **>30 mL/min/1.73 m²** and serum potassium is **<5.0 mEq/L**, with close monitoring at initiation and afterward. [2022 heart failure guideline, section 7.3.3](https://professional.heart.org/en/-/media/832EA0F4E73948848612F228F7FA2D35.pdf).
- **SGLT2 inhibitors in HFrEF.** DAPA-HF and EMPEROR-Reduced showed fewer HF events in patients with or without diabetes (p1181). The trials improved their respective composite outcomes, with differing effects on cardiovascular mortality. [DAPA-HF](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1911303) · [EMPEROR-Reduced](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2022190).
- **Hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate** — older-adult start 12.5–25 mg TID–QID / 10 mg TID–QID (p1182).
- **Loop diuretics** — furosemide 20–160 mg, bumetanide 0.5–5 mg, torsemide 5–100 mg/day; add **metolazone 2.5–10 mg daily** if refractory (p1182).
- **Digoxin** — 0.125 mg daily in older adults with normal renal function; target serum level **0.5–0.9 ng/mL**; toxicity can occur even at "therapeutic" levels (p1182–1183).
- **Refractory HF** — high-dose oral furosemide 200 mg BID or bumetanide 10 mg daily; or continuous IV furosemide 5–40 mg/h or bumetanide 0.5–1 mg/h (p1189).
- **Tafamidis** (ATTR cardiac amyloidosis) — meglumine 80 mg daily or free-salt 61 mg daily (not per-mg substitutable); ATTR-ACT: mortality **29.5% vs 42.9%** placebo; cost **~$225,000/year** (p1188).

### Device Therapy
- ICD: NYHA II–III HFREF, EF **≤35%** (primary prevention) or post-arrest VT/VF (secondary); benefit declines with age; patients **≥80y** are **twice as likely** as younger patients to have major implant complications (p1184).
- **CRT:** the strongest standard indication includes LVEF **≤35%**, sinus rhythm, **LBBB** and QRS **≥150 ms**, with NYHA II, III or ambulatory IV symptoms despite guideline-directed therapy. In selected older adults, CRT improves symptoms, quality of life and survival. ICD therapy targets arrhythmic sudden death (p1184, AHA/ACC/HFSA 2022).
- MitraClip (COAPT trial, **n=614**, HFREF + moderate-severe secondary MR despite max GDMT): lower HF hospitalization and lower all-cause mortality vs medical therapy alone (p1184).

### HFPEF-Specific Therapy
- SGLT2 inhibitors reduced HF events in preserved/mildly reduced EF trials. Subsequent **FINEARTS-HF** also demonstrated a reduction with finerenone in the composite rate of total worsening HF events and cardiovascular deaths among participants with LVEF ≥40% (p1184 describes the earlier evidence).
- **EMPEROR-Preserved:** in 5,988 patients with EF >40%, empagliflozin reduced the combined risk of **cardiovascular death or hospitalization for HF** (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.90, p<0.001), mainly through fewer HF hospitalizations. Results were consistent regardless of diabetes. PRESERVED-HF, a smaller dapagliflozin trial of 324 patients, assessed symptoms and exercise function at 12 weeks (p1185, 1188).
- Digoxin **not recommended** in HFPEF except as an adjunct for rate control in AF (p1188).

### Prognosis & End-of-Life
- 5-year survival among older adults with established HF is **<50%**; in patients **≥80y** hospitalized for HF, **fewer than 25%** survive >5 years (p1190).
- Death causes: **40–50%** progressive HF, **40%** arrhythmias, **10–20%** other (p1190).
- Overall HF survival is lower than for most cancers (p1190).

### Prevention
- Hypertension treatment reduces HF incidence by **>50%** across multiple trials (p1176).
- HYVET: treating HTN in patients ≥80y → **64%** reduction in incident HF. SPRINT intensive arm (<120 mmHg) → **36%** lower rate of acute decompensated HF vs standard (140 mmHg) arm (p1191).

### Exam traps / book contradictions
- **Two EF numbers for HFREF, not a contradiction — a general range narrowed to a working cutoff**: the chapter first gives the general literature range ("less than 40% to 50%"), then explicitly adopts **EF <45%** as its working definition for HFREF (**≥45%** for HFPEF) for the rest of the discussion (p1175). A question could test either number.
- The three **guideline-recommended HFrEF beta blockers** are carvedilol, metoprolol succinate and bisoprolol. US labeling for bisoprolol alone is for hypertension, although its HFrEF efficacy is established. Among 2,128 patients aged ≥70, SENIORS found a benefit from nebivolol on the composite of death or cardiovascular hospitalization. These are separate evidence and regulatory questions (p1179).
- Digoxin lowers HF hospitalizations but has **no mortality benefit** in either HFREF (DIG trial) or HFPEF (DIG ancillary) — a trap for assuming any drug that reduces hospitalizations also reduces mortality (p1182, p1188).

**1. What US heart failure prevalence did Hazzard report, and how many affected adults did the cited projection anticipate by 2030?**

Hazzard reported about **6.5 million** US patients and projected **over 8 million by 2030** (p1166). Both figures refer to the chapter's reporting period and forecast.

**2. Of 2014 primary-HF hospital admissions, what fraction occurred in patients 75 years or older, and what fraction in the (much smaller) population aged 85 and up?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

53% were in patients ≥75y; 25% occurred in the 2% of the population aged ≥85y (p1166).

</details>

**3. Name the four principal age-related changes in the cardiovascular system that predispose to heart failure.**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

(1) Reduced β-adrenergic responsiveness (receptor desensitization), (2) increased arterial stiffness/afterload, (3) altered left ventricular diastolic filling with increased reliance on atrial contraction, (4) altered mitochondrial energy metabolism under stress (p1166–1170).

</details>

**7. What BNP cutoffs make heart failure highly likely versus much less likely in a nonobese older adult, and why does BNP become a less reliable rule-in test with age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

> A BNP above 500 pg/mL strongly supports active heart failure in the appropriate clinical context. A BNP below 100 pg/mL makes heart failure much less likely in a nonobese older adult. Natriuretic peptide levels rise modestly with normal aging (especially in women) and with declining renal function, so specificity for HF declines with age (p1174).

</details>

**8. The chapter gives a general EF range for HFREF, then a narrower working cutoff. What are the two numbers?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

General range: "less than 40% to 50%." Working cutoff adopted for the rest of the chapter's discussion: EF <45% for HFREF (HFPEF is ≥45%) (p1175).

</details>

**9. Which three β-blockers are proven, evidence-based agents for HFREF, and what are their target maintenance doses?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Carvedilol 25–50 mg BID, metoprolol succinate 100–200 mg daily, bisoprolol 10 mg daily (p1179).

</details>

**11. Which renal and potassium findings does Hazzard list as contraindications to an MRA in HFrEF, and what spironolactone dose range does it give?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Hazzard lists **creatinine ≥2.5 mg/dL** or **preexisting hyperkalemia** as contraindications. It calls for close monitoring of renal function and serum potassium in older patients. The spironolactone dose is **12.5–50 mg once daily** (p1181).

</details>

**12. What target serum digoxin concentration is associated with improved survival in HFREF, and does digoxin reduce mortality?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

0.5–0.9 ng/mL (DIG analyses: low-dose levels in this range *may* be associated with improved survival); no — digoxin reduced HF hospitalizations in the DIG trial but did not reduce mortality (34.8% vs 35.1%, not different from placebo) (p1182–1183).

</details>

**13. At what ejection fraction and QRS duration is cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) indicated, and how does its benefit differ from an ICD's?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

The clearest CRT indication is symptomatic NYHA II, III or ambulatory IV HFrEF despite guideline-directed treatment, **EF ≤35%**, sinus rhythm, **LBBB** and **QRS ≥150 ms**. CRT can improve function and survival in selected octogenarians. An ICD prevents arrhythmic sudden death (p1184).

</details>

**14. Which drug class was tested in EMPEROR-Preserved, and which outcome drove the trial's composite benefit?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Empagliflozin, an **SGLT2 inhibitor**, reduced cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization in EMPEROR-Preserved (n=5,988, EF >40%, HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.69–0.90). Fewer HF hospitalizations drove the result, irrespective of diabetes status (p1188).

</details>

**15. What is the 5-year survival for older adults with established heart failure, and for patients 80 years or older specifically hospitalized with heart failure?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Less than 50% for older adults generally; fewer than 25% for patients ≥80y hospitalized with heart failure survive more than 5 years (p1190).

</details>
