# Osteoporosis

A low-trauma fracture can change treatment even when bone density alone does not. Assess fracture risk, secondary causes and the consequences of interrupting therapy.

## Bone strength and fracture risk

### When a fracture changes the treatment decision

A fall from standing height sufficient to fracture a vertebra identifies skeletal fragility even when the previous DXA showed only low bone mass. A low-trauma L2 fracture changes the decision: this patient needs secondary prevention alongside nutrition and fall assessment. An oral bisphosphonate is reasonable when the patient can follow its administration instructions. A yearly infusion is an alternative when swallowing or adherence is a problem and renal function permits. <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a> gives the available regimens (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">pp768</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p773">773</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p776">776</a>).

DXA compares mineral density with a young-adult reference mean. Read the T-score rather than the age-matched Z-score on an older adult's report. An L2–L4 T-score is −3.5, so the threshold of −2.5 has already been crossed. Alendronate 70 mg weekly is a treatment dose. Extra calcium alone cannot supply the antifracture benefit. Hazzard lists fragility fractures of the **wrist, vertebrae or hip** as sufficient to trigger therapy without BMD testing. A baseline BMD can still help monitor therapy (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">p768</a>).

> **Current practice: Fracture treatment regardless of BMD.** BHOF recommends treatment after hip or vertebral fracture regardless of BMD. After a distal forearm fracture it recommends treatment with low bone mass and individualization with normal BMD. [BHOF Clinician’s Guide, secondary fracture prevention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9546973/).

As many as **1/3 or more** of those with "osteopenia" on a plain x-ray may have a T-score worse than **−2.5**, and as many as **half** will be in the **−2.5 to −1.0** range — so x-ray "osteopenia" is an indication for BMD measurement (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">p768</a>).

In one study, **3/4** of postmenopausal women with a distal radius fracture were undiagnosed or untreated; as many as **50%** of women with a hip fracture leave hospital without treatment (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">p768</a>).

Harrison's June 2022 question about vertebral fracture was testing the history, not a numerical DXA threshold. Loss of height, kyphosis or a new vertebral deformity may reveal a fracture that went unrecognized. A previous vertebral fracture predicts another in either sex. The face and skull are excluded from the usual definition of osteoporotic fragility fractures (Hazzard <a href="?chapter=51#p768">pp768</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p770">770</a>). [Harrison 20e, Chapter 404, Definition](https://accessmedicine.mhmedical.com/content.aspx?bookid=2129&sectionid=192530678)

Repeat fracture risk in the first year after a fracture: **20%** (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">p768</a>).

### Connecting bone biology to prevention

Osteocytes respond to mechanical loading. Osteoblast-lineage cells regulate osteoclast development through RANK ligand, and its decoy receptor osteoprotegerin limits that signal. Resorption and formation normally replace bone in coupled cycles. With advancing age, fewer bone-forming cells and a shift of marrow progenitors toward adipocytes reduce replacement of resorbed bone. Loss of estrogen accelerates remodeling at menopause. Sex-steroid loss and secondary disorders contribute in older men as well (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">pp760</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p768">768</a>).

<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-1">Table 51-1</a> on <a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a> prints **normal >−1**, **osteopenia ≤1 but >−2.5** *[sic — the upper bound lacks a minus sign]*, **osteoporosis ≤−2.5**, and severe osteoporosis **≤−2.5 plus a fragility fracture**.

> **Current practice: DXA diagnostic thresholds.** Standard DXA interpretation uses normal **≥−1.0**, low bone mass **below −1.0 and above −2.5**, and osteoporosis **≤−2.5**. [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/diagnosis-information/bone-density-examtesting/).

**54 million** Americans at risk of fracture; **~10 million** have osteoporosis, **43 million** have low bone mass (NOF 2018) (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>).

Women: **>250,000** hip + **>500,000** spine fractures/year. Men: **250,000** fractures/year, **75,000** hip (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>).

By age 90: **1/3 of women**, **1/6 of men** will have a hip fracture; lifetime vertebral fracture risk **12%** in both sexes (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>).

**20%–25%** mortality in the year after hip fracture; **~50%** of women do not fully recover prior function (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>).

Annual US cost **>$22 billion**; projected **$25 billion**/3 million fractures by 2025 (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>).

Cortical bone predominates in long bones. Hazzard prints that trabecular bone predominates in vertebrae and pelvis and “makes up 80% of skeletal mass” (<a href="?chapter=51#p760">p760</a>). *[sic — adult skeletal mass is approximately 80% cortical and 20% trabecular; WHO Technical Report Series 921.]* Trabecular bone has greater metabolic activity.

Menopause: rapid spine bone loss **~3%/year** for ~5 years, then **>0.5%/year** generalized (<a href="?chapter=51#p765">p765</a>).

Age, prior fracture, gait and strength help explain why people with the same T-score have different risks. Standard FRAX estimates 10-year fracture probability but omits fall history. Use a falls history and a brief functional examination alongside DXA. A person who needs their arms to rise from a chair may have a remediable fall risk. Post-fracture physical therapy can address strength, balance and body mechanics. In residential care, the ability to swallow and follow a dosing schedule, renal function and the facility's ability to maintain treatment determine whether a chosen regimen is practical (<a href="?chapter=51#p769">pp769</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p772">772</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p778">778</a>).

Each 1-SD drop in T-score ≈ **doubles** fracture risk (<a href="?chapter=51#p759">p759</a>).

**Postmenopausal** osteoporosis: women **51–75 years**, driven by estrogen loss. **Senile** osteoporosis: **>60 years**, both sexes, from reduced bone turnover/osteoblast numbers (<a href="?chapter=51#p759">p759</a>).

Bone remodeling cycle (basic multicellular unit) lasts **6–9 months** (<a href="?chapter=51#p763">p763</a>).

Age-related decline **~0.5%/year** after the 3rd decade; marrow fat can occupy up to **90%** of marrow cavity in older adults (<a href="?chapter=51#p766">p766</a>).

**Osteosarcopenia**: the term for concurrent osteopenia/osteoporosis + sarcopenia, coined from muscle-bone cross-talk research (<a href="?chapter=51#p767">p767</a>).

Men: testosterone declines **~1.2%/year** with aging (<a href="?chapter=51#p767">p767</a>).

Estrogen & androgen both **inhibit bone resorption and stimulate bone formation** (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-2">Table 51-2</a> lists them under stimulators of formation and inhibitors of resorption); vitamin D is listed as a stimulator of **both resorption and formation** (as are fibroblast growth factors and platelet-derived growth factor) (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-2">Table 51-2</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p764">p764</a>).

## Assessment and secondary causes

After an unexpected compression fracture or markedly low density, ask what can be reversed. Excess thyroid hormone accelerates remodeling, so the hyperthyroidism row pairs the diagnosis with TSH and thyroxine. Hyperparathyroidism points instead to calcium and PTH, myeloma to a blood count and protein electrophoresis, and androgen deprivation or hypogonadism to the endocrine history in men. Female sex helps estimate baseline risk. It is not a secondary illness. Distinguish a brief prednisone burst from cumulative exposure, and remember that type 2 diabetes may raise fracture risk even with relatively preserved BMD (<a href="?chapter=51#p768">pp768</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p769">769</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-3">Table 51-3</a>).

Secondary-cause workup: primary hyperparathyroidism → ionized calcium + PTH; Paget disease → alkaline phosphatase; glucocorticoid use → medical history (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-3">Table 51-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p769">p769</a>).

Prednisone can reduce osteoblast activity and increase osteoclast-mediated resorption, with bone loss often beginning within six months of starting therapy. The chapter describes an association with ≥7.5 mg/day for more than three months. Age, fracture history and BMD help determine the need for pharmacologic prevention (<a href="?chapter=51#p769">p769</a>). Chronic steroid exposure may suppress the adrenal stress response (Ch 97 <a href="?chapter=97#p1532">p1532</a>).

> **Current practice: Assessment during glucocorticoid therapy.** ACR recommends fracture-risk assessment in adults receiving prednisone-equivalent **≥2.5 mg/day for >3 months**. The ESE/Endocrine Society guidance distinguishes oral stress dosing in minor illness from parenteral coverage when vomiting or hemodynamic instability is present. [ACR glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.25240) · [ESE/Endocrine Society 2024, recommendation 3.1 and Table 8](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae250).

Key clinical risk factors: **age** (the single most critical contributor), current smoking, body weight **< 127 lb**, corticosteroid use **> 3 months**, prior fracture, family history (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-4">Table 51-4</a>) (<a href="?chapter=51#p769">p769</a>).

**FRAX**: estimates 10-year absolute fracture risk; major limitation — **does not include falls** (<a href="?chapter=51#p770">p770</a>).

**Garvan tool**: includes history of previous falls; gives **5- and 10-year** risk (<a href="?chapter=51#p770">p770</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=51#book-figure-51-7">Figure 51-7</a>** algorithm: combines fall/fracture/age/SARC-F screening with DXA + ALM to jointly diagnose osteoporosis and sarcopenia (<a href="?chapter=51#p770">p770</a>).

**SARC-F** score **≥ 4** predicts clinically important sarcopenia (~95% specificity) (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

Fall-risk red flags: can't hold a **semi-tandem stand 10 seconds**; gait speed **< 0.8 m/s**; **Timed Up and Go > 14 seconds** (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

DXA is preferred; in >200,000 postmenopausal women screened in primary care, ~**50%** had previously undetected low BMD (**7%** osteoporosis); BMD screening reduced hip-fracture incidence by **36%** in another study (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

> **Current practice: Who to screen for osteoporosis.** The USPSTF recommends screening women aged 65 or older and postmenopausal women younger than 65 whose clinical risk assessment indicates increased risk. Evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against screening men. This applies to adults without known osteoporosis or a fragility fracture. [USPSTF, 2025](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/osteoporosis-screening).

10-year fracture probability in an 80-year-old is **>2×** that of a 50-year-old at the same T-score (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

## Nutrition and prevention

Hazzard discusses total elemental calcium **1,200–1,500 mg/day** and vitamin D **at least 800 IU/day**, sometimes **2,000 IU/day or more** to reach a bone-disease target of **75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL)** (<a href="?chapter=51#p772">p772</a>).

> **Current practice: Calcium intake.** Current BHOF calcium targets from diet plus supplements are **1,200 mg/day for women ≥51 and men ≥71** and **1,000 mg/day for men 50–70**. Individualize supplementation and avoid exceeding the total target through unnecessary tablets ([BHOF calcium and vitamin D](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/calciumvitamin-d/get-the-facts-on-calcium-and-vitamin-d/)).

Frank deficiency (**≤30 nmol/L**): loading dose **50,000 IU** cholecalciferol or ergocalciferol; all others: daily **1000–4000 IU** cholecalciferol (<a href="?chapter=51#p773">p773</a>).

All residents: vitamin D **≥800 IU/day** + total calcium **1200 mg/day** from food and supplements as needed. Individualize in CKD or hypercalcemia and check 25(OH)D first (<a href="?chapter=51#p778">p778</a>).

If 25(OH)D **≤50 nmol/L** → 2000 IU/day; if **≤30 nmol/L** → loading dose **50,000 IU**, then **3000–4000 IU/day** (<a href="?chapter=51#p778">p778</a>).

Bisphosphonates are first-line (IV favors adherence); denosumab an equal alternative given SC route (<a href="?chapter=51#p778">p778</a>).

## Drug treatment and follow-up

### Choosing treatment, interrupting it and recognizing harm

The date of the last dose can identify the drug. Two missed denosumab injections can be followed by new lumbar compression fractures. Denosumab blocks RANK ligand and loses its effect between doses because it does not bind bone mineral. <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a> gives **60 mg subcutaneously every six months** and lists rapid loss of bone after cessation. The prose on <a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a> misprints the unit. Arrange timely injections or a transition to another antiresorptive, especially if vertebral fractures have already occurred (<a href="?chapter=51#p774">pp774</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p776">776</a>). [Prolia prescribing information, §§2.2 and 5.6](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=49e5afe9-a0c7-40c4-af9f-f287a80c5c88)

**Alendronate 70 mg orally weekly** reduced hip and vertebral fracture risk by roughly 50% over three years in the studies summarized (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p774">p774</a>). The age subgroups ≤75 and >75 had **51% and 38%** lower vertebral fracture risk at 12 months, respectively. Estimated time to benefit in those ≥70 was **8 months** (<a href="?chapter=51#p775">p775</a>). <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a> gives **eGFR <35 mL/min** as a contraindication (<a href="?chapter=51#p774">p774</a>).

> **Current practice: Alendronate and renal function.** US labeling does not recommend alendronate at **creatinine clearance <35 mL/min**. Contraindications to oral use include delayed esophageal emptying, inability to remain upright for 30 minutes and hypocalcemia (FDA Fosamax prescribing information).

**Risedronate** 35 mg weekly PO (or 75 mg × 2 consecutive days/month, or 150 mg monthly PO): **41%–49%** ↓ vertebral, **36%** ↓ nonvertebral fracture over 3 years; also approved for glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p774">p774</a>).

**Denosumab** 60 mg SC every 6 months (dose per <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a>, <a href="?chapter=51#p774">p774</a> — the chapter's own prose on <a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a> prints "60 μg," a book table-vs-text discrepancy, see chapter note): **40%** ↓ hip, **68%** ↓ vertebral, **20%** ↓ nonvertebral fracture; antifracture effect declines to pretreatment levels **12 months** after stopping, and fracture risk rises markedly in those with prior vertebral fracture (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a>).

> **Current practice: Denosumab, advanced CKD and stopping treatment.** Plan antiresorptive follow-on before stopping or delaying denosumab. The US boxed warning identifies severe, sometimes fatal hypocalcemia in advanced CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²), especially dialysis or CKD–mineral and bone disorder. Before treatment, a clinician experienced in CKD-MBD should assess mineral and bone status and correct hypocalcemia. Ensure appropriate calcium and activated vitamin D. In advanced CKD monitor serum calcium weekly for the first month and monthly thereafter. Most severe events occurred 2–10 weeks after injection, especially weeks 2–5. [FDA boxed warning, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-boxed-warning-increased-risk-severe-hypocalcemia-patients-advanced-chronic-kidney-disease) · [Prolia label, §§2.2 and 5.1, 2026](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=49e5afe9-a0c7-40c4-af9f-f287a80c5c88).

**Teriparatide 20 micrograms SC daily** reduced vertebral and nonvertebral fractures by **65% and 53%**, respectively, in the trial (<a href="?chapter=51#p774">pp774</a>,<a href="?chapter=51#p776">776</a>). <a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a> limits therapy to **24 months** because of the osteosarcoma signal in rats; <a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a> likewise says **no longer than two years**.

> **Current practice: Teriparatide treatment duration.** FDA removed the teriparatide osteosarcoma boxed warning in 2020. The risk remains in §5.2. US labeling permits treatment **beyond two years only if high fracture risk persists or returns**. Avoid in people at increased baseline osteosarcoma risk, including open epiphyses, Paget disease, bone metastases, prior skeletal malignancy or radiation involving the skeleton. Avoid in underlying hypercalcemic disorders as a separate precaution (FDA Forteo prescribing information). [FDA label change, 2020](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/appletter/2020/021318Orig1s054ltr.pdf). [Forteo prescribing information, §§2.3 and 5.2](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/021318s059lbl.pdf).

<a href="?chapter=51#book-table-51-5">Table 51-5</a> lists **approximately 57%** lower vertebral fracture risk with **abaloparatide 80 μg SC daily** and a maximum duration of **24 months** (<a href="?chapter=51#p774">p774</a>). *[sic — the chapter’s 18-month trial account gives an **86%** reduction in new vertebral fractures and **43%** in nonvertebral fractures (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>).]*

> **Current practice: Abaloparatide duration.** US labeling does not recommend treatment for more than **two years in a lifetime**. [Tymlos prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=712143d9-e21e-4013-bb3b-3426a21060a8).

Bisphosphonates are retained in bone and can still exert an effect after treatment stops. Consider a patient with no previous fracture and a hip T-score above −2.5 after seven years of alendronate. A monitored holiday can be considered if current risk is modest. Reassess age, falls, interval fractures and BMD before pausing. Reassess during the pause because a new fracture or substantial density loss can change the decision. Patients with high ongoing risk may benefit from continuing longer (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">pp776</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p777">777</a>).

**Romosozumab 210 mg SC monthly for 12 doses**. In FRAME, romosozumab versus placebo for one year, followed by denosumab in both groups, reduced new vertebral fractures **75% at two years**. In ARCH, romosozumab versus alendronate for one year, followed by alendronate in both groups, reduced vertebral, nonvertebral and hip fractures by **48%, 19% and 38%**, respectively (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>).

> **Current practice: Romosozumab and cardiovascular risk.** The anabolic effect wanes over the course. Follow treatment with an antiresorptive when indicated. US labeling advises against starting within **one year of MI or stroke** and calls for individual cardiovascular risk assessment (FDA Evenity boxed warning). [Evenity prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=471baba2-7154-4488-9891-0db2f46791e7).

Teriparatide and abaloparatide should be considered as **starting therapy** for **very high fracture risk** and/or multiple fractures (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>); after teriparatide, treatment with an antiresorptive is imperative to maintain BMD gains (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a>).

Hazzard limits **teriparatide and abaloparatide to 2 years** because animal studies found osteosarcoma with teriparatide (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">pp776</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p777">777</a>). It limits **romosozumab to 12 months**, citing excess cardiovascular events in one phase 3 trial and a concern about possible oncogenesis extrapolated from the teriparatide rodent findings (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>).

New thigh or groin pain after several years of bisphosphonate exposure warrants femur radiographs for a possible incomplete atypical femoral fracture. Look for a transverse or short oblique lesion of the lateral cortex in the subtrochanteric region or shaft, sometimes with local cortical thickening. Symptoms can precede a complete fracture. If plain films are unrevealing and suspicion persists, MRI may identify a stress injury. The absolute risk is low compared with the number of usual fragility fractures prevented (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">pp776</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p777">777</a>).

**AFF**: **3.2–50 per 100,000** person-years on bisphosphonates; **>100 per 100,000** with long-term use (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>). Hazzard states that **fracture precedes pain in most cases**. New groin or thigh pain warrants femur radiographs for an atypical femoral fracture; a bone scan or MRI may be needed (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">pp776</a>–<a href="?chapter=51#p777">777</a>).

> **Current practice: Prodromal thigh pain.** FDA bisphosphonate labeling notes that prodromal thigh or groin pain may occur weeks or months before a **complete** fracture. [Alendronate prescribing information](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=81589178-10c9-4806-91eb-54e638d35b8d).

Raloxifene's demonstrated fracture benefit is vertebral. Zoledronic acid can cause a transient flu-like illness after infusion. Jaw osteonecrosis is rare with osteoporosis doses and is a recognized complication of denosumab as well. Think of exposed jaw bone that has failed to heal for eight weeks, especially after an extraction. Teriparatide gives a daily, intermittent PTH signal that favors bone formation. Plan an antiresorptive afterward to preserve BMD gains (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a>).

**Zoledronic acid 5 mg IV yearly for three years** reduced vertebral, nonvertebral and hip fracture risk by **70%, 25% and 41%**, respectively, in the postmenopausal osteoporosis trial (<a href="?chapter=51#p775">p775</a>). A separate trial in patients treated after hip-fracture repair reported deaths in **9.6%** with zoledronic acid versus **13%** with placebo (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a>).

> **Current practice: Zoledronic acid trial results.** HORIZON reported the unrounded placebo mortality of **13.3%** versus **9.6%** with zoledronic acid. US labeling contraindicates the osteoporosis infusion in acute renal impairment or CrCl <35 mL/min. [HORIZON Recurrent Fracture Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17878149/).

**ONJ**: very rare at osteoporosis dosing, **0.001%–0.01%** (<a href="?chapter=51#p776">p776</a>); in postmenopausal women on oral bisphosphonates specifically, **0.02%–0.06%** — a dental check should *not* delay treatment (<a href="?chapter=51#p777">p777</a>).

A SARC-F score ≥ 4 (out of 10) is predictive; SARC-F assesses Strength, Assistance with walking, Rising from a chair, Climbing stairs, and Falls (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

## Key numbers

- SOF cohort: **54%** of women who suffered a hip fracture had a hip T-score *better* than −2.5 at baseline — **BMD alone under-captures risk**; age matters more (<a href="?chapter=51#p771">p771</a>).

- High-dose vitamin D **bolus** dosing (500,000 IU/year or 60,000 IU/month) **increases fall risk** — avoid (<a href="?chapter=51#p773">p773</a>).

> **Current practice: Vitamin D for prevention in otherwise healthy adults.** For generally healthy adults aged 75 or older, the Endocrine Society suggests empiric supplementation, preferably with daily lower doses. Routine 25(OH)D testing is discouraged without an indication such as hypocalcemia. This prevention guidance addresses people without an established indication for testing or treatment. [Endocrine Society, recommendations 6–7, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgae290).
