# Chapter 69 Study: Management of Common Nonpain Symptoms

## Summary

**Symptom burden**
- Multiple nonpain symptoms are common with multimorbidity and may impair function beyond what individual diagnoses predict; screen routinely within geriatric assessment (p1071).
- A nationally representative study found at least **3** symptoms in **28%** of older adults; higher burden accompanied more falls and reduced physical function (p1071).

**Fatigue assessment**
- Ask about onset, daily pattern, aggravating/relieving factors and effects on activity and relationships. Review sleep, medications and depression (p1073).
- Consider anemia, infection, electrolyte problems, malignancy and other systemic causes; new fatigue can reflect MI or heart failure. Let findings guide investigation (p1073).
- Fatigability relates perceived fatigue to a defined activity; this can standardize assessment but is harder to use routinely (p1072–1073).

**Management of fatigue**
- Counseling, functional training and correcting vitamin D deficiency have shown some promise; improving sleep may help (p1073).
- Protein supplementation, thyroid supplementation for subclinical hypothyroidism and testosterone have not shown promise in the studies summarized here (p1073).
- Psychostimulant evidence is mixed. Methylphenidate can cause hypertension and tachycardia; avoid psychostimulants with known cardiovascular disease or arrhythmias (p1073).

**Breathlessness**
- Patient self-report is the most appropriate measure; breathlessness need not correlate with hypoxia, hypercarbia or tachypnea (p1073).
- Treat the cause when known, while using a fan, breathing techniques, posture, relaxation, anxiety management and energy conservation (p1074).
- In hypoxic COPD, long-term oxygen therapy improves quality of life and prolongs survival. Bilevel noninvasive ventilation can also improve quality of life and prolong survival in selected patients with COPD. In patients with advanced cancer and dyspnea who were not hypoxic (median age 65), a cited trial found no dyspnea advantage of oxygen over cannula-delivered air (p1074).
- Noninvasive ventilation may help selected patients, but mask intolerance and anxiety can cause discontinuation (p1074).


### Finding the source of distress and choosing a tolerable intervention

- Ask what activity provokes breathlessness and what the patient now avoids. The Medical Research Council scale ties symptoms to daily function, ranging from symptoms on strenuous exercise to breathlessness with dressing or inability to leave the house. Leisurely walking in clinic may conceal how much patients have restricted activity at home (p1073–1074).
- Work through three mechanisms: increased respiratory effort from airway obstruction or restriction, respiratory muscle weakness, and ventilation–perfusion problems. Heart failure and pulmonary embolism are among the clinical causes to consider. Ask about anxiety and social or existential distress alongside the physical assessment. In one hospice survey, **24%** of patients without a known cardiopulmonary condition still experienced dyspnea (p1073).
- A fan, altered posture, pursed-lip breathing and longer exhalation can provide comfort. The comparison of nasal oxygen with nasal air addresses symptom relief in people without hypoxemia. It does not settle the separate indication for long-term oxygen in hypoxemic COPD. Measure response by how the patient feels and functions when possible (p1073–1074).
- In a trial of **200** predominantly older adults with advanced cancer, noninvasive ventilation reduced dyspnea and morphine requirement compared with oxygen alone, but mask-related anxiety and intolerance caused more discontinuations. The chapter's case illustrates a patient who initially chose BiPAP to gain time with family, then asked for a change. The treatment's value depends on goals, the ability to use the interface and repeated assessment of its burden (p1074–1075).
- For distress persisting despite treatment of the underlying illness, opioid effects vary. Renal impairment increases risk from morphine metabolites. New myoclonus, pain from normally nonpainful touch or increased sensitivity to pain may signal toxicity. Ask whether relief is worth any change in alertness and prevent expected constipation if treatment continues (p1074–1075).

**Drugs & exam traps**
- Opioids may help refractory dyspnea after optimal disease treatment, but evidence is inconsistent. Morphine metabolites can accumulate in renal impairment and cause neurotoxicity (p1074).
- Cognitive adverse effects may outweigh breathing relief for some patients. Choose symptom treatment around individual priorities for function, cognition, comfort and survival (p1074–1075).

## Exam traps

- The comparison of nasal oxygen with nasal air in nonhypoxemic cancer patients cannot answer the survival question in hypoxemic COPD (p1074).
- A lower opioid requirement during noninvasive ventilation does not establish that the mask is tolerable for the individual wearing it (p1074–1075).

## Recall drill

**1. What is the difference between fatigue and fatigability?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fatigue is the patient's symptom. Fatigability relates that symptom to a defined activity, such as a standardized walking task. (p1072–1073)</details>

**2. What should a fatigue history establish before testing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Onset, pattern, activities that worsen or relieve symptoms, effect on life and relationships, sleep, mood and medication exposure. (p1073)</details>

**3. What important acute causes can present as new fatigue?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Myocardial infarction or heart failure can present this way. The rest of the history and examination guide testing for anemia, infection and other systemic causes. (p1073)</details>

**4. What limits enthusiasm for psychostimulants in fatigue?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Trial results are inconsistent. Hypertension and tachycardia are important potential harms, especially with cardiovascular disease or arrhythmia. (p1073)</details>

**5. Whose report best measures breathlessness when it is available?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The patient's. Oxygen saturation, carbon dioxide and respiratory rate may not track how breathless the person feels. (p1073)</details>

**6. How can an activity-based breathlessness scale improve assessment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It connects the symptom to a concrete task, such as walking on a hill or dressing, and helps show the effect on function. (p1074)</details>

**7. What three broad mechanisms can cause breathlessness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Increased breathing effort from obstruction or restriction, weakness of respiratory muscles and impaired ventilation–perfusion matching. (p1073)</details>

**8. What can relieve dyspnea without adding a medication?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Directed airflow, breathing techniques such as pursed-lip breathing, posture changes, relaxation and energy conservation. (p1074)</details>

**9. In which group did nasal oxygen give no more symptom relief than nasal air?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In the cited comparison of patients without hypoxia. This differs from evidence for long-term oxygen in hypoxic COPD. (p1074)</details>

**10. What limits noninvasive ventilation as a comfort treatment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The mask can cause anxiety or intolerance. Reassess relief, burden, ability to cooperate and the patient's goals rather than selecting it solely for the diagnosis. (p1074–1075)</details>

**11. What does refractory dyspnea mean in this discussion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Breathlessness that persists despite appropriate treatment directed at the underlying condition. (p1074)</details>

**12. How strong is the evidence for opioids in refractory dyspnea?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Some studies show improvement, but results are inconsistent and many studies are underpowered. Assess individual benefit and adverse effects. (p1074)</details>

**13. Which findings suggest morphine metabolite accumulation in renal impairment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Myoclonus, hyperalgesia or allodynia may indicate neurotoxicity. (p1074)</details>

**14. Why revisit an opioid trial that has reduced breathlessness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Confusion or reduced alertness may outweigh relief for a patient who values conversation and interaction. Constipation also needs prevention. (p1074–1075)</details>

**15. What can change an end-of-life decision after treatment begins?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A patient may revise priorities once the benefits or burdens are experienced. Revisit the plan and ask which outcomes matter now. (p1075)</details>
