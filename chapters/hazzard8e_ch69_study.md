# Chapter 69 Study: Management of Common Nonpain Symptoms

## Summary

**Symptom burden**
- Multiple nonpain symptoms are common with multimorbidity and may impair function beyond what individual diagnoses predict; screen routinely within geriatric assessment (p1071).
- A nationally representative study found at least **3** symptoms in **28%** of older adults; higher burden accompanied more falls and reduced physical function (p1071).

**Fatigue assessment**
- Ask about onset, daily pattern, aggravating/relieving factors and effects on activity and relationships. Review sleep, medications and depression (p1073).
- Consider anemia, infection, electrolyte problems, malignancy and other systemic causes; new fatigue can reflect MI or heart failure. Let findings guide investigation (p1073).
- Fatigability relates perceived fatigue to a defined activity; this can standardize assessment but is harder to use routinely (p1072–1073).

**Management of fatigue**
- Counseling, functional training and correcting vitamin D deficiency have shown some promise; improving sleep may help (p1073).
- Protein supplementation, thyroid supplementation for subclinical hypothyroidism and testosterone have not shown promise in the studies summarized here (p1073).
- Psychostimulant evidence is mixed. Methylphenidate can cause hypertension and tachycardia; avoid psychostimulants with known cardiovascular disease or arrhythmias (p1073).

> Current practice (not the book): For cancer-related fatigue, first assess reversible causes and consider exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy or mindfulness-based measures as appropriate. ASCO/SIO does not recommend routine psychostimulants or wakefulness agents for this indication. [ASCO/SIO fatigue guideline, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12082589/).

**Breathlessness**
- Patient self-report is the most appropriate measure; breathlessness need not correlate with hypoxia, hypercarbia or tachypnea (p1073).
- Treat the cause when known, while using a fan, breathing techniques, posture, relaxation, anxiety management and energy conservation (p1074).
- In hypoxic COPD, long-term oxygen therapy improves quality of life and prolongs survival. Bilevel noninvasive ventilation can also improve quality of life and prolong survival in selected patients with COPD. In patients with advanced cancer and dyspnea who were not hypoxic (median age 65), a cited trial found no dyspnea advantage of oxygen over cannula-delivered air (p1074).
- Noninvasive ventilation may help selected patients, but mask intolerance and anxiety can cause discontinuation (p1074).


### Finding the source of distress and choosing a tolerable intervention

- Ask what activity provokes breathlessness and what the patient now avoids. The Medical Research Council scale ties symptoms to daily function, ranging from symptoms on strenuous exercise to breathlessness with dressing or inability to leave the house. Leisurely walking in clinic may conceal how much patients have restricted activity at home (p1073–1074).
- Work through three mechanisms: increased respiratory effort from airway obstruction or restriction, respiratory muscle weakness, and ventilation–perfusion problems. Heart failure and pulmonary embolism are among the clinical causes to consider. Ask about anxiety and social or existential distress alongside the physical assessment. In one hospice survey, **24%** of patients without a known cardiopulmonary condition still experienced dyspnea (p1073).
- A fan, altered posture, pursed-lip breathing and longer exhalation can provide comfort. The comparison of nasal oxygen with nasal air addresses symptom relief in people without hypoxemia. It does not settle the separate indication for long-term oxygen in hypoxemic COPD. Measure response by how the patient feels and functions when possible (p1073–1074).
- In a trial of **200** predominantly older adults with advanced cancer, noninvasive ventilation reduced dyspnea and morphine requirement compared with oxygen alone, but mask-related anxiety and intolerance caused more discontinuations. The chapter's case illustrates a patient who initially chose BiPAP to gain time with family, then asked for a change. The treatment's value depends on goals, the ability to use the interface and repeated assessment of its burden (p1074–1075).
- For distress persisting despite treatment of the underlying illness, opioid effects vary. New myoclonus, pain from normally nonpainful touch or increased sensitivity to pain may signal toxicity. Ask whether relief is worth any change in alertness and prevent expected constipation if treatment continues (p1074–1075).

**Drugs & exam traps**
- Opioids may help refractory dyspnea after optimal disease treatment, but evidence is inconsistent. Morphine metabolites can accumulate in renal impairment and cause neurotoxicity (p1074).

> Current practice (not the book): In BEAMS, regular low-dose extended-release morphine did not reduce worst breathlessness more than placebo at one week in adults with COPD and severe chronic breathlessness. Distinguish routine chronic treatment from symptom relief in individualized end-of-life care. [BEAMS randomized trial, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36413230/).
- Cognitive adverse effects may outweigh breathing relief for some patients. Choose symptom treatment around individual priorities for function, cognition, comfort and survival (p1074–1075).

### Starting with the symptom that limits the person's day

A patient with heart failure, chronic kidney disease and arthritis reports having stopped going to lunch with friends. “Is the heart failure worse?” is only one of the questions this raises. Find out whether the obstacle is breathlessness on the way, exhaustion after an outing, difficulty sleeping, loss of appetite, pain when getting into the car or the effort of arranging help. Several can occur together. The symptom that disrupts the patient's life may attract little attention in any one disease clinic. Comprehensive geriatric assessment gives the team a place to ask about these symptoms routinely and to agree on which one to address first (p1071–1072).

Three different measurements illustrate what “burden” can mean. The national survey on p1071 counted **three or more symptoms in 28%**. A population sample using ten items found a mean of **3.7 symptoms**, with about a third reporting five or more. In a housecalls program, **43% of 318** adults reported severe burden from at least one symptom. The first two estimates count symptoms; the third asks about severity in a selected care setting. A patient reporting one very distressing symptom may need more immediate attention than someone reporting several mild ones. Ask about intensity, interference and the patient's priorities as well as the count (p1071).

Timing matters, particularly in advanced illness. In a study of **754** older adults in their last year of life, about **20%** had at least one restricting symptom in a given month during the first half of that year. The proportion rose from **27%** around five months before death to **57%** in the final month. These are longitudinal observations about a population already known to be in its last year of life. They support revisiting symptom questions as function changes. They cannot date the end of life for a patient who newly reports fatigue or dyspnea (p1071).

### Reading the pattern across diagnoses

Figure 69-1 reports the three most frequent symptoms in each of several illnesses. In the cancer column, pain leads at **84%**, followed by fatigue at **69%** and anorexia at **66%**. In heart failure, breathlessness and fatigue are both shown at **88%**, followed by pain at **75%**. COPD shares breathlessness (**97%**) and fatigue (**88%**) with heart failure, but dry mouth (**85%**) is also prominent. Dry mouth can be particularly easy to overlook in a visit devoted to spirometry or rescue medication use. The figure provides questions worth asking, not a score that assigns a symptom to one diagnosis (p1072).

The other columns challenge common assumptions. In chronic kidney disease, fatigue (**88%**), sleep disturbance (**82%**) and anorexia (**55%**) feature prominently. With dementia, breathlessness (**46%**) is followed by constipation (**40%**) and pain (**39%**). In AIDS, fatigue (**73%**), pain (**69%**) and dry mouth (**54%**) appear together. These figures describe the populations from which they were drawn. In particular, the dementia column is a reason to ask about breathlessness or its functional consequences even when a person has difficulty giving a spontaneous account (p1072).

### Choosing the assessment instrument

Match the form to the clinical decision. A brief inventory can uncover neglected symptoms at the initial assessment. A longer multidimensional instrument can distinguish severity from distress when treatment decisions depend on that distinction. The ten-item Brief Symptom Inventory described here was developed from a community sample of older adults. Its items include shortness of breath, fatigue, balance problems or dizziness, weakness, pain, stiffness, constipation, poor appetite, anxiety and anhedonia. A single-disease instrument can miss symptoms due to another illness or to its treatment. Conversely, a long inventory is of little value if there is no time to discuss the patient's main concern (p1071–1072).

The Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale covers **32 symptoms** and asks about features including occurrence, severity and distress. The Edmonton scale uses **nine** common symptoms rated from **0 to 10**. Both have been used beyond cancer, although much symptom-assessment research originated in oncology. Ask what period the person considered in giving the answer. A brief period can describe a difficult day accurately and still miss how the symptom fluctuates over weeks. When repeating an assessment, keep the measure, time window and activity context recognizable so that a change in score has an interpretable meaning (p1072).

### Fatigue, fatigability and the activity that is no longer attempted

Ask for a description of tiredness in the patient's own terms. Does it begin on waking or after effort? Does rest restore energy? What takes longer, what has been abandoned, and how does one poor night affect the following day? A person who has stopped climbing stairs might report less fatigue simply because they no longer do the task that provoked it. Fatigability relates the perceived effort or tiredness to a standard activity, for example after a **5-minute** treadmill task. This improves comparability but may be impractical for someone with severe disability. The two approaches address different parts of the clinical problem (p1072–1073).

Reported prevalence of generalized fatigue ranged from **5% to almost 70%** across studies, depending on the measure, population, assessment time and cutoff. A simple question about feeling tired most of the time identified an approximately one-and-a-half to nearly twofold higher ten-year mortality risk in a primary care study of **492** older people. It did not diagnose the cause of fatigue. The nine-item Brief Fatigue Inventory asks about severity and interference with daily activity in the preceding **24 hours**. A response can guide a follow-up question about function even when its prognostic meaning for an individual is uncertain (p1072).

Figure 69-2 places biological, psychological and social contributors together. A new medicine and a recent infection may be important even when the patient also describes grief and isolation. Ask about sleep, mood, usual activity, recent losses, adverse effects and how the household is coping. Look for evidence of reversible causes such as anemia, infection, metabolic disturbance, inflammatory disease or occult malignancy. A new change in an older adult can accompany myocardial infarction or heart failure despite the absence of the symptom expected in a younger patient. The time course, examination and accompanying features guide the investigation; an extensive routine panel is not the chapter's starting point (p1072–1073).

### Interpreting a fatigue-treatment study

Distinguish treating a cause from giving a drug for the sensation. Vitamin D showed some promise in people with deficiency; this does not establish a role for indiscriminate supplementation. The chapter reports little support for protein supplements as a fatigue intervention, thyroid replacement for fatigue in subclinical hypothyroidism, or testosterone in the men studied. Counseling, functional training and attention to sleep may help selected people. If depression is contributing, consider the adverse-effect profile of treatment, particularly strong anticholinergic effects. A treatment that reduces fatigue but worsens confusion or ability to walk may fail the patient's broader goal (p1073).

Methylphenidate illustrates how study design changes the inference. A prostate-cancer study in a predominantly older group reported improvement in fatigue severity, but some participants stopped because of increased blood pressure or tachycardia. In another study, **43 participants** alternated between methylphenidate and placebo over **9 days** without better fatigue on drug days. The book discusses methylphenidate or modafinil only in selected palliative settings and warns against psychostimulants in older adults with known cardiovascular disease or arrhythmias. It gives no general geriatric starting dose here. A trial of donepezil did not confirm earlier anecdotal benefit for opioid-associated fatigue (p1073).

### Breathlessness as a symptom, a mechanism and a functional limitation

Let the patient describe the breathing sensation and the task that brings it on. The oxygen saturation helps answer a physiologic question; it cannot quantify how breathless a person feels. In the community studies summarized, prevalence in older adults ranged from **17% to 62%** depending on the population and threshold used. These findings support asking directly, even outside COPD and heart failure. Anxiety, financial stress, loss of valued roles and the response of a frightened caregiver can amplify distress and are reasonable targets for care alongside disease treatment (p1073).

Work backward from the likely mechanism. Airway obstruction or a restrictive load makes the work of breathing greater. Respiratory muscle weakness can make ventilation difficult in neuromuscular disease. Pulmonary embolism or heart failure may disturb the matching of ventilation and perfusion. This distinction explains a distractor in May 2024 Q77: weakness is the relevant pattern in the neurologic examples in the chapter. A patient with COPD can also have anemia, congestion, an effusion or anxiety. A diagnosis on the problem list should not preclude asking what has changed since the person last managed the same walk (p1073–1074).

Table 69-1 grades the activity associated with breathlessness. At **grade 1**, it occurs with strenuous exertion; at **grade 2**, with hurrying on the level or going up a slight incline. **Grade 3** includes walking slower than most people on level ground or stopping after about a mile or roughly 15 minutes at one's own pace. At **grade 4**, the person stops after about 100 yards or a few minutes on the level. At **grade 5**, breathlessness prevents leaving the home or occurs while undressing. Ask about actual activity before assigning a grade. A sedentary patient who reports few episodes may be protecting against symptoms by avoiding exertion (p1074).

### What oxygen, airflow and ventilation trials actually tested

May 2024 Q77 asks about dyspnea near the end of life. In the comparison among nonhypoxemic patients, nasal air relieved dyspnea as effectively as nasal oxygen. That finding concerns perceived symptom relief in that population. It cannot be used to deny oxygen for a different indication: the chapter describes improved quality of life and longer survival with long-term oxygen in hypoxemic COPD. Likewise, a benefit in hypoxemic COPD cannot demonstrate an additional effect of oxygen over moving air in every patient with advanced cancer. The grade of breathlessness, saturation and underlying cause each contribute different information (p1073–1074).

Offer low-burden approaches while investigating and treating the cause. A fan, pursed-lip breathing, prolonged exhalation or a change of posture can be tried and the patient's response observed. Relaxation, guided imagery, music, distraction or pacing an activity may help. For a person who values talking through a family meal, the practical question is whether pacing and positioning make the meal possible. None of these measures requires the clinician to conclude that breathlessness is primarily psychological, and their use need not wait until a drug has failed (p1073–1074).

In the advanced-cancer trial of **200** predominantly older patients, mean age **71**, noninvasive ventilation provided greater dyspnea relief and reduced morphine requirements compared with oxygen alone. More patients stopped mask ventilation because of anxiety or intolerance. September 2024 Q100 changes the clinical setting: the patient with advanced COPD is deeply obtunded and the goal is end-of-life comfort. The keyed unsuitable option is CPAP. The trial's result cannot simply be transferred to someone unable to cooperate or indicate whether the interface is intolerable. Distinguish a time-limited trial in a willing person from continuing a burdensome mask in this vignette (p1074–1075).

### Diseases, medicines and the cost of symptom relief

Address demonstrable obstruction or congestion with disease-directed care, such as a beta-agonist for obstructive pulmonary disease or a diuretic for heart failure. Refractory dyspnea is breathlessness at rest or with minimal activity that persists despite appropriate treatment of the underlying chronic illness. The book finds possible opioid benefit in some patients with advanced illness but notes inconsistent results and underpowered studies. It does not identify oxycodone as the universal drug of choice, and this chapter gives no single opioid dosing schedule applicable to multimorbid older people. Document the activity or sensation the patient wants improved and reassess it during treatment (p1074).

Morphine has active metabolites, and renal impairment increases the risk of accumulation and neurotoxicity. Myoclonus, exaggerated pain or pain from usually innocuous touch after medication escalation warrant reconsideration of the drug. Reduced alertness can itself be unacceptable to someone who wants to converse. Anticipate constipation when prescribing an opioid and plan a bowel regimen. The broader issue is the prescribing cascade: a drug used for one distressing symptom can produce another that is mistakenly treated as a new unrelated illness. The chapter also notes that NSAIDs can aggravate hypertension or heart failure. Review the entire regimen when a new symptom appears (p1074–1075).

### A preference-sensitive decision can change overnight

Before asking about a particular device or medicine, ask what the patient is trying to preserve. More time, mental clarity, physical independence, relief of breathlessness, remaining at home and avoiding another admission may carry different weights for different people. Some want to choose the treatment; others want the clinician to recommend it after hearing their priorities. In both situations the patient's account is necessary. Describe the expected benefit, its uncertainty, the burdens that matter to this individual and what would prompt changing course. Evidence is especially limited for older adults with several serious noncancer illnesses (p1075).

The case at the end of the chapter makes the time dimension concrete. A man with advanced COPD wanted enough time to say goodbye to family. He accepted BiPAP while also receiving treatment for a reversible contributor, and declined intubation and ICU transfer. The mask initially brought rapid relief. After seeing his family he found it suffocating and asked for it to be removed. His original consent and later refusal were consistent with the same goal. Reassess whether treatment still fits at the next encounter; the first favorable response need not settle the decision for the rest of the admission (p1075).
The follow-up question can be concrete. Ask whether the person can now finish getting dressed, speak for as long as they wish, or sleep without being woken by breathlessness. Ask separately about drowsiness, fear and the work required from the caregiver. Improvement in one outcome can coexist with deterioration in another. Those answers supply the information needed to decide whether to continue, modify or withdraw the intervention (p1074–1075).

## Exam traps

- Distinguish the number of symptoms reported from the severity and functional impact of the symptom the patient most wants treated (p1071–1072).
- Tiredness after a defined task and general fatigue can move in different directions if the patient has cut back their activity (p1072–1073).
- Muscle weakness is one mechanism of neurologic breathlessness; a ventilation-perfusion abnormality is a different mechanism (p1073).
- A mask can reduce measured dyspnea or morphine requirements in a trial while being unacceptable to the individual patient (p1074–1075).
- When myoclonus or worsening pain appears during morphine therapy in renal impairment, consider metabolite toxicity before increasing analgesia (p1074).
- The comparison of nasal oxygen with nasal air in nonhypoxemic cancer patients cannot answer the survival question in hypoxemic COPD (p1074).

## Recall drill

**1. What is the difference between fatigue and fatigability?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fatigue is the patient's symptom. Fatigability relates that symptom to a defined activity, such as a standardized walking task. (p1072–1073)</details>

**2. What should a fatigue history establish before testing?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Onset, pattern, activities that worsen or relieve symptoms, effect on life and relationships, sleep, mood and medication exposure. (p1073)</details>

**3. What important acute causes can present as new fatigue?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Myocardial infarction or heart failure can present this way. The rest of the history and examination guide testing for anemia, infection and other systemic causes. (p1073)</details>

**4. What limits enthusiasm for psychostimulants in fatigue?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Trial results are inconsistent. Hypertension and tachycardia are important potential harms, especially with cardiovascular disease or arrhythmia. (p1073)</details>

**5. Whose report best measures breathlessness when it is available?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The patient's. Oxygen saturation, carbon dioxide and respiratory rate may not track how breathless the person feels. (p1073)</details>

**6. How can an activity-based breathlessness scale improve assessment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It connects the symptom to a concrete task, such as walking on a hill or dressing, and helps show the effect on function. (p1074)</details>

**7. What three broad mechanisms can cause breathlessness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Increased breathing effort from obstruction or restriction, weakness of respiratory muscles and impaired ventilation–perfusion matching. (p1073)</details>

**8. What can relieve dyspnea without adding a medication?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Directed airflow, breathing techniques such as pursed-lip breathing, posture changes, relaxation and energy conservation. (p1074)</details>

**9. In which group did nasal oxygen give no more symptom relief than nasal air?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>In the comparison of patients without hypoxia. This differs from evidence for long-term oxygen in hypoxic COPD. (p1074)</details>

**10. What limits noninvasive ventilation as a comfort treatment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The mask can cause anxiety or intolerance. Reassess relief, burden, ability to cooperate and the patient's goals rather than selecting it solely for the diagnosis. (p1074–1075)</details>

**11. What does refractory dyspnea mean in this discussion?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Breathlessness that persists despite appropriate treatment directed at the underlying condition. (p1074)</details>

**12. How strong is the evidence for opioids in refractory dyspnea?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Some studies show improvement, but results are inconsistent and many studies are underpowered. Assess individual benefit and adverse effects. (p1074)</details>

**13. Which findings suggest morphine metabolite accumulation in renal impairment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Myoclonus, hyperalgesia or allodynia may indicate neurotoxicity. (p1074)</details>

**14. Why revisit an opioid trial that has reduced breathlessness?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Confusion or reduced alertness may outweigh relief for a patient who values conversation and interaction. Constipation also needs prevention. (p1074–1075)</details>

**15. What can change an end-of-life decision after treatment begins?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A patient may revise priorities once the benefits or burdens are experienced. Revisit the plan and ask which outcomes matter now. (p1075)</details>
