# Frailty

Frailty describes vulnerability to a stressor. Begin by distinguishing a physical phenotype from accumulated deficits and from dependence in everyday activities.

## Defining frailty

Frailty (consensus definition): medical syndrome, multiple causes, ↓ strength/endurance and ↓ physiologic function → ↑ vulnerability to dependency/death; should not overlap with disability, and not exhaustively defined by sarcopenia or multimorbidity; screening endorsed for all individuals **70 and older** (<a href="?chapter=42#p617">p617</a>)

## Measuring the physical phenotype

### Counting Fried criteria at the bedside

Start with what changed over the last year. Ask whether weight loss was unintentional, whether ordinary tasks feel exhausting and how much the patient usually moves. Observe gait speed over a measured distance and measure handgrip strength. A person who stays on the couch most of the day may meet the low-activity criterion even with stable chronic diseases. A cognitive score, ejection fraction or number of admissions cannot substitute for that history (<a href="?chapter=42#p619">pp. 619</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p620">620</a>).

**Count domains once.** Three or more of the five define frailty. Slowness, weak grip, low activity and exhaustion already satisfy four domains. With the Fried phenotype, very slow walking, exhaustion and a 7-kg loss support three if the loss was unintentional. Ask about the intention rather than inferring it from the scale reading. The printed narrative on <a href="?chapter=42#p619">p619</a> calls two criteria prefrail and one a risk factor, while <a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a> uses one or two as the intermediate category. Both agree that three define frailty (<a href="?chapter=42#p619">pp. 619</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p621">621</a>).

**Physical Frailty Phenotype** (Fried/Cardiovascular Health Study) — 5 criteria: unintentional weight loss, weakness, exhaustion, slowness, low activity (<a href="?chapter=42#p619">p619</a>)

**≥3 of 5** criteria = frail; **2 of 5** = prefrail; 1 alone = risk factor only, not frailty itself (<a href="?chapter=42#p619">p619</a>) — note <a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a> instead scores intermediate/prefrail as **1–2** criteria (<a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>)

**A fall is an event. Slowness is a measurement.** For the original Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) phenotype, time the 15-foot walk and interpret it by sex and height. A cane, fear of falling, two falls or an unsteady gait may explain reduced activity, but none supplies the timed result. Likewise, BMI helps interpret handgrip strength. It cannot replace a grip measurement or a history of weight change (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-2">Table 42-2</a>).

Weight loss: **>10 lb** lost unintentionally in the prior year (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>)

Weakness: grip strength lowest 20% (stratified by gender/BMI) (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>)

Slowness: **15-ft** walk time, slowest 20% (by gender/height) (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>)

Low activity: lowest 20% kcal/wk; cutoffs **<383 kcal/wk in men** and **<270 kcal/wk in women** (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>)

The Fried phenotype omits cognition. Its weight-loss criterion can miss frailty in sarcopenic obesity, and its thresholds came from the Cardiovascular Health Study (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>).

**When a vignette omits the measurements**, ask which measurements are still missing. A history alone may leave more than one plausible classification. The woman’s 2-kg loss is below the original absolute threshold of more than 10 lb, and difficulty cleaning does not measure grip. The man’s walking difficulty, trouble lifting groceries and exhaustion after transfers suggest three domains that warrant confirmation. Neither history documents three CHS measurements (<a href="?chapter=42#p619">pp. 619</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p620">620</a>).

## Deficits, function and clinical scales

### Grading dependence with the Clinical Frailty Scale

Ask what the patient usually manages alone. Someone who depends on others for shopping, meals and housework has instrumental limitations. When the person also needs help bathing or dressing, **moderate frailty (CFS 6)** fits. Walking independently with a rollator and living alone do not undo the need for personal assistance. Use baseline dependence to grade the centenarian (<a href="?chapter=8#p124">p124</a>, <a href="?chapter=8#book-figure-8-2">Figure 8-2</a> and <a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a>).

A wheelchair user who also needs complete help with personal care can meet grade **7**. The original CFS describes severe frailty at that level as complete dependence for personal care, or terminal illness. A wheelchair user may transfer, dress and bathe independently. Ask who performs these tasks before assigning a grade clinically. Chapter 42 describes the original seven-category instrument. Chapter 8 reproduces the later nine-point instrument, which has a separate category for terminal illness (<a href="?chapter=8#p124">pp. 124</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p621">621</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p623">623</a>).

**<a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a>** also spells out the **original CFS’s seven categories** (<a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>): 1 = very fit; 2 = well (no active disease); 3 = well, with treated comorbid disease; 4 = apparently vulnerable (not frankly dependent, "slowed up"); 5 = mildly frail (IADL help only); 6 = moderately frail (help with IADLs + ADLs); 7 = severely frail (ADL-dependent) or terminally ill. The current nine-point CFS scores terminal illness as 9.

### Choosing an instrument before TAVI

**Use the information the tool requires.** A comprehensive geriatric assessment records diagnoses, signs, symptoms and functional limitations. An FI constructed from suitable data divides the number of deficits present by the number considered. Ten deficits among forty assessed gives 0.25. Different combinations can produce the same fraction. A shorter FI can be generated from a sufficiently complete clinical record (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">pp. 620</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p623">623</a>).

**Deficit Accumulation / Frailty Index (FI)** (Rockwood): unweighted ratio of deficits present ÷ total considered, built from a series of **70** measurements ("deficits") (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">p620</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p623">p623</a>); rises nonlinearly with age (Gompertz-like), higher in women, ceiling **FI ~0.7** (<a href="?chapter=42#p623">p623</a>)

The original Fried assessment needs timed walking and measured grip. The FRAIL screen can be administered by asking about fatigue, climbing stairs, walking a block, illnesses and weight loss. The CFS draws on clinical judgment about baseline function. The Edmonton scale organizes problems across nine domains so a clinician can see which aspects of vulnerability need attention. These tools answer different practical questions even when each identifies a patient at greater risk (<a href="?chapter=42#p621">pp. 621</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p623">623</a>).

**Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)**: the **original seven-category** version used clinical judgment and predicted institutionalization and death similarly to the FI (<a href="?chapter=42#p623">p623</a>). The [current CFS has nine levels](https://www.dal.ca/sites/gmr/our-tools/clinical-frailty-scale.html).

**Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS)**: **9 domains** — cognition, general health, function, social support, meds, nutrition, mood, continence, performance (<a href="?chapter=42#p623">p623</a>)

**<a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a>** (<a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>) catalogs further named frailty instruments beyond FPP/FI/CFS/EFS — Gill Frailty Measure, Brief Frailty Instrument, Vulnerable Elders Survey (VES-13), FRAIL Scale (<a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p622">622</a>)

**VES-13**: frail if score **≥3** (range 0–10). **FRAIL Scale**: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, **more than 5 illnesses**, and **more than 5% weight loss**. Score **0** = no frailty, **1–2** = intermediate, **≥3** = frailty (<a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p622">p622</a>).

> **Current practice: FRAIL scale thresholds.** the validation study’s Methods scored **5 or more of 11 illnesses** and **at least 5% weight loss over 12 months**. Its appendix uses **>5%** for weight loss. [Morley et al., 2012, Methods and appendix](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4515112/).

<a href="?chapter=42#book-table-42-3">Table 42-3</a> summarizes general frailty instruments, including the VES-13 and FRAIL questionnaire (<a href="?chapter=42#p622">p622</a>). The same table describes the FI on <a href="?chapter=42#p621">p621</a>, and <a href="?chapter=42#p623">p623</a> explains its calculation. The underlying choice concerns which information can be obtained from an assessment and which measurements must be made directly.

## Biological reserve and change over time

### Energetics and the falling resting metabolic rate

<a href="?chapter=42#book-figure-42-2">Figure 42-2</a> links muscle loss to reduced resting metabolic rate and exercise capacity. Weakness and slower walking limit activity. Undernutrition and low energy expenditure can perpetuate the cycle. The direction depends on the stage: the energy cost of compensating and repairing damage may initially raise resting metabolism. Later, energy production becomes limiting, and resting metabolic rate falls despite ongoing stress (<a href="?chapter=42#p617">pp. 617</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p619">619</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p625">625</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p626">626</a>).

The clinical features form the outer layer of the model. The next layer includes candidate biomarkers of altered signaling, body composition, energy balance and neural function. The inner layer comprises proposed cellular mechanisms of aging. Chronic mild inflammation can accompany reserve loss even without overt disease. A clinician can use physical function to estimate risk without first identifying which cellular process caused it (<a href="?chapter=42#p617">pp. 617</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p618">618</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p623">623</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p625">625</a>).

Community-dwelling age ≥65: average prevalence **11%** (reported range **4.0–59%** across definitions/settings) (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

Estimated frailty prevalence with broader definitions versus the Fried phenotype: **14% vs 10%** (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

Hospitalized inpatients: **27–80%**; institutionalized: **30–70%** (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

Exhaustion is typically the first CHS criterion to appear (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

Nearly **60%** of adults >70 transition between frailty states at least once over **4.5 years**; progression to a worse state is more common than recovery, and frail→nonfrail reversal is rare (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

These estimates come from particular populations and instruments, so an estimate for a ward should not be applied directly to an independent person in the community. Frailty is more prevalent at both low and very high BMI. Muscle weakness and slow gait may be present despite stable weight in sarcopenic obesity. A shift from robust to intermediate function is clinically meaningful because state transitions can precede dependence (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">pp. 620</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p626">626</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p627">627</a>).

### Pathophysiology ("onion" model, <a href="?chapter=42#book-figure-42-1">Fig 42-1</a>)

Concentric layers: outer = clinical presentation (multimorbidity, physical/cognitive impairment); middle = biomarker layer (inflammation, hormones, body composition, energetics); inner = core biological hallmarks of aging (<a href="?chapter=42#p617">p617</a>)

"Inflammaging": chronic elevation of IL-6 and TNF-α, present even in disease-free older adults (<a href="?chapter=42#p624">p624</a>); accumulating senescent cells spilling SASP molecules may account, at least in part, for the high circulating inflammatory markers (<a href="?chapter=42#p625">p625</a>)

Multi-hormonal decline: DHEAS, testosterone, estrogens, GH/IGF-1, vitamin D ↓, with relative preservation of cortisol/thyroid hormones (<a href="?chapter=42#p625">p625</a>)

Muscle energy demand rises as much as **100-fold** from rest to contraction — mitochondrial decline + reduced satellite-cell repair capacity impair this reserve (<a href="?chapter=42#p625">p625</a>)

RMR = **60–70%** of total daily energy expenditure; initially rises (compensation), later falls as frailty develops — hypothesized that this RMR shift marks failure of resilience and accelerated, irreversible deterioration (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>)

Reduced muscle quality helps explain why strength can fall faster than lean mass. Motor neuron loss, imperfect reinnervation, fat infiltration, impaired mitochondria and diminished repair capacity can all contribute. Peripheral motor units become fewer and larger, and sensory conduction and reflexes slow. Central changes can also compromise executive function, processing speed and multitasking. These changes affect the ability to perform daily activities even when an individual organ test appears reassuring (<a href="?chapter=42#p625">pp. 625</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p626">626</a>).

In the proposed geroscience framework, frailty, multimorbidity and geriatric syndromes can share aging-related mechanisms. Higher IL-6 over time was associated with faster accumulation of chronic diseases. In people who already have obesity, unintentional loss of weight can accompany worsening health. Researchers are studying whether molecular profiles and measured function can identify groups who respond differently to treatments. No biomarker panel presented in this chapter replaces clinical assessment (<a href="?chapter=42#p629">pp. 629</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p630">630</a>).

## Cognition, chronic illness and prognosis

### When cognition is part of frailty

An older adult can meet the physical phenotype with normal cognition. For **cognitive frailty**, establish physical frailty and mild cognitive impairment, operationalized here as CDR 0.5, and exclude Alzheimer disease and other dementias. A screening score alone cannot settle that differential. The suggestion that physical frailty precede cognitive impairment aims to distinguish physically driven cognitive decline. It is presented separately from the three operational criteria (<a href="?chapter=42#p627">p627</a>).

**Cognitive frailty**: physical frailty + mild cognitive impairment (**CDR 0.5**), with AD/dementia excluded; it has been suggested that physical frailty should precede the cognitive impairment, to separate physically driven cognitive decline (<a href="?chapter=42#p627">p627</a>)

Declining mobility and cognition together identify a group at particularly high risk. Frail people with a comparable burden of brain pathology have a higher risk of dementia, although the mechanism remains uncertain. Evaluate new confusion, depression and established dementia on their own merits (<a href="?chapter=42#p620">pp. 620</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p626">626</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p627">627</a>).

### Prognosis in the patient with chronic disease

Functional assessment adds information that a diagnosis list misses. A patient who has recovered from pneumonia on paper may still need help transferring because reserve across systems was limited before admission. Reduced reserve increases susceptibility to disability, hospital care and death even without a new acute illness. The consensus described in the chapter supported screening from age 70. The authors emphasize the limited intervention evidence available at the time and distinguish observational associations with activity or diet from proof that an established syndrome can be reversed (<a href="?chapter=42#p616">pp. 616</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p619">619</a>, <a href="?chapter=42#p626">626</a>).

> **Current practice: Exercise prescription for frailty.** Offer a multicomponent physical activity programme that includes progressive resistance exercise to older adults with physical frailty. Adapt balance, gait and strength work to functional ability and fall risk. [ICFSR clinical practice guideline, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6800406/).

**Before surgery**, consider postoperative pneumonia, delirium, longer stay and the possibility of discharge to institutional care. **Before dialysis**, explain that frailty predicts hospitalization and mortality among new dialysis patients. Frail patients started at a higher estimated GFR on average. The observations do not show that an earlier start improves function or survival. CKD and other illnesses can contribute to frailty, while frailty can also develop independently (<a href="?chapter=42#p627">pp. 627</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p628">628</a>).

**Before a cardiac procedure**, consider reserve when discussing rehabilitation and the likely care needs after discharge. In diabetes, CHS participants with frailty had higher glucose and insulin both at baseline and with oral glucose loading. The direction of causality remains uncertain. In cancer, assess function, cognition, social support and reserve to estimate tolerance of burdensome treatment. In transplantation, add the patient’s goals for independence and quality of life to organ-specific estimates of risk (<a href="?chapter=42#p627">pp. 627</a>–<a href="?chapter=42#p629">629</a>).

### Frailty in specific disease contexts

Surgery: frailty predicts postop pneumonia, delirium, UTI, longer stay, nursing-home discharge, and higher mortality; a multidimensional frailty score was more useful than conventional methods for predicting surgical outcomes (<a href="?chapter=42#p627">p627</a>)

CKD: CHS participants with CKD had **2-fold** risk of frailty + disability; frail patients start dialysis earlier (higher eGFR) with no proven survival/functional benefit (<a href="?chapter=42#p628">p628</a>)

CVD: frailty **~3×** more prevalent with CVD; CAD in women → de novo frailty **12% vs 5%** over 6 yr; frail elders develop CAD events at **3.6% vs 2.8%/yr** (<a href="?chapter=42#p628">p628</a>)

HIV: frailty prevalence **5–20%** in younger HIV+ patients; risk ↑ with **CD4 <350 cells/mm³**, further risk factors: **CD4 <100 cells/mm³**, clinical AIDS, previous opportunistic illnesses (<a href="?chapter=42#p628">p628</a>)

Transplant: MELD predicts waitlist mortality but predicts posttransplant outcomes only at scores **above 35**; Fried-criteria frailty tracked quality of life better than MELD severity in end-stage liver disease (<a href="?chapter=42#p629">p629</a>)

## Key numbers

- Frailty prevalence was **16%** at ages **80–84** and **26%** at age **85 or older** (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>).

- Frailty was more common in women than men with both definitions. Prevalence was **9.6% versus 5.2%** with the Fried phenotype and **39% versus 37%** with the frailty index (<a href="?chapter=42#p626">p626</a>).

<details class="study-self-check" data-check-key="42s:recall-14"><summary>What CD4+ T-cell count thresholds are linked to HIV-related frailty risk?</summary>

CD4 <350 cells/mm³ (a marker of advanced HIV disease) is independently associated with a frailty-related phenotype; CD4 <100 cells/mm³ is a further risk factor, as are clinical AIDS and previous opportunistic illnesses (<a href="?chapter=42#p628">p628</a>).
