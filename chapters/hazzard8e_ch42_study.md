## Counting Fried criteria at the bedside

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7269ecbc837c5195db734120 mcq-441ff6c6aea9f3c3b5d0348a mcq-7444d692ff82667f2d542ca2 mcq-e1593b4c91f0875d66348128 mcq-5243aa556e0d2320e60b280e mcq-0e6d84b15f6cc59dec2289b4" data-practice-ids="mcq-30c82a2cc97c7930197d674a mcq-d6e4989704d2ecdd8b9bcf09 mcq-b02034474131921b67085132 mcq-80f80447923ccdae3ce03cfa mcq-bc24e7f8e5a730eac8743b21 mcq-c49291f4ec6d557db5a215f2 mcq-07d656d350160581b0bc7267" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Start with what changed over the last year. Ask whether weight loss was unintentional, whether ordinary tasks feel exhausting and how much the patient usually moves. Observe gait speed over a measured distance and measure handgrip strength. A person who stays on the couch most of the day may meet the low-activity criterion even with stable chronic diseases. A cognitive score, ejection fraction or number of admissions cannot substitute for that history (pp. 619–620).

**Count domains once.** Three or more of the five define frailty. The woman with slowness, weak grip, low activity and exhaustion in June 2026 Q45 already has four. For June 2025 Q93, very slow walking, exhaustion and a 7-kg loss support three if the loss was unintentional. Ask about the intention rather than inferring it from the scale reading. The printed narrative on p619 calls two criteria prefrail and one a risk factor, while Table 42-3 uses one or two as the intermediate category. Both agree that three define frailty (pp. 619–621).

**A fall is an event. Slowness is a measurement.** For the original Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS) phenotype, time the 15-foot walk and interpret it by sex and height. A cane, fear of falling, two falls or an unsteady gait may explain reduced activity, but none supplies the timed result. Likewise, BMI helps interpret handgrip strength. It cannot replace a grip measurement or a history of weight change (p620, Table 42-2).

**When a vignette omits the measurements**, distinguish an exam choice from a completed assessment. September 2024 Q3 accepted two histories. The woman’s 2-kg loss is below the original absolute threshold of more than 10 lb, and difficulty cleaning does not measure grip. The man’s walking difficulty, trouble lifting groceries and exhaustion after transfers suggest three domains that warrant confirmation. Neither history documents three CHS measurements (pp. 619–620).

## Grading dependence with the Clinical Frailty Scale

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-2f9b0a4a271f750ea9bfb769 mcq-9f480bcc831187d54db7aa64" data-practice-ids="mcq-bd0f0052aa882a252622a349" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Ask what the patient usually manages alone. Someone who depends on others for shopping, meals and housework has instrumental limitations. When the person also needs help bathing or dressing, **moderate frailty (CFS 6)** fits. Walking independently with a rollator and living alone do not undo the need for personal assistance. This pattern determines the grade for the centenarian in June 2026 Q38 (p124, Figure 8-2 and p621, Table 42-3).

For the wheelchair user in September 2024 Q21, the exam credits **7**. The original CFS describes severe frailty at that level as complete dependence for personal care, or terminal illness. A wheelchair user may transfer, dress and bathe independently. Ask who performs these tasks before assigning a grade clinically. Chapter 42 describes the original seven-category instrument. Chapter 8 reproduces the later nine-point instrument, which has a separate category for terminal illness (pp. 124, 621, 623).

## Choosing an instrument before TAVI

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-427dd5d1617e80e5e1500067" data-practice-ids="mcq-0455ddd0fbd88a1d8794fc36 mcq-6eed0d03806d69f3a013331f mcq-a06f2f2499492064230c8ca0" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Use the information the tool requires.** A comprehensive geriatric assessment records diagnoses, signs, symptoms and functional limitations. An FI constructed from suitable data divides the number of deficits present by the number considered. Ten deficits among forty assessed gives 0.25. Different combinations can produce the same fraction. A shorter FI can be generated from a sufficiently complete clinical record (pp. 620–623).

The original Fried assessment needs timed walking and measured grip. The FRAIL screen can be administered by asking about fatigue, climbing stairs, walking a block, illnesses and weight loss. The CFS draws on clinical judgment about baseline function. The Edmonton scale organizes problems across nine domains so a clinician can see which aspects of vulnerability need attention. These tools answer different practical questions even when each identifies a patient at greater risk (pp. 621–623).

The June 2025 TAVI question cites p622, where Table 42-3 compares the questionnaire-based tools. The same table describes the FI on p621, and p623 explains its calculation. The underlying choice concerns which information can be obtained from an assessment and which measurements must be made directly.

## When cognition is part of frailty

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-d50163430af0b0650aca37fe" data-practice-ids="mcq-0fdff73e3a9bc14b8b0602a6" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

An older adult can meet the physical phenotype with normal cognition. For **cognitive frailty**, establish physical frailty and mild cognitive impairment, operationalized here as CDR 0.5, and exclude Alzheimer disease and other dementias. A screening score alone cannot settle that differential. The suggestion that physical frailty precede cognitive impairment aims to distinguish physically driven cognitive decline. It is presented separately from the three operational criteria (p627).

Declining mobility and cognition together identify a group at particularly high risk. Frail people with a comparable burden of brain pathology have a higher risk of dementia, although the mechanism remains uncertain. Evaluate new confusion, depression and established dementia on their own merits (pp. 620, 626–627).

## Energetics and the falling resting metabolic rate

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-c248e85aeb9a0ac89190125f" data-practice-ids="mcq-b908f69d4209b7df1626ff41 mcq-0d9c67b9956bfdeaad3acc11" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Figure 42-2 links muscle loss to reduced resting metabolic rate and exercise capacity. Weakness and slower walking limit activity. Undernutrition and low energy expenditure can perpetuate the cycle. This figure supports the answer to June 2025 Q76. The direction depends on the stage: the energy cost of compensating and repairing damage may initially raise resting metabolism. Later, energy production becomes limiting, and resting metabolic rate falls despite ongoing stress (pp. 617–619, 625–626).

The clinical features form the outer layer of the model. The next layer includes candidate biomarkers of altered signaling, body composition, energy balance and neural function. The inner layer comprises proposed cellular mechanisms of aging. Chronic mild inflammation can accompany reserve loss even without overt disease. A clinician can use physical function to estimate risk without first identifying which cellular process caused it (pp. 617–618, 623–625).

## Prognosis in the patient with chronic disease

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-bbf366bf4045abbb79de704d" data-practice-ids="mcq-b2952aa21770b352a5cbc44b mcq-cd1e5b9e55753699940d9d75 mcq-74e2cad216a2756c12498a40 mcq-1c1d22553b1e9359cd9cf5c0 mcq-1de979eb6f87a866165b9192 mcq-e1c101998a9937cc6c4f62c5 mcq-25e4fe27c7b411eb023f5473" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Functional assessment adds information that a diagnosis list misses. A patient who has recovered from pneumonia on paper may still need help transferring because reserve across systems was limited before admission. Reduced reserve increases susceptibility to disability, hospital care and death even without a new acute illness. The consensus described in the chapter supported screening from age 70. The authors emphasize the limited intervention evidence available at the time and distinguish observational associations with activity or diet from proof that an established syndrome can be reversed (pp. 616–619, 626).

**Before surgery**, consider postoperative pneumonia, delirium, longer stay and the possibility of discharge to institutional care. **Before dialysis**, explain that frailty predicts hospitalization and mortality among new dialysis patients. Frail patients started at a higher estimated GFR on average. The observations do not show that an earlier start improves function or survival. CKD and other illnesses can contribute to frailty, while frailty can also develop independently (pp. 627–628).

**Before a cardiac procedure**, consider reserve when discussing rehabilitation and the likely care needs after discharge. In diabetes, CHS participants with frailty had higher glucose and insulin both at baseline and with oral glucose loading. The direction of causality remains uncertain. In cancer, assess function, cognition, social support and reserve to estimate tolerance of burdensome treatment. In transplantation, add the patient’s goals for independence and quality of life to organ-specific estimates of risk (pp. 627–629).

## Summary

**Definitions & diagnostic models**
- Frailty (consensus definition): medical syndrome, multiple causes, ↓ strength/endurance and ↓ physiologic function → ↑ vulnerability to dependency/death; should not overlap with disability, and not exhaustively defined by sarcopenia or multimorbidity; screening endorsed for all individuals **70 and older** (p617)
- **Physical Frailty Phenotype** (Fried/Cardiovascular Health Study) — 5 criteria: unintentional weight loss, weakness, exhaustion, slowness, low activity (p619)
  - **≥3 of 5** criteria = frail; **2 of 5** = prefrail; 1 alone = risk factor only, not frailty itself (p619) — note Table 42-3 instead scores intermediate/prefrail as **1–2** criteria (p621)
  - Weight loss: **>10 lb** lost unintentionally in the prior year (p620)
  - Weakness: grip strength lowest 20% (stratified by gender/BMI) (p620)
  - Slowness: **15-ft** walk time, slowest 20% (by gender/height) (p620)
  - Low activity: lowest 20% kcal/wk; men cutoff **<383 kcal/wk** (p620)
  - Drawbacks: no cognitive dimension; weight-loss criterion can miss sarcopenic-obesity frailty; thresholds derived from CHS cohort (p620)
- **Deficit Accumulation / Frailty Index (FI)** (Rockwood): unweighted ratio of deficits present ÷ total considered, built from a series of **70** measurements ("deficits") (p620, p623); rises nonlinearly with age (Gompertz-like), higher in women, ceiling **FI ~0.7** (p623)
- **Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)**: the **original seven-category** version used clinical judgment and predicted institutionalization and death similarly to the FI (p623). The [current CFS has nine levels](https://www.dal.ca/sites/gmr/our-tools/clinical-frailty-scale.html).
- **Edmonton Frail Scale (EFS)**: **9 domains** — cognition, general health, function, social support, meds, nutrition, mood, continence, performance (p623)
- **Cognitive frailty**: physical frailty + mild cognitive impairment (**CDR 0.5**), with AD/dementia excluded; it has been suggested that physical frailty should precede the cognitive impairment, to separate physically driven cognitive decline (p627)
- **Table 42-3** (p621) catalogs further named frailty instruments beyond FPP/FI/CFS/EFS — Gill Frailty Measure, Brief Frailty Instrument, Vulnerable Elders Survey (VES-13), FRAIL Scale (p621–622)
  - **Table 42-3** also spells out the **original CFS’s seven categories** (p621): 1 = very fit; 2 = well (no active disease); 3 = well, with treated comorbid disease; 4 = apparently vulnerable (not frankly dependent, "slowed up"); 5 = mildly frail (IADL help only); 6 = moderately frail (help with IADLs + ADLs); 7 = severely frail (ADL-dependent) or terminally ill. The current nine-point CFS scores terminal illness as 9.
  - **VES-13**: frail if score **≥3** (range 0–10); **FRAIL Scale** (fatigue, resistance, ambulation, ≥5 of 11 specified illnesses, weight loss ≥5% over the preceding year): **0** = no frailty, **1–2** = intermediate, **≥3** = frailty (p622; validated FRAIL questionnaire, Morley et al., PMCID PMC4515112).

## How frailty changes over time

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-b873bb5b1df46aca06bbca1b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Epidemiology**
- Community-dwelling age ≥65: average prevalence **11%** (reported range **4.0–59%** across definitions/settings) (p626)
- Broader definitions > Fried tool: **14% vs 10%** (p626)
- Rises with age: **16%** at 80–84 → **26%** at ≥85 (p626)
- Women > men: Fried **9.6% vs 5.2%**; FI **39% vs 37%** (p626)
- Hospitalized inpatients: **27–80%**; institutionalized: **30–70%** (p626)
- Exhaustion is typically the first CHS criterion to appear (p626)
- Nearly **60%** of adults >70 transition between frailty states at least once over **4.5 years**; progression to a worse state is more common than recovery, and frail→nonfrail reversal is rare (p626)

These estimates come from particular populations and instruments, so an estimate for a ward should not be applied directly to an independent person in the community. Frailty is more prevalent at both low and very high BMI. Muscle weakness and slow gait may be present despite stable weight in sarcopenic obesity. A shift from robust to intermediate function is clinically meaningful because state transitions can precede dependence (pp. 620, 626–627).

## Biological changes and geroscience

**Pathophysiology ("onion" model, Fig 42-1)**
- Concentric layers: outer = clinical presentation (multimorbidity, physical/cognitive impairment); middle = biomarker layer (inflammation, hormones, body composition, energetics); inner = core biological hallmarks of aging (p617)
- "Inflammaging": chronic elevation of IL-6 and TNF-α, present even in disease-free older adults (p624); accumulating senescent cells spilling SASP molecules may account, at least in part, for the high circulating inflammatory markers (p625)
- Multi-hormonal decline: DHEAS, testosterone, estrogens, GH/IGF-1, vitamin D ↓, with relative preservation of cortisol/thyroid hormones (p625)
- Muscle energy demand rises as much as **100-fold** from rest to contraction — mitochondrial decline + reduced satellite-cell repair capacity impair this reserve (p625)
- RMR = **60–70%** of total daily energy expenditure; initially rises (compensation), later falls as frailty develops — hypothesized that this RMR shift marks failure of resilience and accelerated, irreversible deterioration (p626)

Reduced muscle quality helps explain why strength can fall faster than lean mass. Motor neuron loss, imperfect reinnervation, fat infiltration, impaired mitochondria and diminished repair capacity can all contribute. Peripheral motor units become fewer and larger, and sensory conduction and reflexes slow. Central changes can also compromise executive function, processing speed and multitasking. These changes affect the ability to perform daily activities even when an individual organ test appears reassuring (pp. 625–626).

In the proposed geroscience framework, frailty, multimorbidity and geriatric syndromes can share aging-related mechanisms. Higher IL-6 over time was associated with faster accumulation of chronic diseases. In people who already have obesity, unintentional loss of weight can accompany worsening health. Researchers are studying whether molecular profiles and measured function can identify groups who respond differently to treatments. No biomarker panel presented in this chapter replaces clinical assessment (pp. 629–630).

## Disease-specific findings

**Frailty in specific disease contexts**
- Surgery: frailty predicts postop pneumonia, delirium, UTI, longer stay, nursing-home discharge, and higher mortality; a multidimensional frailty score was more useful than conventional methods for predicting surgical outcomes (p627)
- CKD: CHS participants with CKD had **2-fold** risk of frailty + disability; frail patients start dialysis earlier (higher eGFR) with no proven survival/functional benefit (p628)
- CVD: frailty **~3×** more prevalent with CVD; CAD in women → de novo frailty **12% vs 5%** over 6 yr; frail elders develop CAD events at **3.6% vs 2.8%/yr** (p628)
- HIV: frailty prevalence **5–20%** in younger HIV+ patients; risk ↑ with **CD4 <350 cells/mm³**, further risk factors: **CD4 <100 cells/mm³**, clinical AIDS, previous opportunistic illnesses (p628)
- Transplant: MELD predicts waitlist mortality but predicts posttransplant outcomes only at scores **above 35**; Fried-criteria frailty tracked quality of life better than MELD severity in end-stage liver disease (p629)
