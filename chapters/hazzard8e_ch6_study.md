# Chapter 6 Study: Social Determinants of Health, Health Disparities, and Health Equity

> Excluded from the IMA required reading list (P005-2026); not cited in the 2020-2026 past papers.

## Summary

**Separate the concepts**
- Social determinants are conditions of daily life: education, economic stability, health-care access, neighborhood/environment and social/community context (p95-96).
- Health disparities are differences in outcomes driven by unjust or inequitable conditions. Health equity is an ongoing pursuit of optimal health for all, centering historically disenfranchised people (p97-98).
- Identity itself does not create disparities. Race is a social construct, not a biologic category; structural forces and discrimination shape exposure and opportunity (p98).
- Intersectionality considers overlapping identities and systems of disadvantage; avoid treating any group as homogeneous (p98, p100).

**History and the life course**
- Colonialism, slavery, segregation and later policies can have lasting health effects. Understand mistrust as potentially reflecting trust that institutions have not earned (p98-99).
- The developmental life-course view emphasizes sensitive periods; the structural view emphasizes cumulative, socially patterned exposures and weathering (p100).
- Older Indigenous, LGBT+, incarcerated, immigrant and rural populations may face distinct and intersecting barriers; individual assessment remains necessary (p99-100).

**Translate assessment into action**
- Ask about education, work, income, housing, transport, food, safety, social support and health-care access. Life-story prompts can make screening more meaningful (p101-103).
- Invite discussion of discrimination and prior care experiences; also recognize coping strategies, strengths and health-promoting behaviors (p101-102).
- Use the patient's terms and pronouns, accessible communication and appropriate language services. Cultural knowledge should not become stereotyping (p100, p102).
- Include family, friends and supports chosen by the patient; avoid restrictive definitions of family or imposing clinician preferences (p102).
- Organizational/policy approaches include workforce diversity, integrated health and community services, funding and evaluation of local outcomes (p102-103).
- Community services may meet nutritional, housing, counseling, safety and financial needs, but availability, quality and access vary; check actual local resources before referral (p104).
- Assess consequences of clinical decisions such as stopping driving, including isolation and transport access. Work with the patient to connect medical recommendations with practical support (p104-105).

### Applying the social ecological model to a fall

One person's fall can have causes at several levels. Ask what the person is able to do at home, whether the rental agreement allows repairs and whether safe, affordable housing is available locally. The same housing hazard can persist because of an interpersonal agreement, organizational practice or public policy. A community intervention may reach people who would never be identified through office screening alone (p95–97, p101).

The chapter's US demographic overview placed older-adult poverty around **10%** at the time described, with rates nearly twice as high among racial and ethnic minorities in aggregate. That comparison prompts inquiry about structural exposure. It cannot tell a clinician what a particular patient earns or whether the person wants assistance. Historical restrictions on lending and homeownership affected where families could live and the wealth they could pass on (p98–100).

Ask what an older adult's neighborhood was like growing up, what work they did and who helps them now. These questions can uncover a barrier as well as a source of support. A clinic that asks about transportation or food needs should identify a community partner, document the referral and follow up on whether the person obtained help (p101–104).

### Name the determinant, the unequal outcome and the aim of intervention

A social determinant is a condition in which people grow up, work, live or age that can influence health. The chapter groups these conditions into economic stability, education, access to and quality of health care, neighborhood and built environment, and social or community context. A determinant may expose someone to risk or provide protection. Having a neighbor who clears a snowy entrance is a resource; having a landlord who will not repair an unsafe step is a constraint. Neither fact can be inferred from diagnosis, race, income category or the patient's age alone (p95–97).

A health difference is an observation. Calling it a disparity asks whether unjust or inequitable conditions contributed to that difference, particularly for people who have experienced disadvantage. Pursuing health equity then requires action on those conditions and attention to whether opportunities for good health improve. Recording that two groups have different fall rates is an initial description. Learning that safe housing modifications and access to therapy differ between them identifies possible points for action. The same education leaflet given to everyone may leave the gap untouched when the barrier is housing control, transport or ability to pay (p95–98).

Figure 6-1 uses falls to distinguish a *domain* from a *level of action*. Education includes understanding prevention advice; money and housing tenure affect whether the person can install a rail. Family or neighbors can assist with household tasks, neighborhood design affects opportunities for exercise, and the health system may or may not provide geriatric assessment or public-health prevention. Each domain can operate at individual, interpersonal, organizational, community or public-policy level. The links in the figure are examples rather than an assignment of each domain to one fixed level (p96–97).

### Follow a plausible pathway rather than using identity as an explanation

Figure 6-2 is the National Institute on Aging research framework. Its environmental column includes residential segregation, geographic access, toxins, income, education and health services. The sociocultural column includes social norms, discrimination, family and workplace stress, bias and loneliness. The behavioral column includes coping, resilience, social support and health practices. The biological column includes physiological stress responses, inflammation and cellular processes. All are interpreted over the life course. A group label at the top of the diagram identifies a dimension along which experiences may differ. It does not provide the biological mechanism by which a particular patient developed an illness (p97–98).

For example, repeated relocation may disrupt access to a regular clinician, socially supportive relationships, medications and a place to exercise. The resulting change in blood pressure, mobility or mood can be recorded as a biological or clinical outcome. The diagram encourages asking how that endpoint came about and which part of the sequence can be changed. Observing that an outcome is more common in one group cannot establish which pathway operated, and a proposed stress mechanism should not be treated as proof about a particular individual. Information about opportunities, exposures and the patient's account is necessary to move beyond the association (p96–100).

Figure 6-3 illustrates intersectionality with overlapping identities of an older adult, a Black person and a woman. The overlap matters because an individual's experience may reflect intersecting systems of ageism, racism and gender discrimination. Simply adding the mean risk from separate group comparisons misses that interaction. Two people with the same labels may have different neighborhoods, occupations, social ties, health resources and encounters with institutions. Ask how the person identifies and what experiences have mattered to them. A clinical assessment can recognize patterns of exclusion while avoiding stereotypes about what a patient values, whom they trust or what help they require (p98–100).

### Why history belongs in a present-day assessment

The chapter's historical examples connect a policy with later opportunities. Discriminatory mortgage lending restricted where some households could purchase homes or obtain credit. Housing location affects exposure to hazards, schools, transport, employment and accessible services. Homeownership can also affect whether a person accumulates and transmits wealth or can modify a home when mobility declines. The enduring pathway is constrained opportunity and resources across generations. A neighborhood with many residents sharing an identity is not itself evidence of a harmful cultural preference; involuntary exclusion from other options is the relevant distinction (p98–99).

Other examples include colonial dispossession, slavery and segregation, coercive treatment of people with disabilities, exclusion from benefits and disproportionate incarceration. They affect health through different routes, such as separation from family, trauma, loss of income or barriers to treatment. A patient who has previously been denied respectful care may have good reason to hesitate when a new clinician requests sensitive information. Describing this only as a patient's “mistrust” obscures what the institution must do to earn trust. Explain why a question is being asked, whom an answer will be shared with and how the information could help (p98–102).

The chapter's U.S. population figures illustrate why a uniform older-adult profile is inadequate. At the time described, roughly **10%** of older adults lived in poverty, with nearly double that rate among racial and ethnic minority populations in aggregate. Its demographic projection for **2060** placed people aged 65 or older at **23%** of the total U.S. population. These are a historical estimate and a projection, respectively. A high group-level risk supports attention to barriers and allocation of resources; it does not establish one patient's income, need for assistance or desire for a particular service (p99).

### Developmental timing, cumulative exposure and resilience

Figure 6-4 spans infancy, childhood, early and middle adulthood, and late adulthood. The illustrative foci move from family and education to occupation and retirement. Environmental quality, housing, psychological stress, income, social connectedness and health-care access can operate over different parts of that sequence. The diagram places outcomes such as maternal and fetal health, disease burden, functional capacity and life expectancy on different time scales. Arrows indicate that onset and duration vary; they are not a timetable requiring every person to experience a fixed exposure at a fixed age (p100–101).

The developmental life-course perspective emphasizes critical or sensitive periods during which an exposure may have long-lasting consequences. An adverse exposure during fetal development or infancy may affect health long after the immediate exposure has ended. The structural perspective asks why harmful and protective exposures recur unevenly over a lifetime. Housing insecurity, hazardous employment, restricted access to education and difficulty obtaining care can accumulate rather than acting as isolated events. The weathering hypothesis links chronic exposure to such structural disadvantage with a greater burden of disease. The two perspectives answer different questions about *when* an exposure matters and *why* it remains concentrated (p100–101).

A life history can also reveal health-promoting resources. Long-standing relationships, community organizations, spiritual practices and strategies learned during prior adversity may help an older adult manage illness or maintain independence. Asking only about deficits can miss the person who provides transport, the group that shares meals or the coping routine that sustains activity. Ask what has helped the patient address similar problems before and which support they want involved now. Recognizing resilience does not imply that an individual should be left to compensate for a remediable structural barrier (p97, p100–103).

### Population-specific barriers require individual questions

The chapter describes Indigenous communities affected by displacement, historical trauma and uneven access to care, as well as cultural and community resources that may support well-being. For an older immigrant, clarify language, prior health-care experience, insurance or eligibility concerns, and whether relocation has disrupted support networks. An individual in a rural area may have a regular clinician but impractical travel for specialty care, or strong local support despite geographic isolation. Ask what makes the proposed care hard to obtain and which local resources are already working instead of assuming that identity, geography or immigration history determines access in one direction (p99–102).

For LGBT+ older adults, family-of-choice relationships may be central to daily help, care decisions and safety. Earlier discrimination and restrictions on benefits may influence whether someone feels safe disclosing a relationship. Ask who matters to the person, which names or pronouns they use and who may receive information. A marriage entry, emergency contact or next-of-kin field cannot answer each of those questions. Invite the chosen supporter with the patient's permission and give the patient space to speak privately when needed. The same open approach can benefit people who do not use any particular identity label (p98–102, p104–105).

Older incarcerated adults face barriers during imprisonment and after release. The chapter notes that studies often use **age 50** as the threshold for older prisoners, reflecting an earlier burden of chronic disease and disability; this is not a universal biological threshold for old age. A person may need mental health care, accessible accommodation or nursing-home-level support after release while facing barriers to admission, benefits or housing. The applicable question is whether an actual transition plan can provide those services. Counting an eligible referral as completed can conceal the very barrier the team hoped to address (p100, p103–104).

### Use a life story to choose one useful action

Table 6-1 pairs open questions with social domains. Childhood and early-adult history can disclose education, segregation, family ties and work exposures. Asking who relies on the patient may uncover caregiving responsibilities that limit availability for appointments. A description of the house and neighborhood can reveal steep steps, unsafe conditions, absence of food shops or lack of transport. A clinician who asks only “Do you have any social problems?” is unlikely to learn which part of the current treatment plan is practically impossible (p101–103).

A question about a usual day can be more informative than an inventory of deficits. If the person stopped attending a valued activity, ask what changed. The answer may be fatigue, a bus route that no longer runs, fear of falling outside, cost, caregiving for a partner or a combination. A question about what gets in the way of self-care can elicit difficulty paying for medication, finding a clinician who speaks the preferred language or understanding an instruction written in technical English. Distinguish a positive screening response, a need the patient identifies as important, and the intervention they are ready to accept (p102–104).

The book suggests using more than one visit when necessary. Explain the reason for the questions, invite disclosure of previous discrimination without demanding it, and avoid duplicating or collecting sensitive details without a clinical purpose. A structured form can support consistency, while a conversational history can give meaning to an apparently simple entry such as “lives with family.” The person living with relatives may provide all the household's care, receive substantial assistance, or both. Record the practical implication for the plan and check the patient's preferred next step (p101–103).

### What changes at the level of a team or an organization

A clinician can immediately adapt communication, obtain language assistance, ask permission before involving relatives and identify a practical obstacle to a treatment. An interpersonal intervention may engage the patient's chosen supporters or address unequal power in an encounter. Community partners can identify barriers that are hard to see from the clinic and help design services acceptable to those who will use them. Structural change may require a more diverse workforce, different funding, accessible facilities or stronger connections between medical and social organizations. These approaches can work together: resolving today's missed appointment does not preclude addressing the transport problem affecting many patients (p101–103).

Before introducing a screening template, decide who asks the questions, where patients can answer privately, who responds, which local organizations can realistically help and how the team will learn whether the patient obtained assistance. Tailor what is asked to needs the team can address or document meaningfully. If people report hunger but the only offered meal service has a long wait or a transport barrier, the number of referrals overstates success. Follow up on access, acceptability and what changed in the patient's daily life; recurring gaps may then identify a service or policy change requiring collective action (p102–104).

### Community-based services: match the service to the actual bottleneck

Table 6-2 presents U.S. examples with different functions. Area Agencies on Aging are local or regional coordinating centers focused on older adults, described in the chapter as serving people aged **60 or older**. Aging and Disability Resource Centers can connect people to long-term services and benefits. A meal program can address food access while also providing social contact or an opportunity for someone to notice deterioration. A senior center offers a different kind of connection. Adult day services can provide supervision, activity and respite, but program hours, transport, level of medical support and payment requirements can determine whether attendance is feasible (p104).

Home-based approaches such as CAPABLE connect the older adult's own functional goals with changes in the home and health care. PACE in the U.S. framework described coordinates care for eligible people aged **55 or older** who would otherwise require nursing-home-level care, with a designated network and limited local availability. For a caregiver seeking a break during work hours, adult day care and comprehensive medical coordination are not synonymous. Ask what part of the week is difficult, what the patient can do safely when alone and what the household can afford. A resource locator can identify possibilities, but it does not establish that a service is open, appropriate or available to this person (p103–104).

### The driving case: address safety and preserve the relationship needed to make it possible

The chapter's case concerns a **75-year-old** veteran who identifies as African-American and attends a memory clinic. Visuospatial and short-term memory impairment and repeated minor collisions raise an immediate concern about driving safety. A marital-status entry leads the clinician to assume that the person the patient wants called is his wife. He leaves before a safe plan is arranged and is later hospitalized after a crash. Only then does the team meet his male partner and learn about earlier discrimination during military service. The case does not suggest that the concern about unsafe driving was mistaken. It shows how an assumption about family can undermine the relationship needed to address the risk (p104–105).

A more informative discussion would still name the specific safety concern. Ask whom the patient trusts to join the conversation, how that person should be contacted and what permission the patient gives. Establish how stopping driving would affect access to food, health care, social contact and activities that maintain identity. Address immediate transport and follow up on a sustainable alternative rather than leaving the patient with a restriction and no way home. The relevant life-course information is not a justification for stereotyping a veteran or anyone who identifies as queer. It helps explain what made an apparently routine question consequential and how to avoid repeating the harm (p101–105).

## Exam traps

- A population difference requires investigation of the relevant social and structural mechanisms before attributing causation (p97–99).
- A protective community network can coexist with housing or economic disadvantage. Ask about existing strengths as well as unmet needs (p97, p101–103).
- A documented spouse, a chosen supporter and a person authorized to receive information need not be the same individual (p102, p104–105).
- PACE and adult day services differ in eligibility, scope, provider arrangement and local availability (p104, Table 6-2).

- The level of an intervention and the domain of a determinant are different: housing can be addressed at individual, community or policy level (p95–97).
- Historical disadvantage can accumulate across a lifetime and across generations (p98–100).
- A referral entered into the chart cannot establish that a patient obtained the service (p103–104).
