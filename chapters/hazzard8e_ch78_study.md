## Claudication and the limits of a walking prescription

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-2cae81434ec4479d828221ef" data-exam-read-page="1211" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Ask what makes the patient stop.** A fixed distance to calf or buttock discomfort, relief within about ten minutes of standing still, and recurrence at a similar distance suggest a vascular limitation. Stenosis of the spinal canal produces a more variable distance and often improves with forward flexion over a walker. Hip or knee pain confined to a joint follows a different pattern. Several causes may coexist. Establish which one actually prevents shopping, climbing a slope or crossing the street (pp. 1209–1210).

**Treat the outcome in the question.** For greater walking distance, offer smoking cessation support and structured exercise. A typical session in the chapter consists of repeated walking bouts totaling about 30 minutes, three times a week. Walk past symptom onset to tolerable discomfort, pause for relief and repeat. Increase workload with supervision. Adapt the program for balance, musculoskeletal limitations and cardiac symptoms. Avoid extending the prescription for stable exertional discomfort to a suddenly painful or threatened limb. Trials summarized in the chapter reported gains in distance of roughly 50% to 200% with a formal program (p. 1211).

**Vascular prevention and symptom relief have different targets.** Antiplatelet treatment and a high-intensity statin reduce atherosclerotic events. Full-dose apixaban has no role solely for claudication. In someone still limited despite exercise, cilostazol can improve walking distance. The book calls it a phosphodiesterase **II** inhibitor [sic — the drug inhibits phosphodiesterase III] and advises **caution in heart failure** (p1211). The trial data on exercise demonstrate functional improvement even without a measurable increase in limb blood flow (p. 1211).

> Current practice (not the book): Cilostazol is a PDE-III inhibitor and must not be given in heart failure of any severity. [2024 ACC/AHA PAD guideline, sections 5.1, 5.7 and 6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.02.013).

**Decide whether the limb or the person's usual activity is at risk.** Figure 78-1 illustrates why cardiovascular prevention matters even when exertional leg symptoms stay stable. For persistent, lifestyle-limiting symptoms despite exercise and medical treatment, a short focal stenosis is a favorable target for balloon angioplasty, with stenting when appropriate. Longer occlusions are less durable after endovascular treatment. Vein bypass can restore flow across a longer segment, at the price of more perioperative morbidity. Renal failure, oxygen-dependent lung disease, heart failure, frailty and baseline mobility influence whether a procedure can achieve the person's goals (pp. 1210–1212).

**Rest pain changes the evaluation.** A painful distal toe lesion, persistent forefoot pain at rest, nonhealing ulcer or gangrene suggests insufficient tissue perfusion. Examine the limb and check pulses, Doppler signals, motor function and sensation. A newly painful, cold, numb or weak foot needs emergency assessment for acute limb ischemia, including graft occlusion after bypass. For stable suspected PAD, calculate the ankle–brachial index on each side using the higher ankle systolic pressure divided by the higher brachial systolic pressure. A value of 0.90 or lower is abnormal. Diabetes and kidney disease can produce noncompressible arteries and a misleadingly high ABI, prompting toe pressure or toe–brachial testing. Reserve anatomical imaging for a question that would change management (pp. 1210–1212). [2024 ACC/AHA PAD guideline, sections 3.1 and 11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.02.013).

## Carotid stenosis after a TIA or an incidental bruit

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0248cf414c0e282383f0c986" data-exam-read-page="1213" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Establish the vascular territory.** A recent episode of monocular blindness in the ipsilateral eye, contralateral weakness or a compatible hemispheric language deficit can implicate a carotid plaque. A bruit establishes neither the severity of stenosis nor its responsibility for a symptom. Isolated dizziness or generalized weakness calls for a different evaluation. Duplex ultrasonography estimates stenosis. CTA or MRA can clarify equivocal findings and anatomy when a procedure is being considered. Renal function matters when choosing contrast imaging (pp. 1212–1213).

**At 50%, anatomy alone cannot decide.** Start antiplatelet and lipid-lowering therapy, address smoking, blood pressure and diabetes, and investigate competing stroke mechanisms. June 2023 Q8 gives a right carotid stenosis and a TIA without the deficit or its laterality. If ipsilateral and recent, this is the lower limit of the moderate symptomatic range. The book recommends intervention in symptomatic patients with **more than 70%** stenosis (p1213).

> Current practice (not the book): Endarterectomy can be appropriate in selected patients with symptomatic 50%–69% narrowing. The recommendation is stronger at 70%–99%. [European Stroke Organisation, symptomatic stenosis recommendations, Table 7](https://doi.org/10.1177/23969873211012121).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0248cf414c0e282383f0c986" data-exam-read-page="1214" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Immediate harm and time to benefit.** Limited mobility and nephropathy increase the competing risks in the 78-year-old woman. Weigh functional status, expected survival, severity, timing, operative results and her preference together. The book gives acceptable 30-day stroke/death rates below 6% for symptomatic disease and below 3% for asymptomatic disease. The chapter recommends intervention for >70% symptomatic or >80% asymptomatic stenosis in centers with excellent outcomes and patients with anticipated survival exceeding five years. It should not delay assessment after a recent TIA (pp. 1213–1214).

**Endarterectomy or transfemoral stenting.** Surgery removes plaque and carries more myocardial infarction and local cranial nerve injury. Transfemoral stenting avoids neck dissection but traverses the arch. Older patients often have more calcification and tortuosity there and face greater perioperative embolic stroke risk. Prior neck radiation or surgery can favor a stent. The book also lists significant coronary artery disease and congestive heart failure among reasons to consider carotid stenting. Transcarotid stenting uses direct carotid access with flow reversal, a distinct route from transfemoral stenting (p. 1214).

**An 80% asymptomatic stenosis at age 75.** The historical question offers endarterectomy for an independent patient with severe asymptomatic disease. Hazzard gives **more than 80%** as its asymptomatic threshold on p1213 and describes **greater than 70%–80%** on p1214. Consider anticipated survival and procedural risk. Begin intensive medical management in every case.

> Current practice (not the book): CREST-2 subsequently studied high-grade stenosis without recent symptoms in two parallel trials at experienced centers. At four years, the composite outcome was 2.8% with transfemoral stenting plus medical management versus 6.0% with medical management alone. The corresponding endarterectomy comparison was 3.7% versus 5.3% and was statistically inconclusive. Procedural harm occurred early. The trials did not randomize patients between the two procedures. [CREST-2, primary outcomes and Table 2](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2508800).

## Bilateral gaiter pigmentation and venous duplex

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ee6477061bffbce186548a21" data-exam-read-page="1216" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Read the distribution in photograph 8.** Brown discoloration over both distal lower legs points to chronic venous hypertension. Leaky valves or proximal obstruction allow pressure to persist when upright. Red cells escape into tissue and leave hemosiderin around the malleoli. Fibrosis of subcutaneous fat produces the firmer skin of lipodermatosclerosis. Heaviness and swelling often worsen with dependency and improve overnight. Ask about prior deep vein thrombosis and prolonged sitting. Bilateral edema alone is nonspecific. Heart failure, immobility and lymphedema also cause swelling (pp. 1216–1217).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-ee6477061bffbce186548a21" data-exam-read-page="1217" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

**Choose a test that shows the mechanism.** Venous duplex images the veins and uses Doppler to determine the direction and duration of flow. Reflux lasting at least half a second in a superficial venous segment is significant by the chapter's criterion. ANCA testing investigates selected vasculitic syndromes and troponin measures myocardial injury. Neither identifies venous reflux. CT venography can investigate suspected proximal obstruction, such as extrinsic compression, when duplex and clinical findings suggest it (p. 1217).

**Match treatment to perfusion.** Elevation at rest reduces hydrostatic pressure. Compression counters venous hypertension and supports ulcer healing when arterial inflow is adequate. Assess arterial perfusion before strong compression when pulses are reduced, ischemic pain is present or disease is mixed. A venous ulcer with superficial reflux warrants assessment for early endovenous ablation in addition to compression. Compared with deferring ablation, early treatment accelerates healing and increases ulcer-free time (p. 1217).

## Lipid lowering in later life

**Why the four patients with LDL 160 differ.** A prior myocardial infarction with coronary stenting and documented atherosclerotic PAD each confer a secondary-prevention indication. Diabetes at age 75 supports a statin for primary prevention even with good glucose control. In the 86-year-old smoker with hypertension and no identified atherosclerotic disease, discuss starting a statin in light of life expectancy, preferences, medication burden and potential benefit. Chronological age alone is insufficient to rule it out. The chapter's PAD passage supports the secondary-prevention part of this GRS question (p. 1211). [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline, sections 4.2.5, 4.2.6 and 4.2.8.3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2025.11.016).

## Abdominal aortic aneurysm

**Detect and measure it.** Most abdominal aneurysms arise below the renal arteries and are silent. Smoking, age and a first-degree family history raise suspicion. A normal abdominal examination can miss a small aneurysm. Ultrasound is the usual screening and surveillance method. CTA defines the aortic neck and iliac access for repair. MRI is an alternative when other imaging is unsuitable. Control blood pressure and vascular risk, support smoking cessation, and encourage appropriate aerobic activity. Doxycycline did not slow expansion of small aneurysms (pp. 1214–1215).

**Schedule imaging by size and symptoms.** Table 78-2 (p1216) presents weak SVS recommendations based on low-quality evidence and lists three years for 3.0–3.9 cm, one year for 4.0–4.9 cm and six months for 5.0–5.4 cm. These are intervals for an asymptomatic aneurysm. New abdominal or back pain potentially attributable to an aneurysm needs prompt assessment regardless of the scheduled interval. Sudden severe pain or hemodynamic instability raises concern for rupture (pp. 1215–1216).

> Current practice (not the book): ACC/AHA starts six-monthly surveillance at 5.0 cm in men and 4.5 cm in women. [2022 ACC/AHA aortic disease guideline, sections 6.4.3.2 and 6.5.5.3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2022.08.004).

**Decide when repair offers a gain.** Maximal diameter predicts rupture and drives the elective decision. Hazzard considers elective repair in good surgical candidates with diameters **greater than 5.5 cm in men or 5 cm in women**. Expansion **greater than 0.50 cm in six months** can also indicate intervention. Endovascular repair requires suitable landing zones and access. It usually offers a shorter admission and lower early mortality, followed by long-term imaging for endoleak or sac enlargement. Open repair may suit unfavorable anatomy, but major surgery and possible suprarenal clamping increase physiological and renal stress (pp. 1215–1216).

> Current practice (not the book): ACC/AHA recommends repair at **≥5.5 cm in men or ≥5.0 cm in women**, or for attributable symptoms; growth **≥0.5 cm in six months** may justify repair. [2022 ACC/AHA aortic disease guideline, sections 6.5.5.3 and 6.5.6.2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2022.08.004).

## Summary

**Peripheral arterial disease**
- PAD affects up to **20%** of people over **70** and commonly reflects atherosclerotic narrowing from the infrarenal aorta to tibial vessels (p1209).
- Vascular claudication occurs at a relatively reproducible walking distance and subsides with rest. Spinal stenosis symptoms have more variable onset and may improve with forward leaning (p1210).
- Examine distal lesions and pulses, but assess motor/sensory function when acute or severe ischemia is suspected; foot viability is not determined by pulses alone (p1210).
- ABI is the highest ankle systolic pressure divided by the highest brachial systolic pressure. Further imaging should fit the specialist's diagnostic/intervention plan (p1210–1211).

**Management & drugs**
- Smoking cessation and supervised walking are central. Typical exercise sessions last **30 minutes**, repeated three times weekly, walking as far beyond pain onset as safely tolerated (p1211).
- Antiplatelet therapy and statins address vascular risk. Hazzard describes improved walking distance with cilostazol and advises caution in heart failure (p1211).

  > Current practice (not the book): Cilostazol is contraindicated in heart failure of any severity. [ACC/AHA 2024, §5.7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.02.013).
- Consider claudication intervention for lifestyle limitation after an exercise trial; progression to limb loss is not inevitable (p1209–1210).

**Carotid disease & aneurysm**
- Carotid plaque can embolize and cause stroke; maximize antiplatelet, lipid and risk-factor management, including smoking cessation (p1212–1213).
- The chapter generally favors endarterectomy over stenting in older patients; stenting has specific indications and is not routinely indicated for asymptomatic older patients (p1209–1210).
- Hazzard defines AAA as enlargement of vessel diameter by **more than 50%**, generally **over 3 cm in men** (p1214).

  > Current practice (not the book): Screening guidance commonly defines AAA as an aortic diameter of **3.0 cm or greater**. [USPSTF, 2019](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-screening).
- For a good surgical candidate the chapter gives diameter **>5.5 cm in men or >5 cm in women** or rapid growth **>0.50 cm in six months** as indications for intervention (p1215). Endovascular repair can have short-term benefits, with later outcomes dependent on anatomy, follow-up and reintervention (pp1215–1216).
- Endovascular repair still requires long-term imaging follow-up; medical management includes smoking cessation and control of comorbid vascular risks (pp1215–1216).

**Venous disease & exam traps**
- Chronic venous insufficiency results from venous hypertension, usually incompetent valves; elevation and compression are the mainstays (p1216–1217).
- AAA does not automatically prohibit aerobic activity: the chapter encourages activity and notes no rupture-prevention benefit demonstrated for doxycycline (p1215).
