# Chapter 50 Study: Mobility Assessment and Management

## Summary

**Definition & consequences**
- Mobility is moving the body through space, from bed movement and transfers to walking and stairs. Its limitations threaten independence and predict adverse outcomes (p741).
- Disability depends on the person and environment, not simply an isolated impairment; biomechanical, biomedical and psychosocial perspectives are complementary (p741, p746).

**Assessment**
- Evaluate cardiopulmonary, musculoskeletal, neurologic and psychological contributors; use gait speed, the short physical performance battery or Timed Up and Go as appropriate (p741).
- Triage into nonambulatory, ambulatory or vigorous mobility. Nonambulatory assessment emphasizes bed movement/transfers; more able patients need progressively challenging walking tasks (p751–752).
- Usual walking speed should generally be at least **1 m/s**; the chapter illustrates a **4-m** walk in under **4 seconds** (p750).
- Gait aid use, speed below **1 m/s**, short steps or asymmetry indicate a need for closer assessment (p751).
- For apparently normal walkers, higher-level tests include a **30-second** single-foot stand, tandem walking or over **450–500 m** in **6 minutes** (p751).
- Performance tests measure capacity under test conditions, not actual daily activity; standardize instructions and consider cooperation and fluctuating performance (p745).

**Management**
- Address impairments while practicing mobility tasks; combine strength, balance, endurance and flexibility training as needed (p755).
- Use assistive/orthotic devices, environmental adaptations and caregiver training to improve stability, participation and weight-bearing pain (p741, p755).
- Deconditioning is almost always present as a consequence of reduced mobility and inactivity and is treatable in many sick or frail older adults. General conditioning and progressively complex walking practice may help (p755).


### Matching the mobility test to the clinical question

- The Short Physical Performance Battery combines a timed **4-m** walk, **5** chair rises and **3** standing balance challenges. A total below **10 points** signals risk of disability. A change of **1 point** is substantial. Performance measures can help characterize sarcopenia, whereas Barthel mobility items describe transfers, walking or wheeling, and stairs (Table 50-2, p743).
- In the Timed Up and Go, start in a chair, stand, walk **10 feet**, turn, return and sit. Table 50-2 gives **under 10 seconds** as normal, **under 20 seconds** for community mobility and **over 30 seconds** for needing assistance. A result between the community and assistance thresholds warrants assessment without automatically assigning dependence (p744).
- The six-minute walk measures distance on an uninterrupted course. The table gives **at least 300 m** for community ambulators and changes of **20 m** and **50 m** as small and substantial. The long-distance corridor walk instead records distance over **2 minutes** followed by time for **400 m**. Each extra minute to finish was associated with a **52%** higher rate of mobility disability (p744).
- Compare like with like when following gait speed. A standing versus rolling start and instructions can change the result. Table 50-2 calls speeds below **0.6 m/s** slow and below **0.4 m/s** very slow. A change of **0.05 m/s** is small but meaningful and **0.10 m/s** is substantial (p744).
- Watch each foot through stance and swing. Double support means that both feet touch the ground at the same time. It occupies a larger fraction of the cycle with age as speed slows and steps shorten. A patient can take shorter steps without necessarily widening the lateral distance between footprints (p747–748).

### From impairment to the activity the patient has lost

Start with an activity the person wants to do. “I can no longer get to my usual place of worship” gives a better starting point than “my legs are weak.” Ask about getting out of a chair, reaching the door, negotiating the steps, boarding transport and walking from the stop. The limiting point could be painful loading of one knee, breathlessness after a short distance, fear on an uneven pavement or the absence of a handrail. Each would produce the same loss of participation and a different treatment plan. Table 50-1 uses the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health to separate these observations. Strength and joint motion describe the body, transfers and walking describe tasks, and returning to a valued role describes participation (p741–742).

Ask how the activity is accomplished as well as whether it is possible. Someone who still shops but now uses a trolley for support and takes a rest before walking home has already adapted. A yes/no independence question can conceal that loss of reserve. Conversely, an inability to shop alone may chiefly reflect inaccessible transport. To set a goal, establish the walking or transfer requirement, the help available, and what would count as a worthwhile improvement for this particular person. Maintaining a safe independent toilet transfer can profoundly affect care needs even when community walking is no longer attainable (p742–743, p750–751).

### Choosing a measure and interpreting disagreement between measures

Self-report, observation by a professional and timed performance provide different descriptions of mobility. A patient can report an activity that seldom occurs in a clinic, including how tiring it is or how much help is needed. A nurse may observe several transfers over a shift and identify how much lifting assistance is required. A timed walk can detect a small change that neither person would describe as a change in independence. Each source can be accurate even when they disagree. Ask whether the measurement concerns the same task, circumstances and period of time before attributing the difference to unreliable reporting (p742–745).

Table 50-2 illustrates why choosing a test starts with a question. PROMIS mobility covers a wide range of demands and can use either a short form or computer-adaptive testing. The Avlund scales ask separately about fatigue and help across **six activities**. Someone may need no hands-on help but tire with every task. Asking about changing the way one walks half a mile or climbs stairs can reveal preclinical limitation. At the other end of the scale, asking a wheelchair user about walking a mile will repeatedly give the lowest score even if moving between bed and chair has improved considerably (p742–743).

The Barthel mobility items cover walking or wheeling, transfers and stairs. They contribute to a basic ADL score. The table gives a total possible score of **100**, with higher scores representing more independence, and a minimal clinically important difference of **9.8 points**. FIM also describes the amount of help required in daily function. In the Minimum Data Set, staff can use individual bed mobility, transfer and locomotion items to describe care needs in a skilled nursing facility. These tools answer practical questions about assistance. A good score does not establish adequate endurance for a journey to the shops (p743).

In September 2024 Q28, match the instrument to the question being asked. The SPPB directly samples lower-extremity performance through walking, chair rises and standing balance. It can contribute to the assessment of physical performance in sarcopenia. The Barthel Index assesses basic function, FIM measures independence, and the TUG times a sequence of movements. A TUG result does not tell you whether a person can dress, bathe or use the toilet independently. Similarly, a low SPPB can arise from pain, neurologic disease or poor cardiopulmonary reserve. It cannot establish the cause of the performance deficit by itself (p742–745, p752).

June 2025 Q51 requires keeping **time, distance and test** together. The item describes a TUG of 25 seconds. It falls beyond the book's community-mobility benchmark of **under 20 seconds**, without crossing the **over 30 seconds** assistance benchmark. Watch where the delay occurs: pushing out of the chair, initiating gait and turning place different demands on the patient. The alternative of 400 m during a six-minute walk exceeds the table's 300-m community benchmark. A high SPPB score likewise represents better performance. Finally, the initial timed segment of the long-distance corridor walk records how far the patient travels; its subsequent 400-m segment records how long completion takes. Substituting one measure for the other changes the interpretation (Table 50-2, p743–744).

### Fear, confidence and actual exposure to walking

Ask whether the patient is afraid of falling, how confident they are in a specific activity and whether they have stopped doing it. The answers may diverge. A person who feels reasonably safe indoors may have given up outdoor walking altogether. Another may continue walking despite considerable fear because no one else can obtain groceries. The absence of a recent fall in the first person gives little reassurance about performance outdoors; the threatening activity has largely disappeared from daily life (p745, p749–750).

The scoring direction matters in Table 50-3. On the Falls Efficacy Scale, **1** indicates great confidence and **10** no confidence for each item, and a total above **80** identifies increased fall risk. On the Activities-Specific Balance Confidence Scale, by contrast, **100** marks complete confidence. The modified Gait Efficacy Scale also gives higher scores for greater confidence. A higher number therefore cannot be called “better” without identifying the scale. A performance observation can then test the activity the person fears, while the interview identifies what is actually being avoided at home (p745).

### Population findings and prognosis

Distinguish basic indoor mobility from higher-level outdoor mobility. In the estimates cited, dependence in getting around indoors rose from **5%** among adults aged **65–74** to **30%** among those aged **85 or older**. Among community-dwelling adults aged 65 or older, approximately **5%** needed help with bed or chair transfers and **7.5%** needed help getting around indoors. Among institutionalized people older than 65, about **80%** needed help with these activities. The task and setting are essential parts of the estimate. A clinic caring for independently living adults will encounter limitations in distance and stairs that an indoor dependence question misses (p745–746).

A person who reports no mobility concern can still have a slow measured walk. In the studies summarized, performance predicted later ADL difficulty, hospitalization and mortality even among initially nondisabled people. Poor baseline performance was associated with roughly twice the hospitalization risk over **4 years** in one population study. Slowing may reflect interacting illness, inactivity or declining reserve. For follow-up, record the method and compare the person's trajectory with their previous performance. The pooled relationship between speed and survival provides prognostic information; it cannot tell the clinician how much longer this individual will live after walking faster on a test (p745–746).

### What the footprints and joint movements reveal

Figure 50-1 follows weight transfer across the gait cycle. At initial contact, controlled ankle and knee movement accepts the load. During stance, the hip abductors steady the pelvis over the supporting limb. Late stance brings hip extension and ankle push-off; during swing the foot must clear the ground and advance until it can take weight again. Watch when the abnormality occurs. Failure to control the foot as it meets the floor produces a slap, whereas inadequate clearance during swing can produce a drag. Both can involve weak dorsiflexors, but the phase in which the difficulty is seen guides the examination (p747, p753).

Figures 50-2 and 50-3 separate distance from rhythm. Compare the right-to-left and left-to-right steps for length, then look at several successive steps. A limp may give a consistently shorter step on the same side. Unpredictable variation from one step to the next gives a different pattern associated with falls and disability. Step width describes the lateral space between footprints. A short step and a wide base are independent findings, and neither alone establishes the cause of the gait disorder (p747–748).

June 2023 Q51 asks which change tends to accompany aging. The supported finding is a larger fraction of the cycle in double support, with both feet on the ground. Common accompanying changes are slower speed, shorter steps, less hip extension and more external rotation of the foot. Older walkers may generate less power at the ankle and compensate with hip flexion. The item reverses the rotation or assigns the compensatory movement to the wrong joint in its distractors. More time on two feet can assist stability while making walking less energy-efficient. The patient may therefore use more of their available energy to cover a familiar distance (p747–748).

### How modest impairments add up

Organize the examination around sensory inputs, central processing and effectors. Vision, vestibular input and distal sensation tell a person where their body is. The brain integrates those signals with attention, timing and postural responses. Strength, range of motion, pain and cardiopulmonary endurance then determine whether the intended movement can be carried out. A marked deficit at one level may explain the difficulty. Several less apparent deficits may instead emerge only in combination. Mild neuropathy may be tolerated on a level, well-lit floor and become limiting on uneven ground in the dark (p748–749, p754).

Walking while talking or doing a mental calculation adds an attentional demand. A curved path, an obstacle or a faster turn challenges balance and timing. These tasks can reveal loss of reserve in someone whose ordinary straight walk looks adequate. The chapter describes associations between gait slowing, executive changes and brain imaging findings such as white matter disease, including in people without overt stroke. Those observations invite assessment of cognition and vascular risk. They do not turn a slow walk into a specific imaging diagnosis or prove that correcting one risk will restore mobility (p748–749, p752).

### Relating measured speed to the patient's day

Table 50-4 puts pace, distance and activity side by side. At roughly **0.67 m/s**, six minutes corresponds to about **240 m** and the listed activity level is self-care. At about **0.89 m/s**, the corresponding distance is **320 m** and the example is household activity. Around **1.1 m/s** or **400 m**, the table gives carrying groceries or light yard work; around **1.3 m/s** or **480 m**, climbing several flights. These are approximations to the demand of activities. Directly ask about a person's stairs, handrail, loads and opportunity to rest before deciding whether a particular activity is safe (p750).

Table 50-5 illustrates an energy-based classification. Its most limited level requires help to move from bed or chair; the next uses a wheelchair indoors but transfers independently. That difference determines whether another person must be available for the transfer. At a higher level the person may manage a block, sometimes with a cane, while at still higher levels sustained walking becomes feasible. At the vigorous end the example is at least **30 minutes** at a vigorous pace above **4 METs**. Distance, pace and duration together describe reserve more usefully than a single unqualified statement that a patient “walks” (p750).

### Working backward from an abnormal task

Figure 50-4 sorts the first visit by demonstrated ability and the potential for improvement. For someone who arrives in a wheelchair, first establish what happens when they attempt to rise with appropriate assistance. For an ambulatory patient, inspect speed, symmetry, step length and the need for a device. For an apparently vigorous patient, ask about more demanding activities and test the limitation that prompted the visit. Decide with the person whether the aim is restoration, compensation or both. The onset and course, a reversible impairment, the ability to take part in practice and the patient's preferences all affect that choice (p751–752).

Table 50-6 offers a primary-care starting point. With exertional dyspnea or fatigue, consider oxygenation during activity, airflow, anemia and cardiac function. With hesitancy or unsteadiness, examine tone, rapid tapping, executive function and peripheral sensation. With stiffness or weight-bearing pain, examine the hip, knee and back, then watch a chair rise and test strength. Exertional leg symptoms merit attention to pulses and to the possibility of spinal stenosis. A multidisciplinary assessment can be particularly helpful when several explanations coexist or the patient fails to improve after an apparently relevant impairment is treated (p751–754).

Table 50-7 links a movement to plausible causes rather than forcing a named diagnosis. High stepping may compensate for foot drop. A pelvic drop suggests weakness of the abductors on the opposite side of the drop. A patient can hike a hip to clear a limb with insufficient hip or knee flexion. Hesitant initiation occurs in several central disorders; a weaving path raises a vestibular question, while a broad base can occur with cerebellar or peripheral sensory disease. Observe the patient from more than one direction and examine the relevant joints and muscle groups before choosing an orthosis or prescribing task practice (p753).

### Choosing a treatment at the level of the problem

Table 50-8 gives examples of how an examination changes action. Check visual fields, depth and lighting when a patient misjudges a step, vibration and touch when foot placement is unreliable, and orthostatic symptoms and blood pressure when the patient feels faint on standing. Review medicines that could worsen postural hypotension, attention or dark adaptation. A medication trial for Parkinson-related slowing may improve speed while balance remains impaired; faster movement can expose the patient to injury unless rehabilitation accompanies it. The chapter gives treatment categories for these mechanisms and refers organ-specific prescribing to the relevant disease assessment (p754–755).

A light touch on a stable surface can improve balance through sensory information even when it supports little weight. A cane can therefore help a person with sensory loss, while a different patient may need a device primarily to unload a painful joint. Consider range-of-motion work for contracture, strengthening for loss of force and repeated practice of the actual transfer or turn. Split-belt treadmill work after stroke is one way of practicing more symmetrical stepping. For body-weight-supported treadmill approaches the chapter notes that well-delivered physical therapy can offer similar benefit. Select the treatment for the limiting task and reassess its effect on that task (p754–755).

Table 50-9 moves the assessment into the person's usual environment. Ask about depression, fear, support, transport, cost and the physical barriers at home. A ramp or handrail may permit participation immediately, while walking and strengthening are being developed. An unnecessary lift chair or a helper who performs every transfer can instead remove the very activity needed to retain strength. For the person unable to walk, practice rolling, scooting, sitting or standing when feasible. Train the helper in safe transfers, consider appropriate seating or transfer equipment, and protect skin exposed to pressure. When movement would add suffering in a person who is actively dying, the chapter makes comfort the relevant goal (p750, p753, p755–756).

## Exam traps

- A measure that assigns higher scores for confidence can move in the opposite direction from a fear or falls-efficacy scale (Table 50-3, p745).
- Distinguish the threshold for increased future risk, the tested person's baseline and a meaningful change after treatment (p743–744, p746).
- A shorter step on the same side on every cycle differs from unpredictable step-to-step variation (Figure 50-3, p748).
- A six-minute distance for community ambulation and a six-minute distance used to challenge an apparently vigorous walker answer different questions (p744, p751).
- Improvement in walking speed after medication may uncover persistent difficulty turning or balancing (p755).


- Gait speed is an integrative functional marker, not a diagnosis of the responsible impairment; assessment scales help choose tasks and monitor treatment (p750, p752).
- In a pooled community cohort, each **0.1 m/s** faster speed was associated with **13%** lower mortality risk; this is an association, not a guaranteed treatment effect (p746).
- With aging, step length and speed decline and double-support time increases; step length and stride length describe different distances (p747).

## Recall drill

**1. What question can reveal preclinical mobility limitation in an independent patient?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Ask whether the patient has changed how they walk longer distances or climb stairs, even if they still complete those activities. (p743)</details>

**2. Which tasks make up the SPPB?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Timed four-meter walk, five chair rises and three standing balance challenges. (p743)</details>

**3. How do you interpret an SPPB score below ten?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It identifies risk of disability. A one-point change is considered substantial in the table. (p743)</details>

**4. What does the Timed Up and Go include besides walking?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Rising from the chair, turning after walking ten feet, returning to the chair and sitting down. (p744)</details>

**5. What does a TUG between twenty and thirty seconds suggest?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The time is slower than the book's community-mobility benchmark but does not reach its greater-than-thirty-second assistance benchmark. Assess the tasks that were difficult. (p744)</details>

**6. Which distance changes are meaningful on the six-minute walk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The table describes 20 m as a small meaningful change and 50 m as substantial. (p744)</details>

**7. How is the long-distance corridor walk scored?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Record distance in the first two minutes and then the time to complete 400 m. Slower completion is associated with later disability. (p744)</details>

**8. Why record walking instructions and starting method?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Standing and rolling starts and different instructions can affect measured speed. Repeat the same protocol when following change. (p744–745)</details>

**9. How does a stride differ from a step?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A step runs between consecutive opposite-foot contacts. A stride spans successive contacts of the same foot. (p747–748)</details>

**10. How does double support change with age?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>A greater proportion of the gait cycle is spent with both feet on the ground. Gait speed and step length tend to decrease. (p747)</details>

**11. Why can an adequate clinic walk coexist with restricted activity at home?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The test measures performance under defined conditions. The home environment, available support and daily behavior influence actual mobility. (p745, p749–750)</details>

**12. What should be observed first when the patient arrives in a wheelchair?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assess bed and chair movement and whether standing or walking is possible with assistance. Select subsequent tasks accordingly. (p751–752)</details>

**13. What can reveal difficulty when usual walking appears normal?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Single-leg stance, tandem walking, longer distances or complex tasks such as obstacles, turns and walking while doing another task. (p751–752)</details>

**14. Why can too much help worsen mobility?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Assistance that replaces a task the patient can perform reduces practice. Repeated disuse can contribute to weakness and loss of function. (p750)</details>

**15. What should accompany treatment of an identified gait impairment?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Practice the relevant task and address strength, balance, endurance and flexibility. Adapt the environment or walking aid to the patient's goals and limitations. (p755)</details>
