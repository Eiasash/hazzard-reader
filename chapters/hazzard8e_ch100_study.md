## Giant cell arteritis with threatened vision

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-6a914458cacf7c8f2fe7390b" data-exam-read-page="1605" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>A patient over 50 with new headache, jaw pain on chewing and sudden visual loss has a high probability of cranial giant cell arteritis (GCA). Inflammation narrows arteries supplying the optic nerve and retina. Even transient monocular blindness or diplopia may precede irreversible loss. Start high-dose glucocorticoids immediately and arrange urgent specialist assessment. Active or threatened visual loss favors intravenous pulse glucocorticoids. In GCA without threatened vision, the chapter describes oral prednisone around 1 mg/kg/day, usually 40–60 mg/day. A PMR starting dose is inadequate for this emergency. (pp. 1603–1605)

**Understand a negative biopsy.** Disease can skip segments of an artery. Sample a long segment of an abnormal artery when possible and obtain the biopsy promptly after starting glucocorticoids. The chapter prefers the first week. A negative result occurs in up to one-third of cases. First review specimen adequacy and sections. If more evidence of cranial disease is needed, a biopsy of the opposite artery may help, although the additional yield is small. Assess for extracranial involvement when the diagnosis remains uncertain. (pp. 1604–1605)

**Where imaging fits.** The US ACR/Vasculitis Foundation guideline conditionally favors initial unilateral biopsy in its practice setting. After a negative biopsy it favors noninvasive large-vessel imaging with clinical assessment. EULAR recommends temporal and axillary ultrasound as first-line imaging where an experienced examiner can perform it promptly. With threatened vision, start glucocorticoids immediately. Neither imaging nor biopsy should delay treatment. [ACR/VF 2021, GCA diagnostic testing](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.41774) · [EULAR 2023 update, overarching principle A and recommendation 1](https://ard.bmj.com/content/83/6/741)

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0aa5f686e3b59340f14db1f7" data-exam-read-page="1605" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Who needs early tocilizumab?** It blocks the IL-6 receptor and helps sustain remission while glucocorticoids are tapered. In newly diagnosed GCA, substantial risk from prolonged steroids, anticipated prolonged treatment or relapse favors early addition. Someone presenting with visual symptoms still needs urgent glucocorticoids. Once symptoms are controlled, taper over months. Recurrent cranial symptoms or new limb claudication during taper require prompt reassessment. ESR and CRP alone cannot distinguish relapse from other inflammation. (pp. 1605–1606)

**Disease beyond the temporal artery.** Examine pulses and listen for bruits. Arm claudication may reflect subclavian disease, and aortic aneurysm or dissection may appear years after cranial symptoms resolve. Choose vascular imaging and follow-up according to the vessels involved. Address fracture prevention and monitor for steroid toxicity and infection during treatment. (pp. 1603–1606)

## Classifying giant cell arteritis

**The 1990 question.** In someone with vasculitis, three of five findings classified GCA in the original traditional criteria. These were onset at age 50 or later, new localized headache, an abnormal temporal artery on examination, Westergren ESR at least 50 mm/hour, and characteristic arterial histology. The examination finding is tenderness of the temporal artery itself or diminished pulsation. A biopsy can show mononuclear arteritis or granulomatous inflammation, often with multinucleated giant cells. A generic polynuclear infiltrate does not qualify. Neither leukopenia nor CRP three times normal was a criterion. (p. 1605, Table 100-5) [ACR 1990, Table 3](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.1780330810)

**The later classification system uses different evidence.** The 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria apply to research classification after vasculitis is diagnosed and mimics excluded. Age of at least 50 at diagnosis is required. Positive biopsy or a temporal artery halo carries five points, and ESR at least 50 mm/hour or CRP at least 10 mg/L carries three. A total of six or more points classifies a patient in the eligible research population. Neither scoring system should delay treatment when the clinical picture threatens vision. [ACR/EULAR 2022, Figure 1 and discussion](https://doi.org/10.1002/art.42325)

## Polymyalgia rheumatica

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-1fcd40e8291a57c865060963" data-exam-read-page="1601" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Pain-limited movement or true weakness?** PMR causes symmetric aching in the neck and proximal limb girdles, prolonged morning stiffness and stiffness after inactivity. Shoulder symptoms occur in 75–90% and hip symptoms in 50–70%. Patients may struggle to dress or rise because movement hurts. Test individual muscle groups, since strength is usually preserved when pain permits. Mild fever, malaise, sweats or weight loss can accompany this pattern. High fever is unusual. (pp. 1600–1601)

**Age, sex and peripheral joints.** PMR begins after age 50, roughly 90% of cases occur after 60 and women outnumber men about two to one. Inflamed bursae, tendon sheaths and synovium help explain why pain extends beyond one joint. About a third develop mild, symmetric, nonerosive peripheral arthritis. Persistent small-joint synovitis or erosions strengthen the case for elderly-onset rheumatoid arthritis. (pp. 1600–1601)

**Read the labs in context.** An elevated ESR or CRP accompanies at least 80–90% of presentations. Inflammatory anemia is typically normocytic, CK is usually normal, and both ANA and rheumatoid factor are usually negative. A negative ANA fits the syndrome but is nonspecific. Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP help investigate rheumatoid arthritis when the presentation overlaps. Inflammatory EMG changes have rarely been reported in PMR. Marked CK elevation or objective weakness instead warrants a myopathy evaluation. Microcytosis deserves its own workup. (p. 1601)

**Treat and reassess.** Use the lowest effective initial prednisone dose within the chapter’s 12.5–25 mg/day range, balancing relapse risk against toxicity. Expect clear improvement by two weeks and near-complete response by four. If response is poor, reconsider the diagnosis. Reach 10 mg/day in about four to eight weeks when feasible, then taper slowly through remission. Recurrent symptoms guide management more reliably than an isolated ESR rise. Methotrexate can reduce steroid exposure in selected patients. About 15–20% of patients with PMR develop GCA, while 40–60% of those with GCA have PMR. Ask at follow-up about new headache, jaw claudication and visual symptoms. (pp. 1600–1603)

## Dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b278a02fd7eb7f2e75f74912" data-exam-read-page="1592" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Recognize the combination.** Symmetric subacute proximal weakness, increased muscle enzymes and a characteristic rash suggest classic dermatomyositis. The June 2023 photograph shows erythema over the dorsal hand and finger joints, a distribution compatible with Gottron lesions. Ask about stair climbing and reaching overhead. The rash can precede weakness, and some patients have skin disease with little muscle involvement. A history of colorectal cancer raises concern for the recognized malignancy association without proving recurrence. Review age-appropriate screening and pursue abnormal symptoms or examination findings. (pp. 1592–1593)

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-b278a02fd7eb7f2e75f74912" data-exam-read-page="1593" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Match the test to the tissue.** Affected proximal muscles can show short, low-amplitude motor units on EMG and spontaneous activity with active inflammation. Muscle biopsy may show injury to capillaries, perifascicular atrophy and perivascular or perimysial mononuclear cells. Capillary complement deposition differs from high circulating C3 or C4. Assess dysphagia and dyspnea because aspiration, interstitial lung disease and respiratory muscle weakness affect management. (pp. 1590–1594)

**Which inflammatory disorder?** Necrosis with scant inflammation suggests immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. Anti-HMGCR can follow statin exposure, while anti-SRP disease can feature severe proximal weakness and cardiac involvement. Antisynthetase syndrome brings varying combinations of myositis, interstitial lung disease, inflammatory arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon and cracked “mechanic’s hands.” Isolated polymyositis is a diagnosis to revisit when a more specific phenotype fits. For treatable inflammatory myopathies, tailor glucocorticoids, early steroid-sparing therapy and supervised exercise to organ involvement, comorbidity, infection risk and function. (pp. 1593–1595)

**Inclusion body myositis.** In an older adult, years of falls from quadriceps weakness plus loss of grip from finger-flexor weakness suggest sporadic IBM, especially when weakness is asymmetric. CK can be normal. Endomysial inflammation with invasion of intact fibers or rimmed vacuoles supports the pattern. Dysphagia may precede limb weakness and increases aspiration risk. Immunosuppression rarely changes progression. Strength training, bracing, falls prevention and swallowing assessment address its consequences. (pp. 1595–1596)

## Drug-related weakness and muscle injury

**Statins during rehabilitation.** Ask about timing, statin dose, renal and thyroid function and interacting drugs, including amiodarone. New symptoms can occur months after initiation. Hazzard gives approximate proportions of treated patients with myalgia and normal CK (5–10%), myositis with elevated CK (1%), and rhabdomyolysis (0.1%). These are not annual risks. The [European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehv043) estimates rhabdomyolysis at about one per 100,000 treated patients per year. A CK above ten times the upper limit warrants stopping the drug and evaluating the injury. Persistent weakness or high CK after withdrawal raises concern for anti-HMGCR-associated necrotizing myopathy. (p. 1598)

**Chronic steroids and colchicine.** Prednisone can cause hip-girdle weakness and difficulty rising from a chair despite a normal CK. Type II fiber atrophy and a generally noninflammatory EMG help distinguish steroid myopathy from active inflammatory muscle disease. Taper steroids according to the underlying indication and begin reconditioning. Colchicine can cause proximal weakness, CK elevation and a vacuolar myopathy. Adjust the regimen to kidney function because even prophylactic doses can cause toxicity in renal impairment. (pp. 1597–1598)

**Other drug and toxin patterns.** Acute alcohol-related muscle injury can cause rhabdomyolysis. Chronic intake can cause proximal weakness with little CK elevation. Long-term hydroxychloroquine can cause a lysosomal myopathy with dysphagia, axial weakness or cardiac involvement. Checkpoint-inhibitor myositis can coexist with myocarditis, so cardiopulmonary symptoms during treatment need prompt assessment. (pp. 1597–1599)

## Evaluating other causes of weakness

**Localize first.** Ask which tasks have become difficult, test strength on the MRC scale, watch the patient rise from a chair and look for sensory loss or abnormal reflexes. Electrolytes, CK and aldolase help identify reversible metabolic causes and muscle injury. EMG can distinguish muscle from nerve involvement. MRI locates active muscle and helps select a biopsy site with informative tissue. Interpret histology in light of age-related changes. (pp. 1589–1592, Figure 100-1 and Table 100-1)

**Endocrine and infiltrative causes.** Hypothyroidism can cause aching, delayed reflex relaxation, proximal weakness and increased CK. Thyrotoxicosis can produce muscle wasting with relatively little CK elevation. Vitamin D deficiency with osteomalacia may present with diffuse bone pain, proximal weakness and a waddling gait. Consider amyloid with an unusually firm or enlarged muscle, nodules, dysphagia or otherwise unexplained myopathy. (pp. 1596–1597)

**Weakness evolving over years.** Facial weakness and scapular winging suggest facioscapulohumeral dystrophy. Ptosis and dysphagia before limb weakness suggest oculopharyngeal dystrophy. Limb-girdle dystrophies have varied genetic causes. Family history and molecular testing help distinguish them. Late-onset mitochondrial myopathy may present with exertional fatigue and proximal leg weakness. Ragged-red fibers require interpretation in context because they also occur with aging. (pp. 1599–1600)

**Bent spine or dropped head.** Axial weakness may correct when lying down or with passive extension. Look for primary muscle disease and neurologic causes before attributing it to vertebral kyphosis. Physiotherapy, orthoses and walking aids can improve function. A demonstrable inflammatory cause can change treatment. (p. 1600)

## Summary

**Myopathy assessment**
- Most myopathies cause proximal weakness; evaluate comorbidity, medications, functional impact and alternative metabolic/systemic causes (p1589–1591).
- Inclusion body myositis is the commonest inflammatory myopathy in older adults: slow, often asymmetric finger-flexor/quadriceps weakness, falls and possible dysphagia (p1589, p1595).
- Normal or only mildly elevated muscle enzymes do not exclude inclusion body myositis; biopsy and the characteristic clinical pattern matter (p1595–1596).
- Persistent weakness or CK elevation after statin withdrawal suggests another myopathy or immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy; anti-HMGCR antibodies may be relevant (p1598).
- For inflammatory myopathies, assess swallowing/lung involvement and malignancy; glucocorticoids with early steroid-sparing treatment require attention to infection, renal/hepatic disease and drug interactions (p1594–1595).

**Polymyalgia rheumatica**
- PMR produces symmetric neck/shoulder/hip-girdle pain and stiffness, often with constitutional symptoms; exclude myopathy, malignancy, infection and other rheumatic disease (p1601).
- GCA occurs in an estimated **15–20%** of PMR patients; conversely, PMR occurs in **40–60%** of GCA patients (p1600).
- Prednisone: minimum effective initial dose within **12.5–25 mg daily**. Improvement should appear by **2 weeks**, with near-complete response by **4 weeks** (p1601).
- Reduce toward **10 mg/day** within **4–8 weeks**, then slowly during maintained remission; the chapter describes decrements of **1 mg every 4 weeks** (p1601–1602).
- Poor initial response warrants reconsidering the diagnosis. Follow symptoms, not ESR alone, and monitor cumulative steroid toxicity (p1602).

**Giant cell arteritis**
- New headache, scalp sensitivity, jaw claudication and visual symptoms are key clues; constitutional illness or PMR can predominate (p1603).
- Negative temporal artery biopsy does not exclude GCA. Ideally obtain biopsy within **1 week** of starting steroids, but do not delay treatment for it (p1605).
- Suspected GCA: prednisone **1 mg/kg/day**, commonly **40–60 mg daily**; active visual symptoms warrant higher-dose urgent treatment as described in the full chapter (p1605).
- Treatment often lasts around **2 years**, with slow taper and relapse surveillance; inflammatory markers supplement rather than replace clinical assessment (p1605).

**Exam traps**
- PMR and GCA overlap but require different initial steroid doses; visual risk makes suspected GCA an immediate treatment priority (p1600–1605).
- Steroids can both treat inflammatory disease and cause myopathy; distinguish drug toxicity from persistent inflammatory weakness (p1589, p1594).
