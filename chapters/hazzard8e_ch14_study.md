# Chapter 14 Study: Models of Hospital and Outpatient Care

## Summary

**Choose a model that fits**
- Define the local problem, population, patient goals, team and payment environment; measure baseline outcomes and implementation fidelity (p191-194).
- Models aim to preserve function and quality of life in the least restrictive safe setting; sustaining and spreading them requires leadership, resources and outcome feedback (p191, p203-204).

**Hospital and perioperative care**
- ACE combines a supportive environment, interdisciplinary assessment, functional care and early discharge planning; ACE consultation applies its principles without a dedicated unit (p194-197).
- HELP addresses cognition, sleep, mobility, vision, hearing and hydration; the described target is age **70+** with at least one delirium risk factor (p198).
- NICHE develops nursing expertise through geriatric resource nurses and evidence-based care processes (p199).
- Hospital VA STRIDE provides supervised walking after early assessment; selection excludes patients for whom the protocol is unsuitable (p199).
- AGS CoCare: Ortho co-manages hip fractures; GSV sets geriatric surgical standards; POSH coordinates geriatrics, surgery and anesthesia for elective surgery (p200).

**Community and home models**
- GRACE combines primary care with nurse-practitioner/social-worker support for low-income older adults (p200-201).
- PACE requires age **55+**, state-certified nursing-home eligibility, residence in the service area and ability to remain safely in the community with services (p201).
- STEADI screens, assesses modifiable fall risks and intervenes; outpatient STRIDE is a separate nurse-led fall-injury prevention model (p202).
- Home-Based Primary Care serves severe disabling chronic illness; Independence at Home targets high-risk, high-cost patients with limited mobility and coordinated longitudinal care (p202-203).
- CAPABLE combines occupational therapy, nursing and home repairs to improve function and support aging in place (p203).
- UCLA dementia care uses specialist-supported nurse-practitioner co-management to support patients and caregivers; Gerofit offers exercise for eligible older veterans (p203).
- Fit services to patient abilities and exclusion criteria; similar program names do not imply identical populations or evidence (p199, p202-203).
- Sustain the model with an advisory team including a patient, outcome reporting, staff development and plans for dissemination (p204).

### What the outcome tells you

In the ACE studies, assess the ability to function at discharge as well as length of stay. Patients on ACE units had less disability at discharge and were less likely to leave for skilled nursing care than those receiving usual care. In HELP, fewer delirious days and episodes did not translate into lower severity or recurrence in the comparison described. Fidelity matters when interpreting both a successful trial and a disappointing local result (p194, p198–199).

GRACE paired an in-home assessment with interdisciplinary review and a plan worked out with the primary care clinician. In the high-risk group, reduced emergency and hospital use contributed to lower costs by the **second year**. CAPABLE takes a different route to remaining at home. The nurse and occupational therapist identify goals with the older adult, and a worker makes practical changes to the home so those tasks become possible (p196, p200–201, p203).

### Start with a care failure rather than the program name

An older person can receive technically correct treatment of pneumonia and still leave hospital less able to walk, more confused and unable to return home. An outpatient can receive all the recommended disease prescriptions but be unable to reach the clinic or use the medicines. The chapter's models address these gaps between disease treatment and daily life. Table 14-1 draws attention to remaining safely at home, preventing disability, preserving quality of life and respecting individual goals. Caregiver needs and psychosocial circumstances belong in that definition of quality from the outset (p191–192).

Specify the population before selecting the intervention. Frequent falls among community patients, delirium on an acute ward and disability created by hazardous housing may all lead to an emergency visit, but preventing them requires different components. A walking assistant on an inpatient ward cannot repair a patient's stairs. A home repair cannot substitute for management of delirium during admission. Define where the event begins, who can identify the at-risk person and what action is feasible at that point. Then choose an outcome that would actually reveal improvement (p191–194).

Table 14-2 distinguishes a best-practice model, quality improvement and research. A model organizes care for a defined population using a set of components. A local improvement project starts with a specific gap and changes a process while following outcomes over time. Research aims to answer a question that extends beyond the immediate service. All three require attention to who receives the intervention and how consistently it was delivered. A project can appear ineffective because no one implemented its key component, or appear effective because the population changed between baseline and follow-up (p192–193).

Table 14-3 addresses payment. Under fee-for-service arrangements a clinician may be paid for a face-to-face visit while team discussion, follow-up calls or contact with a caregiver generate little direct reimbursement. A prepaid or integrated organization may benefit directly when avoidable institutional care falls. The same model can thus improve outcomes in two places and be financially sustainable in only one. State whose costs are counted, when the benefit appears and whether the organization asked to pay for the team is the one that receives the savings (p192–194).

### Acute Care for Elders: a unit, an assessment and a way of working

On admission to an ACE unit, ask what the patient could do before the current illness, how much help was needed at baseline, what has changed and what support would be needed to return home. The purpose is to prevent the routines of hospitalization from producing additional dependence. Mobility, self-care, cognition, sleep, nutrition and medication exposure require active attention while the medical illness is being treated. A patient who remains in bed because the team is focused solely on oxygenation may satisfy a disease-specific discharge criterion while losing the ability to reach the bathroom (p194–197).

Figure 14-1 connects a prepared environment, care focused on maintaining function, frequent review of medical care and discharge planning begun at admission. A supportive environment makes activity and orientation easier. Interdisciplinary review makes it possible to identify a new restraint, sedative, catheter or impediment to mobility and ask whether it remains necessary. Discharge planning uses the person's baseline and trajectory to identify barriers early. These components reinforce each other; teaching a patient to walk on one shift is less effective if unnecessary tethers keep them in bed on the next (p194–197).

Figure 14-2, the ACE Tracker, illustrates the distinction between making risk visible and correcting it. A concise patient-level display can bring ADL impairment, risk exposures or other geriatric issues to the attention of a team reviewing many patients. It cannot determine by itself which medication can safely stop or which patient needs direct assistance out of bed. Figure 14-3 places interdisciplinary assessment and action within ordinary clinical care. The team has to assign someone to act, see whether it was done and follow function to discharge (p197–198).

A hospital that cannot concentrate all older patients on one unit can use an ACE consult service. Staff review patients across existing wards and communicate recommendations to the primary team. This extends reach, but the consultant has less control over the physical environment or the ward's daily routines. For a credible local comparison, ask which patients were eligible, whether the recommendations were implemented, and how much of the original model was delivered. A consultation automatically generated for every older patient is not equivalent to a functioning interdisciplinary unit (p194–198).

Table 14-4 summarizes which models target a ward, a subgroup or people living in the community. For ACE, relevant outcomes include lower disability at discharge and less discharge to skilled nursing care in the studies described. Readmission or length of stay can also matter, but neither alone measures the goal of preserving independence. If a new program reduces length of stay by transferring more people to institutions, the hospital statistic and the patient-centered outcome may move in opposite directions (p194–196).

### Preventing delirium, building nursing expertise and restoring walking

HELP is selected prevention. The chapter targets hospitalized adults aged **70 or older** with **at least one** delirium risk factor. Figure 14-4 links cognitive impairment to orientation, stimulation and social contact; sleep deprivation to a nonpharmacologic sleep routine; immobility to activity and fewer tethers; and visual or hearing impairment to effective use of aids. Oral volume repletion and feeding assistance address hydration and intake. The relevant intervention is the combination matched to this patient's risks. A brief orientation conversation alone is not the full program (p198).

A trained interdisciplinary team identifies eligible patients, and a specialist trains and supervises volunteers delivering specific activities. Nurses, therapists, pharmacy, nutrition and other ward disciplines continue to provide their own clinical care. In the prospective matched comparison, the **incidence of delirium fell by 40%**. There were fewer delirium episodes and days, without a reduction in severity or recurrence in the comparison described. This is prevention evidence, not proof that an established episode will immediately resolve after HELP enrollment. A change in staffing or volunteer coverage can change how much of the prevention protocol actually reaches the patient (p198–199).

NICHE has a different point of entry. The organization develops nurses with geriatric expertise, including geriatric resource nurses who can support colleagues and disseminate evidence-based care across units. Its effect is mediated by the nursing workforce and by clinical processes, not by admitting every patient to a special ward or scheduling a volunteer visit. For a hospital with widespread gaps in geriatric nursing practice, this may address the mechanism more directly than a small specialist unit. Assess whether staff retain the skills and whether bedside practice changes (p199).

The VA hospital STRIDE program addresses mobility during an acute admission. A physical therapist assesses gait and balance; a trained assistant then supervises daily walking and monitors safety. The book's exclusions include a bed-rest order, chest pain or angina, a new neurological deficit, inability to follow a simple command or inability to walk. This is not a list of people who can never benefit from rehabilitation. It defines who was suitable for this particular supervised walking protocol. The alternative for someone outside it is an individualized assessment (p199–200).

Initial hospital STRIDE comparisons found the approach feasible and participants less likely than similar controls to be discharged to skilled nursing care. The prevention mechanism is early mobilization rather than a new diagnostic test. If a local version is staffed only intermittently and most patients miss their walks, a lack of benefit does not resolve the question about the original intervention. Measure completed assessments and walks as well as outcomes such as discharge destination. Keep this hospital service distinct from the outpatient fall-injury trial also named STRIDE (p199–200, p202).

### Perioperative models do different jobs

AGS CoCare: Ortho brings medical and orthopedic expertise together for older people with hip fracture. Prompt attention to pain, delirium risk, comorbidity, mobility and discharge needs can proceed alongside planning fracture repair. The key difference from a late geriatric consultation is shared attention to predictable complications throughout the episode. At handoff, the receiving rehabilitation team needs the previous mobility and cognitive baseline as well as the procedure and immediate surgical restrictions (p200).

The American College of Surgeons Geriatric Surgery Verification Program specifies standards by which a surgical organization builds and evaluates its care of older patients. It is an institutional framework, rather than a particular patient's preoperative visit. Duke POSH instead illustrates a clinical model of coordinated preoperative assessment and management involving surgery, anesthesia and geriatrics for selected older patients undergoing elective operations. A hospital can use perioperative standards to identify a service gap and implement a clinic or co-management service to address it; these are complementary levels of intervention (p200).

### Community assessment, comprehensive services and fall prevention

GRACE was designed for low-income older adults receiving primary care. A nurse practitioner and social worker assess the individual at home, the interdisciplinary team reviews the findings, and the resulting plan is worked out with the primary clinician. The home setting can reveal poor medication access, an unsafe transfer or a caregiver unable to provide assumed support. The nurse practitioner and social worker contribute different information and remain involved in carrying out the plan; simply forwarding an assessment report to a busy practice omits a central part of the model (p200–201).

The chapter describes the low-income entry criterion as income below **200% of the federal poverty level**, alongside the published program's other eligibility conditions. Outcomes differed by baseline risk, and utilization-related savings in the high-risk group appeared by the second year. This is a useful warning about evaluation periods. A first-year budget that includes startup and staff time but no time for altered use to emerge may underestimate value. Conversely, expected future savings are not cash that a fee-for-service clinic can automatically use to pay its social worker today (p200–201).

PACE addresses another population: people aged **55 or older** who meet the state's nursing-home-level-of-care requirement, live within the service area and can safely remain in the community with program support. The apparently contradictory criteria identify the purpose of the model. The person has substantial long-term-care needs but can receive coordinated services without current institutional placement. The interdisciplinary program assumes broad responsibility for services and uses its payment structure to coordinate them. A primary-care consultation alone cannot provide the same package of care (p201).

For a patient who has fallen at home, STEADI begins by identifying fall risk, assessing modifiable contributors and intervening on the findings. A positive screen is the beginning, not the intervention. If the patient is unsteady when standing, the action could involve review of blood pressure, medicines, gait, vision or exercise needs according to the assessment. Outpatient STRIDE used a nurse-led approach to multifactorial fall-injury prevention. Its target is community fall injury and its trial outcomes must be distinguished from the discharge outcomes of the hospital walking program (p202).

The community STRIDE cluster-randomized trial did **not** find a statistically significant reduction in the first adjudicated serious fall injury. That is a more specific endpoint than “any fall,” improved balance or increased confidence. Its nurse and primary clinician still identified issues such as postural hypotension, hazardous medications, gait impairment, sensory problems and environmental obstacles, then drew up an individualized plan and followed it. The practical lesson is to act on an identified risk while describing honestly what the trial demonstrated. The negative primary endpoint neither proves every component ineffective nor justifies claiming a demonstrated reduction in serious first injuries (p202).

### What must be brought to the home?

Home-based primary care is longitudinal primary care for people whose serious chronic disability makes office-based visits difficult or inadequate. It differs from a short home assessment followed by a report. The team can review the interaction between diseases, medicines, function, the caregiver and the physical setting over time. If a patient keeps missing visits because leaving the house requires assistance that is unavailable, scheduling the same office visit more frequently does not solve the access problem. Follow whether home delivery enables timely assessment across subsequent changes in condition (p202–203).

Independence at Home illustrates targeted enrollment and aligned incentives. The book describes fee-for-service Medicare beneficiaries with **at least two chronic conditions**, assistance needed with **at least two basic ADLs**, and a nonelective hospitalization plus acute or subacute rehabilitation in the preceding **12 months**. The first home visit is described as occurring within **24–72 hours** of discharge. These criteria identify a complex group at risk during transition. The model adds longitudinal accountability across settings and quality-linked payment; it is more than an isolated postdischarge check (p202–203).

CAPABLE begins with the activities the older adult wants to regain. An occupational therapist observes the task and the home, a nurse addresses limiting symptoms or medication problems, and a worker makes practical modifications. A person who cannot safely bathe may be limited simultaneously by pain, an awkward transfer and lack of a stable handhold. A prescription for exercise without a safer environment, or an installed handhold without training and attention to pain, may not restore bathing. The combined intervention targets that mismatch between the person's ability and the demands of the home (p203).

The UCLA dementia care program coordinates a nurse practitioner, a physician dementia specialist, the primary and specialty teams, community services and the caregiver. The assessment is deliberately about both the person with dementia and the person providing care. An individualized plan may involve caregiver training, support groups, management of behavioral symptoms and a way to obtain advice when circumstances change. The chapter describes active follow-up with at least a telephone interaction every **4 months**. The planned contact allows a new safety problem or caregiver strain to trigger revision rather than waiting for the next acute admission (p203).

Gerofit targets selected older veterans for exercise and health promotion. The chapter describes eligibility at age **65 or older** with sufficient independence to participate in a group. Inability to transfer, oxygen dependence, unstable cardiac disease and moderate to severe cognitive impairment are among its exclusions. A deconditioned person using an aid can still be eligible; a person unable to participate safely may need another service. Outcomes reported included improved physical function and well-being. Apply these results to the sort of patient the program could safely enroll (p203).

### Implementation, sustainability and dissemination

Start by establishing the local problem and who owns it. If the concern is discharge disability, collect functional outcomes before introducing a mobility or acute-care model. If the concern is caregiver breakdown, admission counts alone may be too late and too nonspecific to reveal benefit. Involve direct-care staff, leaders and patients or caregivers early enough to choose an outcome they value. Then train the people who will actually deliver the intervention, document the essential components and agree on how to distinguish an incomplete rollout from a fully delivered but ineffective intervention (p191–194, p203–204).

Sustainability is a different question from an initially successful pilot. An enthusiastic clinician or temporary grant can make a service work briefly, but the chapter describes the need for leadership, an advisory group including a patient, an ongoing workforce plan and regular outcome reports. When proposing expansion, identify which functions have to be preserved and which can be adapted to local resources. A volunteer-supported delirium program, a prepaid comprehensive service and a home-repair intervention have very different requirements. Reusing the name without funding or staffing the defining components does not reproduce the model (p198–204).

## Exam traps

- A consultation can bring ACE methods to an ordinary ward. The dedicated unit also changes the ward environment and daily routines (p194–197).
- Distinguish a fall-risk screen from an intervention that actually addresses the patient's identified risks (p202).
- PACE enrollment requires safe community living with program support at enrollment even though nursing-home-level care is needed (p201).
