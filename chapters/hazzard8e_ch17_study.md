## US care models — match the service to the missing help

Ask who is available when the patient bathes, takes medications or transfers at night. Personal help may come from family or a paid aide. Skilled home health adds nursing or therapy for a defined need. A patient may need both. In the US chapter, Medicare skilled home health requires homebound status and a skilled need. A visit for wound care or therapy leaves other hours uncovered (pp237–239, Table 17-1).

Adult day care fills a daytime gap while the person keeps the same residence. Social programs provide supervised activities. Medical programs can also give medication and monitor the patient. Assisted living brings services into an apartment, adult foster care involves moving into a family home, and a continuing care retirement community offers different residential care levels as needs change. Ask about preferences, practical caregiver capacity and local availability before deciding on a move (pp239–241, Table 17-1).

PACE combines medical, social, rehabilitative and personal care. A coordinated plan can address medication supervision, transport and caregiver respite. Enrollment still requires age 55 or older, residence in the service area, state certification of nursing-home-level need, and the ability to live safely in the community with PACE support. A day center alone is an incomplete discharge plan for someone who needs help with every evening transfer. Patients without Medicaid may face premiums. The chapter reports less hospital use in studied populations and mixed costs across payers (pp238–239, 243–244, Tables 17-1 and 17-2). [Medicare, PACE eligibility and costs](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/health-plans/your-coverage-options/other-medicare-health-plans/PACE).

After fracture or stroke, assess rehabilitation needs now and plan continuing support in parallel. A skilled nursing facility (SNF) can provide short-term skilled rehabilitation when transfers and mobility remain unsafe at home. Reassess function and home help before discharge. The number of covered days in a vignette is not a prescription for length of stay. A Medicaid home- and community-based services waiver may provide ongoing personal support, subject to state eligibility and program capacity (pp238–242). [Medicare, Skilled nursing facility care](https://www.medicare.gov/coverage/skilled-nursing-facility-care) · [Medicaid, 1915(c) waivers, eligibility and covered services](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/home-community-based-services/home-community-based-services-authorities/home-community-based-services-1915c).

## Home visits, transitions and outcomes

Use two questions to identify a model. How long will the team stay involved, and how much medical care must it deliver? Home-based primary care takes ongoing responsibility for patients who struggle to reach the office. A co-management team works with the existing primary clinician. Integrated medical and social care also addresses behavioral and social needs. Palliative care can accompany disease treatment and helps clarify goals and relieve symptoms. Home hospice focuses on comfort near the end of life (pp238–243, Figure 17-1).

Hospital at home supplies acute hospital-level treatment with medical and nursing oversight. Substitution starts directly from the emergency department. Transfer moves an admitted patient home while hospital-level needs continue. In selected patients, studies found lower costs and fewer complications. Rehabilitation at home instead substitutes postacute skilled therapy for a rehabilitation stay. Transitional care supports the handover with medication review, education and follow-up. Community paramedicine may assess an urgent call or make a planned postdischarge visit (pp239, 242–244, Tables 17-1 and 17-2).

Preventive visits seek risks in relatively independent adults. CAPABLE brings an occupational therapist, nurse and handyperson to address functional goals set by the older adult. MIND at Home connects families affected by dementia to a dementia-care team through a community worker. A preventive assessment, a home modification and ongoing dementia coordination each solve a different problem (pp238–241, 243–244).

Unpaid caregivers deliver much of the help. Care can cost them wages, health and future retirement income even when no bill is submitted. A consumer-directed program can pay a chosen helper, sometimes a relative, while giving the patient responsibility for arranging the work. Expansion can also reveal previously unmet needs and increase spending. Interpret cost comparisons in light of the population, comparator, outcomes and payer. Studies of selected patients do not give a universal threshold for safe home care (pp241–245).

Video visits, transmission of images for later review and remote measurements can extend the reach of home care. Their value depends on what is being monitored and who can act on the result. The chapter also describes point-of-care tests and incentives for teams accountable for a population's costs and outcomes. Evidence varies by condition, setting and comparison group (pp245–246).

## Summary

**Match home care to need**
- LTSS covers personal, nursing and social support; home-based medical care adds clinician-delivered care. Terminology overlaps (p237).
- Most people needing long-term care prefer remaining in the community; unpaid family and friends provide much of the care (p237, p241).
- Consider function, cognition, chronic illness, goals, caregiver capacity and local services when selecting a model (p237-241).
- Episodic models include skilled home health, transitional care, rehabilitation and hospital at home; longitudinal models include primary care, co-management, integrated medical/social care and PACE (p238-241).
- Day care, assisted living and continuing-care communities offer different mixes of housing, supervision and services (p239-241).

**Delivery and payment**
- Home-based primary care targets complex, often homebound patients; hospital at home substitutes acute hospital-level treatment for selected patients (p238, p243).
- PACE integrates medical, social and functional care for eligible nursing-home-level patients; home palliative/hospice care addresses serious illness and symptoms (p238-239).
- In the United States, funding varies across Medicare, Medicaid, private payment and local resources; coverage of skilled care differs from ongoing personal assistance (p239-242).
- Caregiver work has substantial indirect costs: lost earnings, productivity, health effects and future social-insurance losses (p241).

**Evidence and implementation**
- Reviews of home-based primary care report less emergency/hospital use and improved satisfaction, but models and target populations differ (p243).
- Transitional care emphasizes coordination, education, medications and follow-up; community paramedicine offers urgent triage or planned postdischarge support (p243).
- Home-based services are most effective when appropriately targeted. Heterogeneity, selection and attrition complicate comparisons (p243-245).
- Cost-effectiveness depends on who receives services and the payment system; meeting previously unmet needs may increase service use (p245).
- Telemedicine can improve access and outcomes in some situations; study methods and comparisons with in-person care vary (p245).
- Broader implementation needs coordinated financing, workforce and technology, while retaining quality and patient-centered goals (p246).
