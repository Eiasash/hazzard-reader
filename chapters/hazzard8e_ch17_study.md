# Chapter 17 Study: Community-Based Long-Term Services and Support, and Home-Based Medical Care

## Summary

**Match home care to need**
- LTSS covers personal, nursing and social support; home-based medical care adds clinician-delivered care. Terminology overlaps (p237).
- Most people needing long-term care prefer remaining in the community; unpaid family and friends provide much of the care (p237, p241).
- Consider function, cognition, chronic illness, goals, caregiver capacity and local services when selecting a model (p237-241).
- Episodic models include skilled home health, transitional care, rehabilitation and hospital at home; longitudinal models include primary care, co-management, integrated medical/social care and PACE (p238-241).
- Day care, assisted living and continuing-care communities offer different mixes of housing, supervision and services (p239-241).

**Delivery and payment**
- Home-based primary care targets complex, often homebound patients; hospital at home substitutes acute hospital-level treatment for selected patients (p238, p243).
- PACE integrates medical, social and functional care for eligible nursing-home-level patients; home palliative/hospice care addresses serious illness and symptoms (p238-239).
- In the United States, funding varies across Medicare, Medicaid, private payment and local resources; coverage of skilled care differs from ongoing personal assistance (p239-242).
- Caregiver work has substantial indirect costs: lost earnings, productivity, health effects and future social-insurance losses (p241).

**Evidence and implementation**
- Reviews of home-based primary care report less emergency/hospital use and improved satisfaction, but models and target populations differ (p243).
- Transitional care emphasizes coordination, education, medications and follow-up; community paramedicine offers urgent triage or planned postdischarge support (p243).
- Home-based services are most effective when appropriately targeted. Heterogeneity, selection and attrition complicate comparisons (p243-245).
- Cost-effectiveness depends on who receives services and the payment system; meeting previously unmet needs may increase service use (p245).
- Telemedicine can improve access and outcomes in some situations; study methods and comparisons with in-person care vary (p245).
- Broader implementation needs coordinated financing, workforce and technology, while retaining quality and patient-centered goals (p246).
