## Choosing a goal for the person in front of you

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-05fcd2cc3d52e4ec75c5cf4b mcq-25bc482e2e15dfa244621080 mcq-dfd19ed053a8fd2e83f6dd49"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-1b9947447ef263b529e44bd0 mcq-5e41e7a32048963dcee0a671 mcq-56a18f5157de81e337b3769d"></span>

**Classify health before choosing a number.** In Hazzard's 2019 Endocrine Society framework, good health means no more than two non-diabetes chronic illnesses, no basic ADL impairment and at most one IADL impairment. Three or more chronic illnesses, early cognitive impairment or at least two IADL impairments suggest intermediate health. An end-stage condition, moderate-to-severe dementia, dependence in at least two basic ADLs or long-term nursing-facility residence each qualifies as poor health. Figure 99-7 shows why birthday alone is insufficient. More than half of those over 75 were relatively healthy by the study's criteria (pp1569–1571).

| Overall health | Without insulin, sulfonylurea or glinide | With one of these hypoglycemia-prone drugs |
|---|---|---|
| Good | A1C below 7.5%, fasting 90–130, bedtime 90–150 | A1C 7.0% to below 7.5%, fasting 90–150, bedtime 100–180 |
| Intermediate | A1C below 8.0%, fasting 90–150, bedtime 100–180 | A1C 7.5% to below 8.0%, fasting 100–150, bedtime 150–180 |
| Poor | A1C below 8.5%, fasting 100–180, bedtime 110–200 | A1C 8.0% to below 8.5%, fasting 100–180, bedtime 150–250 |

Glucose ranges are in mg/dL. The lower A1C boundary in the right column reflects concern about hypoglycemia and mortality, especially with insulin. Ask about actual lows even when the average looks satisfactory. An A1C near 8.5% corresponds to an average glucose around 200 mg/dL. Further relaxation can permit glycosuria, dehydration and impaired wound healing. Shared goals can move in either direction with symptoms, safety and preferences (p1571, Table 99-6).

**Apply the rows.** The independent insulin-treated woman in June 2026 Q25 had an A1C of 7.4% without reported lows. Periodic cognitive assessment is more informative than adding mealtime insulin. The nursing-facility resident taking empagliflozin in Q37 had moderate dementia, needed ADL help and had an A1C of 8.1%. Continuing tolerated therapy fits her priorities. June 2025 Q73 asked for the book's poor-health A1C ceiling without insulin. The older ADA 2020 questions used their own Table 12.1. It lists fasting 90–130, A1C below 7.5% and BP below 140/90 in the healthy group. The fasting ceiling of 150 and BP 140/80 in the credited 2020 Q62 choice do not match that row. In advanced dementia, the ADA 2020 row used fasting 100–180 and A1C below 8.5%. [ADA 2020, Table 12.1](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc20-S012)

**Current clinical distinction.** ADA 2026 advises avoiding reliance on A1C in very complex or poor health. Use glucose patterns to prevent hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia. Its treated BP goal is below 130/80 for most older adults when safely attainable, with a more relaxed target such as below 140/90 for poor health, limited life expectancy or substantial treatment risk. [ADA 2026, Table 13.2 and recommendation 13.9](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S013)

## Diagnosing dysglycemia and deciding whom to screen

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e8ffe5e4732e7d63293662ad"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-90bbae3292be580263244c30"></span>

The three diagnostic tests sample different physiology. Fasting glucose reflects basal glucose regulation, a two-hour value tests recovery after a [75-g load](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/keep-me-healthy/common-conditions/diabetes-type-2/), and A1C averages exposure over preceding weeks. Any one of a confirmed fasting plasma glucose of at least 126 mg/dL after eight hours without calories, a two-hour glucose of at least 200 mg/dL or an A1C of at least 6.5% meets a diabetes criterion. Classic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis with a random glucose of at least 200 mg/dL provide another route. Prediabetes corresponds to fasting 100–125, two-hour 140–199, or A1C 5.7%–6.4% (pp1559–1561, Table 99-1).

**When tests disagree.** Repeat the abnormal test in an asymptomatic patient. Isolated postchallenge hyperglycemia is relatively frequent in older people and predicts complications even with a fasting glucose below 126. Shortened red-cell survival, including hemolysis or recent blood loss, can falsely lower A1C. In December 2021 Q32, random glucose was 184 and A1C 6.2%. That A1C is compatible with prediabetes, and further testing can clarify whether another criterion for diabetes is met (pp1560–1561).

**Repeat testing reflects risk.** Hazzard's cited recommendations screen adults over 65 with fasting glucose and/or A1C. Repeat in two to three years according to circumstances, and consider yearly testing with prediabetes or high risk. Table 99-3 includes BMI at least 25, physical inactivity, a first-degree family history, CVD, hypertension, low HDL or high triglycerides, previous gestational diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, insulin resistance and some antiretroviral treatments. A two-hour test may identify diabetes in a high-risk patient whose initial result lies in the prediabetes range. In September 2024 Q92, age 65 did not end diabetes screening. Its other cited chapters discuss the whisper test, availability of several colorectal screening tests and the importance of a roughly ten-year life expectancy for mammography (pp119, 161, 1561).

## Aging, prevention and an exercise prescription

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-f603c1f000f2b50035e3edce mcq-f705217fdbb945bd5ee28a3c mcq-00447e6d3dddf948ba023322 mcq-2c6e1f31c4d665fa00c72e98 mcq-4521ba287fc28c4115f1d742"></span>

**Fasting versus postchallenge glucose.** Among people without diabetes, fasting glucose rises only slightly with age. The larger change is slower return toward baseline after an oral load. Central adiposity and inactivity account for much of the observed decline in insulin sensitivity. An insulin level similar to that of a younger person can still be inadequate for an older person who is more resistant. Figure 99-4 connects genetics, lifestyle and aging to resistance and impaired secretion. Figure 99-5 compared groups matched for glucose exposure and insulin sensitivity. Insulin secretion was lower in older participants and lowest in those with impaired glucose tolerance. Acute illness can unmask a limited reserve by raising counterregulatory hormones and suppressing insulin release. These distinctions separate the answers to September 2024 Q45 and June 2026 Q49 (pp1563–1565).

**What the prevention trial changed.** Among DPP participants over 60, dietary change plus activity reduced progression to diabetes by more than 70% versus control during three years of active intervention. A 5%–7% weight reduction was sufficient. Figure 99-6 compares incidence within age and treatment groups and shows the relative advantage of lifestyle in older participants. Metformin had less preventive effect with increasing age. Fewer older lifestyle participants developed diabetes over 15 years of follow-up, and onset was delayed by about four years overall. May 2024 Q1 and June 2025 Q44 tested the intervention choice and whether 10% weight loss was required (pp1565–1566).

**Prescribing activity.** The June 2026 question required both volume and spacing. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic exercise weekly on at least three days, with no more than two consecutive inactive days. Add resistance work at least twice weekly and flexibility and balance exercises. Ask what would make activity possible, such as a walking partner or a safe route, and assess foot lesions, neuropathy, cardiovascular symptoms, falls and capacity to respond to hypoglycemia. Shorter or lower-intensity sessions may still improve glucose tolerance in sedentary people. A challenging training program warrants cardiovascular assessment (pp1574–1575, Table 99-9).

## Cognition, self-management and the reason for a low

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-fb7008659f533ecc9b813801 mcq-05fcd2cc3d52e4ec75c5cf4b mcq-120672035ba2e91f922d8ff3 mcq-2e8b0536cb4bfe34d45e5e24"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-eab9b27c6d0193e1d23c6a83 mcq-5e41e7a32048963dcee0a671"></span>

Executive dysfunction can prevent a patient from organizing doses, planning meals or responding to alarms even when conversational ability seems preserved. Ask the patient to demonstrate monitoring and dosing. Screen at diagnosis. The chapter suggests yearly repetition after a borderline screen and every two to three years after a normal screen. A positive screen merits diagnostic assessment. The dated ADA 2020 recommendation used in December 2021 Q93 calls for initial assessment from age 65 and annual screening as appropriate. Diabetes is associated with both vascular dementia and Alzheimer disease. Cognitive impairment can produce poor control and severe lows, and hypoglycemia is itself associated with dementia (p1568 and ADA 2020 recommendation 12.3).

Current ADA guidance calls for cognitive screening at the initial visit and annually in adults aged 65 and older with diabetes. New difficulties with dosing, meals or hypoglycemia recognition also warrant assessment. [ADA 2026, recommendation 13.3](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S013).

**Find the failed defense.** Glucagon and the sympathoadrenal response normally counter falling glucose. Autonomic dysfunction reduces warning, the glucagon and growth-hormone responses can weaken with age, and beta blockade may impair adrenergic defenses. The kidney clears part of an insulin dose, so falling filtration can prolong exposure. Liver failure can limit compensatory hepatic glucose release. Repeated prior lows can lower the threshold for warning until it approaches neuroglycopenia. Avoiding further lows can improve awareness. Check meals, alcohol or sedatives, polypharmacy, cognition, hand strength, hearing, mobility and vision. Poor vision can cause misreading of glucose or an insulin pen. These are different explanations for the December 2021, June 2025 and June 2023 Basic questions (pp1583–1584, Tables 99-14 and 99-15).

**When the mean is misleading.** A patient can have an A1C above goal and still have dangerous fasting lows. Review the pattern, food access and medication administration before intensifying. Remove or reduce the drugs driving hypoglycemia, especially long-acting sulfonylureas or excessive insulin. Pair the change with clear instructions, monitoring and caregiver involvement. Hazzard describes A1C at least twice yearly for patients at goal and more often after therapy changes or when goals are unmet. Capillary readings or CGM reveal excursions an average misses (pp1574, 1579, 1583–1584).

## Kidney disease, drug selection and cardiovascular outcomes

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e3682940da67f696841ee7d2 mcq-2816a676311ac49e2dafcfb5 mcq-2d266bac558345e208370988 mcq-c2279ef45642c96c7994044f mcq-37b56e13ad45cb6e9d57eec9 mcq-538cb2dab0c6ea72540a81e9"></span>

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-04182e576b08982e7e75a1af"></span>

**Albuminuria and filtration answer different questions.** About half of people with diabetes older than 65 have albuminuria, reduced filtration or both. Figure 99-3 shows that combined abnormalities are more frequent in older patients and predict high cardiovascular risk. Assess eGFR and a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and at least annually. Two of three elevated samples across three to six months support persistent albuminuria. Albuminuria predicts progression, cardiovascular disease and response to treatment. A creatinine rise can indicate established damage. An ACE inhibitor or ARB is first-line for hypertension with nephropathy in the book's framework (pp1562–1563, 1566–1567, 1572–1573).

**SGLT2 and GLP-1 affect different outcomes.** SGLT2 inhibition causes urinary glucose loss and natriuresis and can reduce heart-failure events and CKD progression. Ask about orthostasis, volume loss, genital infection and symptoms of rare ketoacidosis, especially with postoperative fasting. Acute renal dysfunction during volume depletion and protection from long-term kidney decline are compatible. Low magnesium was the exception in June 2026 Basic Q147. Selected GLP-1 agonists reduce atherosclerotic cardiovascular events, promote satiety and can cause nausea or weight loss. June 2023 Q68 credited both renal protection with SGLT2 inhibition and a GLP-1 choice phrased as heart-failure symptom relief. Table 99-11 describes reduced cardiovascular events with selected GLP-1 agents, a different outcome from symptom relief (pp1181, 1571, 1577–1580).

**Apply the renal thresholds.** June 2026 Q59 described eGFR 18 despite an A1C of 7%. Stop metformin below 30 mL/min/1.73 m². It primarily suppresses hepatic glucose production and seldom causes hypoglycemia alone, but reduced clearance and poor perfusion increase concern for lactic acidosis. Major acute illness with tissue hypoperfusion warrants withholding it. Stable eGFR of 30–44 requires renal and dose review. In May 2024 Q91, eGFR was 40 with albuminuria and coronary disease. Empagliflozin can improve cardiorenal prognosis. Table 99-11 gives an older SGLT2 avoidance threshold of 25–30 depending on agent, and p1580 urges caution within 30–60. ADA 2026 recommends starting an SGLT2 inhibitor with demonstrated benefit for type 2 diabetes and CKD at eGFR at least 20. Continuing a tolerated agent when eGFR later falls below 20 is a separate decision (pp1576–1580). [ADA 2026, recommendations 11.7a and 11.11a](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S011)

**Other agents.** Sulfonylureas stimulate beta cells. Glyburide's long half-life makes prolonged hypoglycemia a particular concern. Shorter-acting glipizide is still hazardous with missed meals or recurrent lows. DPP-4 inhibitors preserve endogenous incretins, have modest efficacy, low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk and neutral weight effects. Linagliptin needs no renal adjustment, whereas sitagliptin does. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors reduce postmeal peaks but cause flatulence. Thiazolidinediones improve sensitivity but cause edema, heart failure and fractures. Glinides target meal-related secretion and can cause lows. Pramlintide adds injection burden and can compound insulin-related hypoglycemia. Other less-used agents have limited older-adult experience. Consider costs, appetite and complexity before adding another drug (pp1576–1581, Table 99-11).

**Vascular prevention.** For many older adults with diabetes, a statin offers benefit within one to two years, particularly in secondary prevention. Primary-prevention evidence beyond age 75 is less certain. Aspirin has an established secondary-prevention role and bleeding can outweigh primary-prevention benefit. Fasting triglycerides above 500 mg/dL warrant considering fish oil or fenofibrate to reduce pancreatitis risk. Hazzard gives below 140/90 as the BP goal for most, with a possible lower goal after stroke or with progressive CKD if tolerated. Check standing BP. Reducing an SBP of 170 toward 150 can help even when aiming below 120 adds orthostatic burden. In ACCORD-BP the intensive strategy reduced stroke without reducing the primary composite cardiovascular outcome (pp1571–1572, Tables 99-7 and 99-8).

## Complications at presentation and during surveillance

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-c2279ef45642c96c7994044f mcq-2d103bb25f66fd57f375f554 mcq-d3d6c26e0d495cd5b61653e7"></span>

Routine laboratory testing most often finds type 2 diabetes in older adults. Polyuria, thirst and weight loss suggest glycosuria and catabolism, but falls, confusion, incontinence or fatigue may be the first complaint. Because the onset may be years earlier, look for existing eye, kidney, nerve and vascular disease at diagnosis. Figure 99-1 shows that about 27% of US adults aged at least 65 met diabetes criteria in 2013–2016, and nearly half had prediabetes. Figure 99-2 illustrates the substantial absolute burden of vascular complications and hypoglycemia-related emergency visits with age. A given relative risk translates into more events when baseline risk is high (pp1561–1563, 1566–1568).

**Put the neurologic signs together.** Distal symmetric neuropathy reduces protective sensation and balance. Test small fibers with temperature or pinprick, large fibers with a 128-Hz tuning fork, and protective sensation yearly with a 10-g monofilament. Inspect skin, shoes and deformity. An isolated third-nerve palsy can cause diplopia and ptosis. Autonomic neuropathy can cause gastroparesis, altered bowel function, anhidrosis, erectile dysfunction, bladder retention or overflow, orthostasis and resting tachycardia. Pain treatment can include pregabalin, gabapentin or duloxetine, alongside foot protection and fall prevention (pp1567, 1573–1574).

**Distinguish retinal and nerve timing.** Retinopathy can begin up to seven years before recognition of type 2 diabetes. Symptomatic neuropathy can prompt the initial diagnosis, but it has no fixed seven-year lead. June 2025 Basic Q107 credited neuropathy before diagnosis and third-nerve manifestations. Figure 99-8 shows new vessels growing from the optic disc, identifying proliferative disease and a risk of vitreous hemorrhage or tractional detachment. Background disease can show microaneurysms, hemorrhages, exudates and cotton-wool spots. Macular edema can occur at any stage. Center-involved edema with visual deterioration warrants intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. The chapter recommends an annual comprehensive eye examination (pp1563, 1566, 1573).

## Insulin, hyperglycemic crises and care near the end of life

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7df358e819f02b4a8d3dd0d7 mcq-8180581eed958ca16d78940c mcq-ab8aa14b6cb35dc299bb7f20 mcq-404df6b6e3e250f64badb08a mcq-803bd75b3c3a8ecbbb228d97"></span>

**Separate basal coverage from mealtime coverage.** Glargine or degludec supplies background insulin. Lispro, aspart and glulisine cover a meal, while NPH has a discernible peak. Consider insulin in type 2 diabetes with severe symptomatic hyperglycemia or catabolic weight loss. In type 1, basal insulin remains necessary to prevent ketosis. Before prescribing, have the patient or caregiver demonstrate injection and monitoring. Titrate cautiously, particularly with poor renal clearance. Insulin needs may fall as glucotoxicity resolves, so a dose that was appropriate weeks earlier can later cause lows (pp1581–1584, Tables 99-12 and 99-13).

**Two ADA 2022 questions used different insulin decisions.** In June 2023 Q24, untreated symptomatic hyperglycemia, dementia and weight loss favor a simple once-daily basal regimen with caregiver support. In Q70, the patient was at an A1C of 7.0% on basal insulin and three 6-unit mealtime injections. Figure 13.1 allows discontinuation of meal doses of 10 units or less and addition of a noninsulin agent with monitoring. For doses above 10 units, its initial step is to halve the dose while introducing the noninsulin agent. Sitagliptin can replace the small Apidra doses without sacrificing the indications for the statin or empagliflozin. [ADA 2022, Figure 13.1 and Table 13.2](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22-S013)

**HHS versus DKA.** Severe insulin deficiency permits lipolysis, ketone production and metabolic acidosis in DKA. Replace insulin, fluid and electrolyte losses and seek the precipitant. In HHS, extreme glucose-driven diuresis produces hyperosmolality, dehydration, renal insufficiency and neurologic changes, with little ketosis. The June 2026 Basic patient had glucose 850 mg/dL, measured osmolality 393 mOsm/kg, hypernatremia, confusion and barely detectable ketones. Restore volume and correct sodium and osmolality carefully, with monitoring for cardiac overload and treatment of the underlying illness. In the postoperative 2020 patient, marked ketonuria pointed instead toward SGLT2-associated DKA (pp1580, 1585).

**Long-term care and hospice.** Match rapid insulin to food actually eaten if intake is erratic, and ensure the facility can recognize and treat lows. Avoid restrictive diets that aggravate poor intake. Table 99-16 distinguishes relatively stable disease, advanced disease with months remaining, deterioration over weeks and the last days. With months of prognosis, simplify the regimen and liberalize food choices. With weeks, agree on less burdensome targets and avoid long-acting sulfonylureas. In the last days, reduce or stop testing and drugs as appropriate to diabetes type and symptoms. Type 1 still requires some insulin to prevent distressing ketoacidosis (pp1585–1586, Table 99-16).

## Nutrition, education and follow-up

Preserve muscle and function while managing glucose. A healthier older adult with central obesity may benefit from modest caloric restriction combined with exercise. Frailty, weight loss or poor intake favor adequate energy and protein, access to regular meals and coordination with insulin. Ask who buys and prepares food, whether the patient can chew and swallow, and whether depression, finances or transportation limit intake. Dietary supplements have not convincingly improved hyperglycemia or diabetes complications in this context (pp1575–1576, Tables 99-9 and 99-10).

The first visit assesses ADLs and IADLs, cognition, mood, falls, nutrition, blood pressure including standing measurements, feet, vision and renal function. Review medicines that raise glucose or impair recognition of lows. Type 2 predominates in older adults. Type 1 reflects severe insulin deficiency and can persist from youth. Pancreatic destruction, glucocorticoids, hormone excess and prior gestational diabetes indicate other diagnostic or risk pathways. Chronic glycemia damages small vessels, while common vascular risk factors compound large-vessel disease (pp1559–1565, 1567–1568).

Coordinate primary care, endocrinology, nursing education, nutrition, eye and foot care according to complexity. Review actual glucose records, hypoglycemia, medication execution, diet, exercise, interval illness and goals. Disease duration and life expectancy determine how much delayed benefit is plausible. Population quality indicators that count A1C or BP thresholds can miss falls, severe lows, hypotension and patient burden. The individual's functioning and preferences guide the visit (pp1569–1570, 1582–1583, Tables 99-5 and 99-13).

## Summary

**Definition & diagnosis**
- Diabetes: **A1C ≥6.5%**, **FPG ≥126 mg/dL**, or **2-h OGTT ≥200 mg/dL**; any criterion should be **confirmed on a separate day** (Table 99-1 footnote, p1560); unless clear hyperglycemic symptoms, ADA verifies with two tests (p1561).
- Prediabetes: **A1C 5.7–6.4%**, **FPG 100–125 mg/dL**, or **2-h OGTT 140–199 mg/dL** (p1560).
- **~90%** of older adults with diabetes have type 2 disease (p1560).
- GDM affects **~4%** of US pregnancies; **5–10%** of those women have persistent T2D postpartum; **20–50%** develop T2D within **5–10 years** (p1560).
- Screen all adults **>65** with FPG and/or A1C; re-screen every **2–3 years**; consider **annual** testing if high-risk (BMI≥25, first-degree relative, HTN, low HDL/high TG, Table 99-3) (p1561).
- Tests are often discordant; unless there are clear hyperglycemic symptoms, confirm with two diagnostic tests; if two tests are discordant, repeat the test whose result is above the diagnostic threshold (p1561).

**Epidemiology & complications**
- **~27%** of US adults 65+ have diabetes; **~50%** have prediabetes (p1562).
- **~20%** of people meeting diabetes criteria are undiagnosed (p1561).
- New-case incidence peaks at **10/1000/yr** (age 45–64) and **9/1000/yr** (age 65–79), 2017–2018 (p1562).
- CVD/stroke history reported by **28%** (35–64y), **43%** (65–74y), **55%** (75y+) of people with diabetes (p1562).
- Diabetic kidney disease (usually eGFR **<60** and/or albuminuria **>30 mg/g**) present in **~50%** of diabetics >65 (p1562); 5-year ESRD survival **<40%** (p1563).
- Retinopathy causes **~10,000** new US blindness cases/yr and may begin as early as **7 years** before diagnosis in T2D (p1563).
- Neuropathy eventually develops in **~50%** of diabetics (range 10–100% depending on criteria) (p1563).
- Lower-extremity amputation risk **~10-fold** higher than in nondiabetic older adults (p1563).
- US diabetes cost **>$300 billion** (2017); **63%** of inpatient diabetes costs are attributable to age 65+ (p1563).
- Depression: up to **60%** higher incident-T2D risk per meta-analysis; diabetics have **2×** the depression rate of nondiabetics (p1568).

**Aging pathophysiology (brief)**
- Normal aging causes only mild ↑ FPG but a **more pronounced delay** in glucose return-to-normal after an oral glucose challenge (p1563).
- Insulin resistance with aging tracks mainly with **central adiposity** and inactivity, not age per se (p1563–1564).
- β-cell secretory defects are demonstrable in older adults even when matched for insulin sensitivity (p1564).
- T2D genetics: **~70** susceptibility loci identified; **≥75%** concordance in identical twins (p1565).

**Prevention**
- DPP: intensive lifestyle intervention cut progression to T2D by **>70%** in patients **>60** over **3 years** of treatment, despite only **5–7%** weight loss (p1565–1566).
- Metformin slowed progression in younger adults but had somewhat less effect in patients **>60** (p1566).
- Benefit persisted at 15-year follow-up; diabetes onset delayed by **~4 years** (p1566).

**Evaluation**
- Comprehensive geriatric assessment (Table 99-4): functional status (ADLs/IADLs), cognition, depression, falls, vision, frailty (p1567).
- Confirm albuminuria on **2 of 3 samples** elevated over **3–6 months**; old terms: microalbuminuria 30–300 mg/g, macroalbuminuria >300 mg/g (being retired) (p1567).
- Annual **10-g monofilament** + **128-Hz tuning fork** testing for neuropathy, starting at diagnosis (p1567).
- The chapter gives **annual** follow-up after a borderline cognitive screen and **every 2–3 years** after a normal screen (p1568). ADA 2026 recommends an initial screen and **annual** screening from age 65, with additional assessment when self-management changes.

**Management goals — individualized A1C framework (Table 99-6, all p1571)**
- 3 tiers by comorbidity/function/cognition: Good, Intermediate, Poor health; Current practice (not the book): individualize targets (grade C): healthy older adults with few stable illnesses and intact cognition/function should have lower goals such as A1C <7.0-7.5% and/or CGM time in 70-180 mg/dL ≥70% and time ≤70 mg/dL ≤4%; complex/intermediate health prioritizes hypoglycemia avoidance, with less stringent goals such as A1C <8.0%, time in 70-180 mg/dL ≥50% and time <70 mg/dL <1%, considering cognition/function, frailty, comorbidity, medication risk-benefit and preferences; very complex/poor health avoids reliance on A1C and focuses on avoiding hypoglycemia and symptomatic hyperglycemia, with no numerical CGM goal specified; fasting/premeal and bedtime ranges respectively are 80-130/80-180 mg/dL (healthy), 90-150/100-180 (complex/intermediate) and 100-180/110-200 (very complex/poor); lower A1C goals require no recurrent/severe hypoglycemia or undue treatment burden - ADA Standards of Care 2026, Section 13, recommendations 13.7a-c pS282 and Table 13.2 pS284
- Good health: A1C **<7.5%** if not on a hypoglycemia-risk drug; **≥7.0 and <7.5%** if on insulin/sulfonylurea/glinide.
- Intermediate health: **<8%** (not on risk drug) / **≥7.5 and <8.0%** (on risk drug).
- Poor health: **<8.5%** (not on risk drug) / **≥8.0 and <8.5%** (on risk drug).
- A1C–mortality relationship is U-shaped: lowest risk at **6–9%** (p1574).

**CVD risk reduction**
- Hazzard gives a BP goal **below 140/90 mmHg** for most (p1572). ADA 2026 recommends **below 130/80 mmHg** when safely attainable. For poor health, limited life expectancy or high treatment risk, a goal such as below 140/90 may be appropriate. Check standing BP (ADA 2026, recommendation 13.9).
- ACE inhibitor or ARB first-line for hypertension when nephropathy is present (p1572).
- Statins may be indicated for nearly all older diabetics except those with end-stage disease and very limited life expectancy; benefit at age ≥75 without ASCVD is still being studied (p1571).
- If a statin alone doesn't reach the LDL-C goal, add ezetimibe or a PCSK9 inhibitor; if fasting triglycerides **>500 mg/dL**, add fish oil and/or fenofibrate to reduce pancreatitis risk (Table 99-8, p1572).
- Aspirin **75–162 mg/d** recommended for secondary prevention (known CVD); primary prevention remains controversial because of bleeding risk (p1572).
- CVD-outcome-proven drug classes (large CVOTs, average participant age >60): SGLT-2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin) and GLP-1 agonists (liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide) (p1570–1571).

**Microvascular complications**
- Annual eGFR + urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio screening (p1572).
- Annual comprehensive eye exam by an ophthalmologist (p1573).
- First-line agents for diabetic neuropathic pain: **pregabalin, gabapentin, duloxetine** (p1573).

**Drugs — noninsulin (Table 99-11, p1577–1578)**
- **Metformin** / metformin ER: printed dose lines 500–1000 mg BID, max **2500 mg/d**, 500–2000 mg qd — the page does not pair each line to an agent (p1577). Cautions: avoid if **eGFR <30**; avoid in unstable heart failure or during acute hospitalization for major illness that could reduce tissue perfusion (lactic-acidosis risk) (p1576).
- **Sulfonylureas** (e.g., glipizide 2.5–10 mg qd–BID) (p1577). Cautions: hypoglycemia; avoid **glyburide** — long half-life, not recommended in older adults (p1579); avoid in renal/hepatic impairment (p1579); Required reading: see [AGS Beers 2023 required-article card](?chapter=laws&lawCard=ima-article-beers-2023#law-card-ima-article-beers-2023) for qualified sulfonylurea avoidance and the preference if one is used (Table 2, p2061)
- **DPP-4 inhibitors** (e.g., sitagliptin 100 mg qd) (p1577). Cautions: renal dose-adjust (except linagliptin); possible pancreatitis; possible heart-failure hospitalization.
- **GLP-1 agonists** (e.g., liraglutide, semaglutide [also oral], dulaglutide) — injectable (semaglutide also oral), dose lines range from twice daily to once weekly; source table does not pair each dose line to its named agent (p1577–1578). Cautions: nausea/vomiting, possible pancreatitis, avoid exenatide/lixisenatide if **eGFR <30**.
- **SGLT-2 inhibitors** (e.g., empagliflozin 10–25 mg qd) (p1578). Cautions: avoid if **eGFR <25–30** — Table 99-11 (p1578); genital/UTI infections; DKA (rare in T2DM; the volume effect may contribute) (p1578, p1580).
- **Thiazolidinediones** (pioglitazone 15–45 mg qd) (p1578). Cautions: avoid in heart failure (fluid retention), bone-fracture risk.
- **Meglitinides** (repaglinide 0.5–4 mg before meals) (p1578). Cautions: hypoglycemia; avoid combining with gemfibrozil (p1580).

**Drugs — insulin (Table 99-12, p1579; dosing p1581–1582)**
- **Glargine U-100** has no peak and lasts **20 to >24 hours**. **Glargine U-300** also has no peak and lasts **28–36 hours** (Table 99-12, p1579). The chapter gives an initial glargine dose of **10 units once daily** or **0.2 units/kg/day** (p1581).
- **Degludec** (ultra-long): duration **>40 h**.
- **Rapid-acting**: Table 99-12 prints onset **5–15 min**, duration **2–4 h** on the lispro line only (aspart/glulisine cells blank); rapid-acting analogs are given just before meals and can even be given within 20 min after starting a meal.
- Total daily dose: type 1 **~0.5–1.0 units/kg**; type 2 up to **1.0–2.0 units/kg**; start low, **0.1–0.2 units/kg/day**.
- Titrate **2–5 units every 3–5 days** (every 7 days for long-acting analogs).

**Hypoglycemia**
- Diabetics may become symptomatic at **80–100 mg/dL**, vs **50–60 mg/dL** in nondiabetics — the counterregulatory threshold adapts to chronic glycemic exposure (p1583).
- Risk factors: insulin/sulfonylurea use, autonomic/glucagon impairment, renal/hepatic failure, cognitive disorder, sensory (vision/hearing) or functional (mobility/hand dexterity) impairment, poor/irregular nutrition, alcohol/sedative use, polypharmacy (Table 99-15) (p1584).
- β-blockers can interfere with hypoglycemia counterregulation — avoid combining with insulin/sulfonylurea when possible (p1584).

**Special populations**
- Nursing-home diabetes prevalence: **26%** men, **22%** women (p1585).
- LTC residents show better observed survival at A1C **8–9%** (p1585).
- End-of-life glucose target: **108–270 mg/dL (6–15 mmol/L)** (p1586).

**Exam traps / book self-contradictions (flagged in source)**
- The chapter states only insulin and sulfonylureas carry significant hypoglycemia risk, yet its own Table 99-6 and Table 99-11 also name **glinides/meglitinides** as hypoglycemia-risk drugs (p1576).
- Table 99-11 prints the DPP-4 row as "↑ Glucagon secretion", while the GLP-1 row directly below prints "↓" and the text says GLP-1 agonists **suppress** glucagon (p1580); the book does not reconcile this (p1577).
- Table 99-16 is sourced to the 2013 (2nd ed.) Diabetes UK end-of-life recommendations, while the chapter's own text notes they were last updated **November 2021** (p1586).
