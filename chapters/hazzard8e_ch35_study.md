# Chapter 35 Study: Sexuality, Sexual Function, and the Aging Woman

## Summary

**Ask without assumptions**
- Sexuality can remain important with age, illness or absence of a partner; use a biopsychosocial approach that includes partner health and context (p503-504).
- Invite discussion privately and nonjudgmentally; ask about partners, pleasure, concerns and the effect of illness or treatment (p516-517).
- A sexual problem is not automatically a disorder: distress, personal goals and relationship context matter (p508, p516).
- Respect sexual and gender diversity, privacy and consensual relationships in long-term care; cognitive impairment does not itself erase sexuality (p505-507).
- Assess STI exposure and prevention needs, and ask sensitively about trauma, coercion and partner violence (p509-510).

**Evaluate the concern**
- Separate desire/arousal, orgasm and pain/penetration concerns; determine lifelong versus acquired and situational versus generalized patterns (p516-520, p523).
- Review physical and mental health, medicines, surgery, cancer treatment and relationship factors (p515, p518, p521).
- Menopausal tissue changes can contribute to dryness and pain; examination can also educate the patient about anatomy and findings (p513, p521-522).
- PLISSIT progresses from permission and limited information to specific suggestions and intensive therapy when needed (p518).

**Tailor treatment**
- Lubricants reduce friction; moisturizers, treatment of underlying disease, pelvic therapy and graded dilation may help selected women (p522-523).
- Psychotherapy, couples/sex therapy and adapted sexual-skills education can address relevant concerns (p522-523).
- The book lists conjugated estrogen, ospemifene and intravaginal prasterone for postmenopausal sexual symptoms; treatment must match the specific problem (p523).
- Testosterone is used off-label in some women; adverse effects may be irreversible and safety in older women is unestablished. DHEA reviews have not shown efficacy (p523).
- Flibanserin is taken daily orally and bremelanotide by pre-encounter injection; the chapter states neither was approved for postmenopausal women (p523).
- Older women are poorly represented in treatment trials; preserve that uncertainty when discussing benefits and harms (p522-523).

> Current practice (not the book): The U.S. flibanserin indication now includes women younger than 65, including after menopause, with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder causing distress or interpersonal difficulty after other causes are excluded. Dose 100 mg orally at bedtime. Hepatic impairment or moderate/strong CYP3A4 inhibitors are contraindications; alcohol close to dosing increases the risk of syncope. - [FDA prescribing information, 2025](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/022526s013lbl.pdf).

### Localizing pain, dryness and loss of interest

- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause can involve burning, itching and painful penetration together with urgency, dysuria or recurrent urinary infections. Reduced lubrication, higher vaginal pH and fewer lactobacilli accompany menopausal tissue change. Consider irritant dermatitis, infection, a vulvar dermatosis and dysplasia or cancer when findings suggest another cause (p513).
- Ask whether symptoms occur with every partner and type of stimulation and whether function was previously satisfactory. The interest/arousal criteria reproduced in the book include clinically significant personal distress and a duration of approximately **6 months**. An encounter frequency or a partner's concern alone cannot establish the diagnosis (p519).
- Explore pain with permission. A cotton swab may help localize vulvar tenderness, although its specificity has been questioned. Examination can help the patient understand her anatomy; graded dilators can illustrate capacity and help selected patients regain comfort (p521–523).
- If condoms are used, discuss lubrication and technique together. The chapter recommends water- or silicone-based products for this purpose and notes that oil can reduce condom effectiveness. Water-based products can dry quickly and may need to be reapplied (p509).

## Exam traps

- Menopause does not remove the need for an STI risk assessment when exposure is possible (p509–510).
- Pain can secondarily reduce desire. Treating the pain and reassessing interest may clarify the presenting complaint (p523).
- Systemic DHEA supplements and intravaginal prasterone have different evidence and uses (p523).

## Recall drill

**1. Who should be offered an opportunity to raise a sexual concern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Older women regardless of age, partner status, orientation or illness. Invite discussion privately and allow the person to set priorities (p503–504, p516–517).</details>

**2. What distinguishes a sexual difficulty from a diagnosable interest/arousal disorder?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter's criteria include persistent symptoms for approximately <strong>6 months</strong> with clinically significant personal distress and assessment of competing causes (p519).</details>

**3. How do acquired and lifelong symptoms differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Acquired symptoms follow a period of relatively normal function; lifelong symptoms have been present since sexual activity began (p519).</details>

**4. What distinguishes generalized from situational symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Generalized symptoms occur across circumstances; situational symptoms are limited to particular stimulation, partners or settings (p519).</details>

**5. Which genital and urinary symptoms can accompany menopausal tissue change?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dryness, burning, painful penetration or bleeding with intercourse can coexist with dysuria, urgency and recurrent urinary infections (p513).</details>

**6. Which tissue changes help explain these symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Lubrication and vaginal blood flow decline, the pH rises and epithelial glycogen and lactobacilli decrease (p513).</details>

**7. What other explanations should be considered for persistent genital symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Irritant dermatitis, infection, other vulvar skin disease and dysplasia or cancer are among the alternatives (p513).</details>

**8. What are the four steps of PLISSIT?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Permission, limited information, specific suggestions and intensive therapy or referral as needed (p518).</details>

**9. What can a cotton-swab examination contribute to the assessment of pain?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It helps map the location and intensity of vulvar tenderness, although its diagnostic specificity is uncertain (p521).</details>

**10. Which measures can help selected patients with painful penetration?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Lubrication, moisturizers, treatment of the cause, pelvic therapy and graded dilation suited to the patient's goals (p522–523).</details>

**11. What should be discussed when choosing a lubricant to use with a condom?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Water or silicone products reduce friction; oil-based products can reduce condom effectiveness (p509).</details>

**12. How might treating vulvovaginal symptoms improve desire?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Reducing pain may improve interest or arousal by making activity comfortable again (p523).</details>

**13. What cautions does the chapter attach to testosterone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use in women is off-label, some androgenic effects can be irreversible, and safety in older women is uncertain (p523).</details>

**14. How can management of orgasm difficulty vary with its pattern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Clarify lifelong versus acquired and generalized versus situational difficulty; sexual-skills training and attention to relationship strain may help (p519, p523).</details>

**15. Why ask about trauma or coercion even in a long-standing relationship?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Exposure and violence can affect both present safety and sexual function; ask sensitively and privately (p509–510).</details>
