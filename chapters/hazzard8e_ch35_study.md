# Chapter 35 Study: Sexuality, Sexual Function, and the Aging Woman

## Summary

**Ask without assumptions**
- Sexuality can remain important with age, illness or absence of a partner; use a biopsychosocial approach that includes partner health and context (p503-504).
- Invite discussion privately and nonjudgmentally; ask about partners, pleasure, concerns and the effect of illness or treatment (p516-517).
- A sexual problem is not automatically a disorder: distress, personal goals and relationship context matter (p508, p516).
- Respect sexual and gender diversity, privacy and consensual relationships in long-term care; cognitive impairment does not itself erase sexuality (p505-507).
- Assess STI exposure and prevention needs, and ask sensitively about trauma, coercion and partner violence (p509-510).

**Evaluate the concern**
- Separate desire/arousal, orgasm and pain/penetration concerns; determine lifelong versus acquired and situational versus generalized patterns (p516-520, p523).
- Review physical and mental health, medicines, surgery, cancer treatment and relationship factors (p515, p518, p521).
- Menopausal tissue changes can contribute to dryness and pain; examination can also educate the patient about anatomy and findings (p513, p521-522).
- PLISSIT progresses from permission and limited information to specific suggestions and intensive therapy when needed (p518).

**Tailor treatment**
- Lubricants reduce friction; moisturizers, treatment of underlying disease, pelvic therapy and graded dilation may help selected women (p522-523).
- Psychotherapy, couples/sex therapy and adapted sexual-skills education can address relevant concerns (p522-523).
- The book lists conjugated estrogen, ospemifene and intravaginal prasterone for postmenopausal sexual symptoms; treatment must match the specific problem (p523).
- Testosterone is used off-label in some women; adverse effects may be irreversible and safety in older women is unestablished. reviews of systemic DHEA have not shown efficacy (p523).
- Flibanserin is taken daily orally and bremelanotide by pre-encounter injection; the chapter states neither was approved for postmenopausal women (p523).
- Older women are poorly represented in treatment trials; preserve that uncertainty when discussing benefits and harms (p522-523).

> Current practice (not the book): The U.S. flibanserin indication now includes women younger than 65, including after menopause, with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder causing distress or interpersonal difficulty after other causes are excluded. Dose 100 mg orally at bedtime. Hepatic impairment or moderate/strong CYP3A4 inhibitors are contraindications; alcohol close to dosing increases the risk of syncope. - [FDA prescribing information, 2025](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/022526s013lbl.pdf).

### Localizing pain, dryness and loss of interest

- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause can involve burning, itching and painful penetration together with urgency, dysuria or recurrent urinary infections. Reduced lubrication, higher vaginal pH and fewer lactobacilli accompany menopausal tissue change. Consider irritant dermatitis, infection, a vulvar dermatosis and dysplasia or cancer when findings suggest another cause (p513).
- Ask whether symptoms occur with every partner and type of stimulation and whether function was previously satisfactory. The interest/arousal criteria reproduced in the book include clinically significant personal distress and a duration of approximately **6 months**. An encounter frequency or a partner's concern alone cannot establish the diagnosis (p519).
- Explore pain with permission. A cotton swab may help localize vulvar tenderness, although its specificity has been questioned. Examination can help the patient understand her anatomy; graded dilators can illustrate capacity and help selected patients regain comfort (p521–523).
- If condoms are used, discuss lubrication and technique together. The chapter recommends water- or silicone-based products for this purpose and notes that oil can reduce condom effectiveness. Water-based products can dry quickly and may need to be reapplied (p509).

### Three questions: interest, capacity and opportunity

Start by asking what the woman wants help with. Loss of a partner, pain, change in orgasm, lack of privacy and a partner's illness can all reduce the frequency of sexual activity, but they call for different responses. Figure 35-1 places the individual and partner in an interactive biopsychosocial model. For each, consider interest and attitudes, physical capacity and opportunity. Someone who has no partner may be satisfied, may miss intimacy, or may want advice as she begins a new relationship. The clinician cannot infer which is true from age, marital status, appearance or living arrangement (p503–505).

Tables 35-1 and 35-2 present older women's own perspectives. Some described more time, less inhibition and knowing their partner and body better. Others described diminished physical response, a poor body image or the need for secrecy. Barriers in the consultation operated in both directions: patients sometimes waited for the clinician to bring the topic up, while clinicians assumed it was unimportant or that another professional had addressed it. A private invitation followed by permission to decline can break that impasse. The patient's account of what is satisfying or distressing determines whether further evaluation is wanted (p504–506).

### Interpreting rates of partnership and sexual problems

The panels in Figure 35-2 have different denominators. One asks whether people have a spouse or intimate partner; the other asks about recent activity **among those with a partner**. In the US NSHAP survey cited, about **85%** of women aged 57–64 had a partner. By age 85 about **40%** had a partner, and fewer than **20%** of all women were sexually active. The latter percentage cannot be read as the proportion who have lost interest or developed dysfunction. Availability and health of a partner help explain why activity may change even when desire is unchanged (p505, p507).

Figure 35-3 estimates remaining sexually active years by age and self-rated health. In the group studied, better health was associated with an estimated **3–6 additional years** of sexually active life among sexually active women. This is a population comparison using survey data, not an expected duration for an individual after treatment of a specific illness. In another community sample described in the chapter, roughly half of partnered people with cognitive impairment were sexually active, including **40%** of those aged 80–91. Cognitive impairment deserves assessment of consent, privacy and support, but a diagnostic label alone cannot establish a person's preferences (p505–507).

For Table 35-3, retain both the sample and selection criteria. The national survey included **1,550 women**. The table's estimates use responses from women reporting sexual activity in the prior year, with adjustment for selection and nonresponse. In the 65–74 group, the table gives **38%** for lack of interest, **43%** for lubrication difficulty, **33%** for inability to climax and **19%** for pain during intercourse. Women who stopped activity because of a problem could be missed. The table does not establish that every respondent with a symptom had a diagnosable disorder (p508).

Figure 35-8 separates the proportion experiencing each problem from the proportion both experiencing it and bothered by it. The bars are not interchangeable. A person can continue activity despite a problem because intimacy is valuable, or because they do not feel able to refuse. Another may feel no distress about infrequent orgasm but significant distress about dryness. Ask which concern belongs to the patient, what she would consider a satisfactory outcome and whether she feels free to decide. A partner's dissatisfaction by itself is insufficient for a diagnosis of dysfunction in the patient (p508, p519–521).

### Safety, consent and access to a private conversation

Invite the patient to identify partners in her own terms. Ask about new or concurrent relationships and the types of contact relevant to STI exposure; pregnancy prevention is no longer the main reason to discuss barrier protection. Figure 35-4 illustrates external and internal condoms and a dental dam. Previous sexual history, current partners, barrier use, symptoms and testing history provide a more meaningful risk assessment than chronological age. A patient may assume HIV or other STI testing was part of routine blood work. Explain what a particular test would answer and arrange a way for the patient to receive the result privately (p508–510, p516–517).

Care in a residential facility can introduce barriers that were absent at home: lack of privacy, staff assumptions about partnership, or physical dependence on the person who controls access to a room or mobility aid. Ask about wishes and capacity for the particular relationship or activity, and look for coercion or inability to express refusal. Include partners when the patient wants their involvement, while still providing time alone. The chapter also includes intimacy at the end of life: serious illness can change which forms of closeness are possible without removing their importance to the individual or partner (p505–507).

Table 35-4 recommends asking about violence privately, explaining confidentiality and its local reporting limits, and using concrete questions about fear, control and unwanted contact. In a woman with disability, interference with an assistive device, medicine, essential care or access to other people may be especially important. A partner's presence can prevent disclosure even if that partner ordinarily helps with communication. Changes in function, injury or behavior may prompt further inquiry; their presence alone does not identify the cause. Avoid an examination without permission and establish how the patient can safely receive follow-up (p509–510).

### Response, hormones and other body systems

The models in Figure 35-5 answer different questions. A physiologic model describes excitement, plateau, orgasm and resolution, with more than one possible pattern in women. A responsive model also considers receptivity, context, intimacy and whether arousal leads to desire. Someone may not experience spontaneous desire before a welcome intimate encounter and still experience interest, satisfaction and pleasure during it. What matters clinically is change, distress and the woman's own goal. A fixed sequence is a poor screening test for a disorder (p511–512).

Estrogen affects epithelium, blood flow, sensation, pelvic support and the vaginal microenvironment. After natural menopause, or more abruptly after bilateral oophorectomy, local symptoms can include less lubrication and greater tissue fragility. Table 35-5 connects narrower, shorter or less elastic tissue and fewer vaginal folds with dryness, pain or bleeding with intercourse. Reduced epithelial glycogen and lactobacilli accompany a higher vaginal pH. Urgency, dysuria and recurrent urinary infections can coexist with genital symptoms in genitourinary syndrome of menopause. Assess bleeding or a focal lesion for other causes rather than treating all symptoms occurring after menopause as estrogen deficiency (p513).

Figure 35-6 distinguishes central effects on desire and subjective excitement from peripheral effects on genital sensation and blood flow. Changes in estrogen, androgen and neurotransmitter signaling can interact with sleep, mood, stress and medication. A serum androgen value is not a validated stand-alone diagnosis of a woman's sexual concern, and treating tissue pain can improve interest without a direct libido effect. The figure also helps explain why lubrication, perceived arousal and the desire to take part can be discordant. Ask about each in ordinary language before selecting a biological explanation (p513–514, p523).

Pain from arthritis, limited positioning, pelvic floor dysfunction, incontinence or bowel symptoms can interfere with intimacy even when desire is intact. Cardiovascular disease may affect confidence as well as vascular response. In the data discussed, women with hypertension were less likely to be sexually active, but among sexually active women overall problem rates were similar across treated, untreated and nonhypertensive groups. No class of antihypertensives was consistently shown to cause female sexual dysfunction in older women. Review a possible temporal association without telling the patient to stop an effective cardiovascular treatment on that basis alone (p514–515, p521).

With cancer, document baseline function and the treatments likely to affect tissue, nerves, hormones, body image or fatigue. Surgery, pelvic radiation and endocrine therapy have different implications. Following pelvic radiation, the chapter discusses dilation to preserve capacity and access for future examinations. A woman may be reluctant to ask about sexual effects during cancer treatment and may wrongly assume that lack of discussion means there are no options. Ask about the effect on both daily comfort and intimacy, and tailor the intervention to the concern and cancer history (p515–516, p522–523).

### Translating permission into a history

Figure 35-7 illustrates how nondiscrimination statements and inclusive patient material can signal that disclosure is welcome. Table 35-6 sets out a transition from routine geriatric history to asking about satisfaction, concerns, pain, partners, STI exposure and safety. Begin privately, speak directly to the patient and check whether she wants a support person involved. If she has no concern now, leave the door open. If a concern emerges late in a visit, acknowledge it, address urgent safety needs and arrange enough time to give it proper attention (p516–517).

Table 35-7A gives the PLISSIT sequence. Permission legitimizes the discussion, limited information addresses the patient's immediate question, specific suggestions connect a finding to a feasible action, and intensive therapy may require a pelvic therapist or appropriately trained sexual-health clinician. Table 35-7B emphasizes empathy, validation, a willingness to work on the problem, realistic expectations and hope. These approaches can be combined. For a woman who stopped intercourse after painful bleeding, the first appropriate action is to understand and evaluate the bleeding. Suggesting ways to increase desire would miss both her concern and the need for assessment (p518, p521–523).

Use Table 35-10 to distinguish individual and relationship histories. Ask how the problem began, whether function was satisfactory previously, what is still comfortable and whether it occurs in every context. Ask about sleep, control over sexual activity, new medicines, physical changes and earlier attempts to obtain help. With permission, a partner can describe their own symptoms, expectations or uncertainty. Agreement about a goal is especially important if the intervention involves both people. An older couple may value comfortable closeness without wishing to resume the particular activity assumed by the clinician (p516–518, p521).

### What the diagnostic criteria require

Table 35-8 separates interest/arousal, orgasmic and genitopelvic pain/penetration disorders. For interest/arousal disorder, the table requires at least **three** manifestations among reduced interest, thoughts, initiation or receptivity, excitement or pleasure, response to cues and genital or nongenital sensations. Some items specify occurrence in nearly all encounters, approximately **75%–100%**. Symptoms should persist for approximately **6 months**, cause clinically significant personal distress and not be better explained by severe relationship distress, another condition, a substance or medication. Low frequency of sexual activity is not one of those diagnostic thresholds (p519).

In orgasmic disorder, marked delay, infrequency or absence of orgasm, or markedly reduced intensity, occurs on almost all occasions in the relevant context. The duration and personal-distress requirements still apply. Determine whether the problem is lifelong or acquired and generalized or situational; a woman may be able to orgasm in some circumstances and not others. Genitopelvic pain/penetration disorder instead includes difficulty with penetration, pain, marked anticipatory fear or pelvic floor tightening. Pain can secondarily reduce desire, and anticipation of recurrent pain can become a barrier even after the original tissue problem has improved. Evaluate the mechanisms separately (p519–520, p522–523).

Table 35-9 collects possible medication associations rather than validated causal effects for each drug. Examples include anticonvulsants such as carbamazepine and phenytoin, antidepressants including SSRIs and SNRIs, antipsychotic or sedative drugs, opioids, and medicines that alter hormonal signaling such as aromatase inhibitors and tamoxifen. Cardiovascular drugs also appear. Ask whether the symptom preceded treatment, followed a dose change or varied with the underlying illness. Depression can impair desire, successful treatment can improve it, and an SSRI may itself cause a new difficulty. These are distinct possibilities when considering a change with the prescribing clinician (p518, p521, p523).

### Matching treatment to the presenting problem

For superficial pain, localize tenderness, inspect for irritant or inflammatory disease, and evaluate for infection or a concerning lesion when indicated. A carefully explained examination can be educational; proceed with consent and stop if too uncomfortable. The chapter discusses cotton-swab localization while noting questions about specificity. Pelvic floor muscle tenderness, anticipatory tightening, abdominal or back pain and restricted joint movement may justify pelvic physical therapy. Graded dilators are one option when narrowing or guarded penetration is important and the patient wishes to pursue that goal (p521–523).

Table 35-11 answers a common practical question. Lubricant is used at the time of activity to reduce friction. A moisturizer is used routinely, typically **two to three times per week**, to improve ongoing dryness. Water-based lubricants may dry quickly; silicone-based products generally last longer and are compatible with latex, but may be unsuitable with particular silicone devices. Oil can weaken a latex condom. Ask about skin sensitivity and the actual products in use before concluding that lubrication has failed. Neither product addresses unexplained bleeding, coercion, infection or lack of desire from another cause (p509, p522).

For persistent GSM-related dyspareunia the book discusses vaginal estrogen, oral ospemifene and intravaginal prasterone. Match route and indication to the patient's risks and preferences. For the local estrogen schedules to which this chapter directs the reader, the **10-microgram** vaginal estradiol preparation is used daily for two weeks and then twice weekly; the low-dose estradiol ring is replaced every **90 days**. A vaginal ring delivering systemic estrogen is a different product. The concentration of a cream is not the dose applied. Review estrogen-sensitive disease, unexplained bleeding and the potential for different systemic exposure before selecting a preparation (p513, p523; Chapter 36, Table 36-3, p536).

For orgasm concerns, education about normal variation and factors affecting arousal, adjustment for pain or disability, and individual or couples therapy can address a specific problem without adding a drug. For interest/arousal symptoms, the book describes daily oral flibanserin and as-needed injectable bremelanotide in the regulatory context of its publication. The labeled update above gives the revised US eligibility for flibanserin, its bedtime schedule and hypotension/syncope precautions. Testosterone has shown benefit in some selected populations but geriatric safety is uncertain and some adverse effects can be irreversible. Systemic DHEA has not shown a consistent benefit for this purpose; local prasterone is a different intervention. Tibolone has limited supporting evidence and lacked US approval. The chapter also cautions about limited evidence and safety for off-label phosphodiesterase inhibitors in women. Identify whether the goal is relieving pain, restoring comfortable arousal or addressing persistent distress about desire before discussing medication (p513, p522–523).
The treatment table in the next chapter also distinguishes **6.5 mg** of intravaginal prasterone as one insert daily from systemic androgen supplementation (Chapter 36, Table 36-3, p536). At follow-up, ask whether the original activity or examination is now comfortable, whether bleeding persists and whether reduced desire remains a concern independently of pain. The answer determines whether to continue local treatment, reassess the tissue diagnosis or explore relationship, sleep and medication contributors. A treatment that improves one component of the sexual response may leave a different component unchanged (p521–523).

## Exam traps

- Always check the denominator in the partnership graphs: all women and women who currently have a partner answer different questions (p505, p507).
- Reduced spontaneous desire can coexist with a satisfactory responsive pattern. Personal distress and context matter (p511–512, p519).
- Postcoital bleeding may accompany fragile tissue but still warrants assessment for other causes (p513, p521–522).
- A medication's appearance in the list of possible associations does not establish causality in an individual woman (p521).
- Lubricants used at the time of activity and regularly scheduled moisturizers address different aspects of dryness (p522).
- A local hormone treatment for painful GSM is not a general treatment for primary interest/arousal disorder (p513, p523).


- Menopause does not remove the need for an STI risk assessment when exposure is possible (p509–510).
- Pain can secondarily reduce desire. Treating the pain and reassessing interest may clarify the presenting complaint (p523).
- Systemic DHEA supplements and intravaginal prasterone have different evidence and uses (p523).

## Recall drill

**1. Who should be offered an opportunity to raise a sexual concern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Older women regardless of age, partner status, orientation or illness. Invite discussion privately and allow the person to set priorities (p503–504, p516–517).</details>

**2. What distinguishes a sexual difficulty from a diagnosable interest/arousal disorder?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter's criteria include persistent symptoms for approximately <strong>6 months</strong> with clinically significant personal distress and assessment of competing causes (p519).</details>

**3. How do acquired and lifelong symptoms differ?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Acquired symptoms follow a period of relatively normal function; lifelong symptoms have been present since sexual activity began (p519).</details>

**4. What distinguishes generalized from situational symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Generalized symptoms occur across circumstances; situational symptoms are limited to particular stimulation, partners or settings (p519).</details>

**5. Which genital and urinary symptoms can accompany menopausal tissue change?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dryness, burning, painful penetration or bleeding with intercourse can coexist with dysuria, urgency and recurrent urinary infections (p513).</details>

**6. Which tissue changes help explain these symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Lubrication and vaginal blood flow decline, the pH rises and epithelial glycogen and lactobacilli decrease (p513).</details>

**7. What other explanations should be considered for persistent genital symptoms?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Irritant dermatitis, infection, other vulvar skin disease and dysplasia or cancer are among the alternatives (p513).</details>

**8. What are the four steps of PLISSIT?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Permission, limited information, specific suggestions and intensive therapy or referral as needed (p518).</details>

**9. What can a cotton-swab examination contribute to the assessment of pain?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It helps map the location and intensity of vulvar tenderness, although its diagnostic specificity is uncertain (p521).</details>

**10. Which measures can help selected patients with painful penetration?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Lubrication, moisturizers, treatment of the cause, pelvic therapy and graded dilation suited to the patient's goals (p522–523).</details>

**11. What should be discussed when choosing a lubricant to use with a condom?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Water or silicone products reduce friction; oil-based products can reduce condom effectiveness (p509).</details>

**12. How might treating vulvovaginal symptoms improve desire?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Reducing pain may improve interest or arousal by making activity comfortable again (p523).</details>

**13. What cautions does the chapter attach to testosterone?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Use in women is off-label, some androgenic effects can be irreversible, and safety in older women is uncertain (p523).</details>

**14. How can management of orgasm difficulty vary with its pattern?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Clarify lifelong versus acquired and generalized versus situational difficulty; sexual-skills training and attention to relationship strain may help (p519, p523).</details>

**15. Why ask about trauma or coercion even in a long-standing relationship?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Exposure and violence can affect both present safety and sexual function; ask sensitively and privately (p509–510).</details>
