# Chapter 54 Study: Therapeutic Exercise

## Summary

**Why and how exercise helps**
- Older adults retain the capacity to improve aerobic fitness, strength and power; limited reserve may make functional gains especially valuable (p805-807).
- Avoid sedentary behavior and tailor activity to ability, chronic disease, goals and access (p805, p814).
- Functional benefit is nonlinear: a small fitness gain can matter greatly near the threshold needed for a daily task (p807).
- Combine aerobic, resistance and balance work as appropriate rather than treating exercise as a single activity (p805-808, p814).

**Conditions and evidence**
- Exercise improves fitness and cardiovascular risk factors. Hazzard lists unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension or arrhythmias, recent myocardial infarction and severe aortic stenosis among conditions in which exercise is generally contraindicated (p809).

> Current practice (not the book): Distinguish exercise during unstable ischemia from supervised cardiac rehabilitation after stabilization. Refer eligible patients for outpatient cardiac rehabilitation before discharge after ACS; in symptomatic severe high-gradient aortic stenosis (Stage D1), exercise testing is contraindicated because of the risk of severe hemodynamic compromise. [ACC/AHA acute coronary syndrome guideline, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40014670/) · [ACC/AHA valve guideline, exercise testing in AS](https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000923).
- During weight loss, exercise attenuates muscle and bone loss but may not eliminate it (p810).
- Weight-bearing activity may help bone health; fracture-trial evidence has limitations, including potential publication bias (p810-811).
- Physical activity is associated with lower cancer risk; exercise can benefit people living with cancer (p811).
- Cognitive trial results are mixed, with modest positive effects overall and substantial heterogeneity in dementia studies (p812).
- Sleep, mood, Parkinson disease and stroke-related function may also benefit from appropriately selected programs (p811-813).

**Prescribe and progress safely**
- The chapter cites a target of **150 min/week**; if chronic conditions prevent this, remain as active as abilities allow (p814).
- Include balance exercise for people at risk of falls and judge effort relative to the person's fitness (p814).
- Begin with tolerable activity and progress gradually; less intense activity maintained over time can still produce benefits (p814).
- Overuse injuries of soft tissues are the most common exercise injuries. Increase duration and intensity gradually (p813–814).
- In diabetes, watch for hypoglycemia as insulin sensitivity can remain increased for **24 to 48 hours** after vigorous activity; attend to feet and footwear (p813).
- Asymptomatic people planning **moderate-intensity** exercise do not need routine screening before starting. Notify the clinician so activity and medication advice can be individualized (p813–814).

> Current practice (not the book): For an inactive adult without suggestive symptoms or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, ACSM permits starting light or moderate activity without clearance and progressing gradually. Known disease, symptoms and the intended exercise intensity change the clearance decision. [ACSM preparticipation screening update, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26473759/).

### Match the training stimulus to the limiting task

- In the LIFE study of frail adults aged **70–89**, a structured, moderate-intensity activity program reduced mobility disability compared with health education. The reported hazard ratio was **0.75** in participants with lower baseline function and **0.95** in those with moderate impairment. The primary trial found no statistically significant interaction by baseline SPPB (P = 0.19); the overall HR for major mobility disability was 0.82. [Pahor et al., Figure 4, 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1875328). Starting reserve helps explain why equal gains can have unequal effects on daily life (p807).
- With peripheral arterial disease, supervised walking that gradually increases speed and distance can improve how far the patient walks before claudication limits activity. The pain threshold and tolerance help set training intensity (p808).
- In people with stage 1 hypertension the chapter reports reductions of **4–10 mm Hg** systolic and **3–8 mm Hg** diastolic with sufficient aerobic exercise volume. Evidence was less clear for stage 2 or resistant hypertension. These estimates describe groups, not an expected fall for each patient (p809).

> Current practice (not the book): The 2025 US guideline defines stage 1 hypertension as systolic 130–139 or diastolic 80–89 mm Hg and stage 2 as systolic ≥140 or diastolic ≥90. Interpret exercise-trial stage labels using the thresholds in the original study. [AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline, 2025](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001356).
- Mechanical loads that stimulate bone act at the loaded site. Dynamic loading and faster strain rates are more osteogenic than static or slowly applied force. Training a muscle group, improving balance and increasing bone density are distinct objectives when prescribing exercise for fracture risk (p810–811).

### Separate habitual activity, training and the outcome of interest

A person who gardens, shops on foot and uses stairs accumulates physical activity even without attending an exercise class. A therapeutic exercise prescription adds an intended stimulus, progression and outcome. The distinction matters when interpreting the chapter's studies. A cohort comparing the most and least active groups can show an association with survival, while a training trial can ask whether a defined program improves walking capacity or strength. Neither study, by itself, specifies the best exercise dose for every patient with the same diagnosis (p805–807).

Ask what the patient wants to achieve. Walking far enough to visit a friend, getting out of a chair before losing balance, and maintaining activity during cancer treatment are different objectives. A single endurance program may improve aerobic capacity without providing enough practice of balance or adequate resistance to improve chair rise. Conversely, gains in isolated muscle strength may not translate into comfortable community walking if claudication, dyspnea or impaired balance is the chief limitation. Identify the task, observe how it fails and choose the training components that address it (p805–808, p813–814).

Large observational differences in mortality have been reported across activity levels. Interpretation requires attention to baseline health, selection of the comparison group, measurement of activity and the distinction between all-cause mortality and disease-specific outcomes. Someone who becomes inactive because of an illness may already be at higher risk. Such limitations do not negate the association; they explain why a program for an individual should be judged by the outcomes it can reasonably change rather than by promising the largest risk reduction quoted from a cohort (p805–806).

### Aerobic reserve, muscle power and the functional threshold

Peak oxygen consumption depends on delivery of oxygen to active muscle and the ability of that muscle to extract and use it. With age, maximal heart rate declines and peripheral changes, including loss of muscle mass, also matter. Figure 54-1 compares cross-sectional and longitudinal estimates of decline. The longitudinal pattern accelerates with age and exceeds **20% per decade** from the seventies onward in the data described. Comparing older and younger volunteers once is different from observing the same person's change over time (p806).

Lower baseline aerobic capacity does not mean the training response has disappeared. Previously sedentary, healthy older adults in the studies reviewed improved maximal oxygen consumption by **6–30%**, with measurable improvement reported after as little as **3 weeks** in some programs. These figures span different participants and protocols. They are not an expected response for a frail person with unstable cardiac disease. An individual may nevertheless gain useful reserve from a lower starting level even if the absolute maximal capacity remains well below that of a younger adult (p806–807).

Strength is the maximal force a muscle can exert. Power incorporates how rapidly force is developed. The distinction becomes apparent when a patient can slowly straighten the knee against resistance but cannot generate force rapidly enough during a chair rise or an unexpected loss of balance. Both strength and power decline with age, with power tending to fall more rapidly and relating closely to function. Resistance training can improve muscle performance through muscular and neuromuscular adaptations. Assess more than maximal force when the failed daily task requires a rapid response (p806–807).

Figure 54-2 is a curve, not a straight line. When fitness is far above the threshold needed for an everyday task, an additional improvement may be impressive on a physiological test yet produce no visible change in independence. Near the threshold, a modest gain can turn an assisted task into an independent one. At the low end, improvement in strength or balance may first be needed before meaningful endurance activity is possible. The figure explains why the same absolute change can be more valuable to the person with the least reserve (p807).

The LIFE comparison illustrates this interaction. The mobility disability hazard ratios differed by baseline function, with the lower-function group showing a larger relative benefit in the figures cited above. These are comparisons between study groups, not percentages of individual participants who could be promised an improvement. Ask which measure of mobility was used and how impaired the participant was at baseline before applying the result to a patient already walking long distances unaided (p807).

### Cardiovascular disease and blood pressure

Table 54-1 compares evidence across aerobic, resistance and combined exercise by disorder. The columns matter: evidence for one mode cannot automatically be transferred to every other mode or disease. Table 54-2 distinguishes possible cardiovascular mechanisms, including adjustments in oxygen delivery and extraction and changes in metabolic risk factors. A training effect on peak oxygen consumption is not the same as a demonstrated reduction in hospital admissions or cardiovascular death. Use the endpoint appropriate to the patient's question (p808–809).

In stable coronary disease, structured rehabilitation can combine exercise with management of risk factors and education. A patient who asks whether regular walking is helpful after a cardiac event is asking something different from whether they should exercise through new unstable angina. Check stability and symptoms first. Chronic disease can make a supervised or adapted program particularly valuable; an acute unstable state calls for reassessment. The chapter explicitly distinguishes conditions in which exercise is generally contraindicated from the asymptomatic person planning moderate activity who need not undergo routine screening (p808–809, p813–814).

In heart failure, exercise tolerance can be limited by peripheral muscular abnormalities as well as the central circulation. An improvement in exercise capacity or symptoms does not require normalization of the underlying cardiac disease. Select a workload the person can sustain, monitor tolerance and distinguish functional improvement from changes in ejection or survival endpoints. In peripheral arterial disease, the relevant practical measure may be how far the patient can walk before claudication intervenes. A supervised walking program can progressively extend distance and speed with symptoms guiding the challenge (p808–809).

Blood-pressure effects depend on starting pressure and the volume of training. Do not use an expected training effect to justify beginning strenuous activity in someone with uncontrolled pressure or to stop medication without reassessment. Reassess the individual pressure and symptoms as activity, conditioning and medication needs change (p809, p813–814).

### Metabolic disease, weight reduction and skeletal health

Training increases insulin sensitivity and can improve metabolic measures while also helping maintain function. The Diabetes Prevention Program combined moderate activity with an intended weight loss of **7%**, substantially reducing progression to diabetes in older participants. The intervention had both activity and dietary components, so the trial cannot assign its entire effect to one exercise type. For a person already using glucose-lowering treatment, improvements in insulin sensitivity may require practical adjustments to avoid hypoglycemia, including after the exercise session has ended (p810, p813).

For an older person with obesity, assess weight reduction and preservation of capacity separately. Restricting energy intake can reduce fat mass but can also cost muscle and bone. Adding exercise attenuates these losses and can improve strength, fitness and the ability to perform daily tasks. It may not eliminate lean tissue or bone loss completely. Follow function, tolerance and bone risk rather than judging success from scale weight alone. A patient who loses weight but becomes slower rising from a chair has not necessarily attained the intended geriatric outcome (p810).

Bone responds to mechanical loading in a site-specific manner. Studies of physically active people can show differences at the sites that bear the load, but these comparisons cannot fully separate training from prior differences between active and less active people. In postmenopausal women, training trials compared with no-exercise controls report changes in bone mineral density that are often small: the chapter describes average increases generally in the range of **1–3%**, with gains up to 5%. A percentage change in density is not a fracture endpoint. The likelihood and mechanics of falling, muscle strength and balance also affect whether a patient fractures (p810–811).

In the fracture literature, distinguish a trial designed prospectively around fractures from studies in which fractures were secondary outcomes. Some pooled results suggested benefit, but the chapter cautions about publication bias. The practical prescription for someone at high risk should protect against falls while delivering an appropriate loading stimulus. Choose activities in which the person can maintain alignment and stability and adapt the challenge to mobility and prior fractures. A task that loads bone but repeatedly exposes the patient to uncontrolled falls is a poor fit for the intended goal (p810–811, p813–814).

### Cancer, depression, cognition and sleep

Physical activity before cancer and activity after cancer diagnosis address different clinical questions. Observational studies link higher activity with lower incidence of several cancers and, in some cohorts, with better cancer-specific or overall survival. Associations differ by site, activity measurement and the comparison group. A study of incidence cannot determine whether an exercise program treats an existing malignancy. During treatment or survivorship, goals can include maintaining endurance and function despite fatigue or the burden of treatment. Adapt the activity to current health and tolerability rather than merely restating a population prevention target (p811).

Depressed mood can reduce the willingness or energy to initiate activity. Exercise programs may improve depressive symptoms, but the practical challenge is designing one the person can begin and repeat. Ask which activity is accessible and valued, what available support would make participation easier and how a small functional gain might reinforce the next session. Changes in mood, attendance and physical capacity should be assessed separately, since the patient may improve in one domain before another (p811–812).

Cognitive studies differ in whether participants were cognitively intact, at risk of decline or already living with dementia, and in the exercise components and cognitive outcomes used. The chapter reports modest favorable findings overall with substantial heterogeneity. An association between activity and subsequent dementia risk does not establish that beginning a particular program reverses established dementia. In a person with impaired learning or judgment, adapt instruction, supervision and the environment so that the exercise can actually be performed and assess function as well as cognitive-test results (p811–812, p814).

Sleep quality can also change with regular exercise. The studies described assess such outcomes as sleep quality, duration or onset, rather than treating all sleep complaints as a single condition. Consider whether the patient can sustain a daytime activity schedule, how the activity interacts with fatigue and whether a separate sleep disorder requires attention. A person who reports feeling better rested may become more active in daily life even without a dramatic change on a laboratory test. Define which outcome will count as improvement before interpreting the response (p812).

### Parkinson disease and stroke

In Parkinson disease the relevant deficits may include slowness, impaired postural responses, loss of power, reduced walking endurance or difficulty performing complex movements. Aerobic, resistance and balance programs target different components. An improvement in endurance does not guarantee safe turns; stronger leg muscles alone cannot be assumed to correct gait initiation. Identify the activity that limits participation and use repeated functional assessment along with measures of fitness. A program should be difficult enough to stimulate change while retaining the support necessary for someone with postural instability (p812–813).

After stroke, residual weakness, low conditioning and prolonged inactivity can limit walking and community participation. The patient's ability to initiate a program may depend on support, equipment and opportunities to practice. Exercise is relevant to restoring mobility and addressing subsequent vascular risk, but a cohort of stable stroke survivors is different from a patient in an acute unstable phase. Start with the activities the patient can perform and adapt the exercise mode to neurological limitations. Measure whether the gain transfers to a practical goal such as walking a useful distance or managing a transfer (p812–814).

For a stroke survivor who has become inactive, break the desired task into its limitations before labeling the program a failure. If impaired balance makes sustained walking unsafe, a supervised or adapted endurance activity can address deconditioning while balance and strengthening work address the barrier to walking itself. When the person returns, reassess safe distance, fatigue and the amount of assistance required. A gain in training capacity that has not yet translated to outdoor walking may still reveal which remaining limitation needs treatment. The same approach applies in Parkinson disease, where a patient may gain endurance before improving the postural control required for a crowded or unfamiliar environment (p812–814).

### Translating Table 54-3 into an individual prescription

The chapter's endurance target is **150–300 minutes/week** at moderate intensity, **75–150 minutes/week** at vigorous intensity or an equivalent combination. Table 54-3 describes moderate exercise for at least **30 minutes/day** in bouts of at least **10 minutes** and vigorous exercise for **20–30 minutes/day** or more. Distinguish the weekly volume from the workload of a particular session. In a deconditioned patient, a pace that seems easy to a trained clinician may represent a substantial relative effort. Adjust intensity to the participant's fitness, symptoms and goals (p813–814).

> Current practice (not the book): Moderate-to-vigorous activity accumulated in bouts of any duration contributes to the health benefit of total activity. Short bouts can count toward the weekly total. [US Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee scientific report, 2018](https://odphp.health.gov/sites/default/files/2019-09/PAG_Advisory_Committee_Report.pdf).

For strength, the printed table lists **8–10 activities** using the major muscle groups, **8–12 repetitions** at moderate to vigorous intensity on at least **2 days/week**. Flexibility work uses sustained static stretches of major muscle groups at moderate intensity on at least **2 days/week**. These are targets to tailor, not the requirement for a first session after prolonged illness. In a very frail patient, strength or balance work may need to precede sustained walking, simply to make the endurance component feasible and safe (p813–814).

Balance training progressively alters what the person must control. Narrow the base from a two-foot stance to semitandem and tandem positions; add controlled turns or tandem walking to challenge the center of mass dynamically. Heel and toe stands challenge postural muscles. Reducing reliance on visual cues represents another challenge and calls for a safe environment and appropriate support. These exercises answer a different need from achieving a weekly aerobic-minute target and should be explicitly considered in people with mobility limitations or recurrent falls (p813).

 Build duration and intensity gradually, check footwear and the surface used, and allow an activity to be modified if pain or imbalance develops. Review medicines and food intake in relation to the planned activity with the treating team. The chapter gives no general medication dose change that applies to everyone beginning exercise (p813–814).

Finally, make the plan specific enough to follow. Ask where the activity will occur, which days are realistic, who can assist if needed, and how the patient will know when to progress. A patient unable to sustain the population target can still benefit from tolerable activity maintained over time. Record a feasible starting dose and the next functional milestone, such as a longer walk without stopping or the ability to rise more easily. Revisit both the exercise and the obstacle if the patient cannot follow the prescription, rather than interpreting lack of attendance as lack of physiological potential (p814).

## Exam traps

- A patient below the weekly activity target can still benefit from a tolerable program (p805, p814).
- Improved bone mass and fewer falls are distinct from demonstrating a reduction in fractures in trials (p810–811).
- The absence of a routine screening requirement applies to an asymptomatic person planning moderate activity; unstable cardiac symptoms change the decision (p809, p813–814).
