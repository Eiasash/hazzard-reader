# Chapter 54 Study: Therapeutic Exercise

## Summary

**Why and how exercise helps**
- Older adults retain the capacity to improve aerobic fitness, strength and power; limited reserve may make functional gains especially valuable (p805-807).
- Avoid sedentary behavior and tailor activity to ability, chronic disease, goals and access (p805, p814).
- Functional benefit is nonlinear: a small fitness gain can matter greatly near the threshold needed for a daily task (p807).
- Combine aerobic, resistance and balance work as appropriate rather than treating exercise as a single activity (p805-808, p814).

**Conditions and evidence**
- Exercise improves fitness and cardiovascular risk factors. Hazzard lists unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension or arrhythmias, recent myocardial infarction and severe aortic stenosis among conditions in which exercise is generally contraindicated (p809).
- During weight loss, exercise attenuates muscle and bone loss but may not eliminate it (p810).
- Weight-bearing activity may help bone health; fracture-trial evidence has limitations, including potential publication bias (p810-811).
- Physical activity is associated with lower cancer risk; exercise can benefit people living with cancer (p811).
- Cognitive trial results are mixed, with modest positive effects overall and substantial heterogeneity in dementia studies (p812).
- Sleep, mood, Parkinson disease and stroke-related function may also benefit from appropriately selected programs (p811-813).

**Prescribe and progress safely**
- The chapter cites a target of **150 min/week**; if chronic conditions prevent this, remain as active as abilities allow (p814).
- Include balance exercise for people at risk of falls and judge effort relative to the person's fitness (p814).
- Begin with tolerable activity and progress gradually; less intense activity maintained over time can still produce benefits (p814).
- Overuse injuries of soft tissues are the most common exercise injuries. Increase duration and intensity gradually (p813–814).
- In diabetes, watch for hypoglycemia as insulin sensitivity can remain increased for **24 to 48 hours** after vigorous activity; attend to feet and footwear (p813).
- Asymptomatic people planning **moderate-intensity** exercise do not need routine screening before starting. Notify the clinician so activity and medication advice can be individualized (p813–814).

### Match the training stimulus to the limiting task

- In the LIFE study of frail adults aged **70–89**, a structured, moderate-intensity activity program reduced mobility disability compared with health education. The reported hazard ratio was **0.75** in participants with lower baseline function and **0.95** in those with moderate impairment. Starting reserve helps explain why equal gains can have unequal effects on daily life (p807).
- With peripheral arterial disease, supervised walking that gradually increases speed and distance can improve how far the patient walks before claudication limits activity. The pain threshold and tolerance help set training intensity (p808).
- In people with stage 1 hypertension the chapter reports reductions of **4–10 mm Hg** systolic and **3–8 mm Hg** diastolic with sufficient aerobic exercise volume. Evidence was less clear for stage 2 or resistant hypertension. These estimates describe groups, not an expected fall for each patient (p809).
- Mechanical loads that stimulate bone act at the loaded site. Dynamic loading and faster strain rates are more osteogenic than static or slowly applied force. Training a muscle group, improving balance and increasing bone density are distinct objectives when prescribing exercise for fracture risk (p810–811).

## Exam traps

- A patient below the weekly activity target can still benefit from a tolerable program (p805, p814).
- Improved bone mass and fewer falls are distinct from demonstrating a reduction in fractures in trials (p810–811).
- The absence of a routine screening requirement applies to an asymptomatic person planning moderate activity; unstable cardiac symptoms change the decision (p809, p813–814).

## Recall drill

**1. Why may a small increase in strength noticeably change independence?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Near the threshold for a daily task, a modest fitness gain may be enough to perform it without help (p807).</details>

**2. What forms of exercise address different limitations?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Aerobic exercise builds endurance, resistance work targets strength and power, and balance work addresses stability (p805–808, p814).</details>

**3. Which group in LIFE showed the larger reduction in mobility disability?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The group with lower baseline physical function. Its reported hazard ratio was <strong>0.75</strong> versus <strong>0.95</strong> for moderate impairment (p807).</details>

**4. Which walking program does the chapter describe for peripheral arterial disease?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Supervised walking with gradual increases in speed and distance, using claudication symptoms to guide intensity (p808).</details>

**5. What blood-pressure changes were described in stage 1 hypertension?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>About <strong>4–10 mm Hg</strong> systolic and <strong>3–8 mm Hg</strong> diastolic with adequate aerobic training (p809).</details>

**6. Which cardiac conditions should prompt reassessment before prescribing exercise?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Examples include unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension or arrhythmias, recent infarction and severe aortic stenosis (p809).</details>

**7. Why include exercise during weight loss in older adults?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>It helps attenuate muscle and bone loss, although some loss may still occur (p810).</details>

**8. What characterizes loading that stimulates bone formation?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Dynamic, local loading, especially with relatively high forces and fast strain rates (p810).</details>

**9. Why does a change in bone density leave uncertainty about fractures?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Fracture outcomes require separate study, and the chapter describes limitations including possible publication bias (p810–811).</details>

**10. What is known about cognition and exercise in dementia?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Trial results are heterogeneous; pooled effects may be modest and the best exercise dose is uncertain (p812).</details>

**11. What is the usual adult activity target cited here?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter cites <strong>150 minutes per week</strong> of activity at moderate intensity. Adapt this when chronic disease limits capacity (p805, p814).</details>

**12. How should exercise intensity be judged in an older adult with low reserve?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Relative to that person's fitness and symptoms, with gradual increases from a tolerable starting level (p814).</details>

**13. Which exercise injuries are most common?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Soft-tissue overuse injuries. Progress duration and intensity gradually (p813–814).</details>

**14. Why can low blood glucose occur well after vigorous activity?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>Increased insulin sensitivity may persist for <strong>24 to 48 hours</strong>; consider glucose monitoring and review treatment and footwear (p813).</details>

**15. Does an asymptomatic person planning moderate exercise require routine pre-exercise screening?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>The chapter says routine screening is unnecessary in that situation. Clinical advice can tailor the activity and medication plan (p813–814).</details>
