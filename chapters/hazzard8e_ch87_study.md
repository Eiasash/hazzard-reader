## A distended abdomen after surgery

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-e4acb3e9acd387f3ab55774a mcq-98debcd301b2d91929e937da" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>A patient develops a tense, tympanitic abdomen after hip surgery. The film shows a markedly dilated colon. **Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome)** is functional failure of propulsion and can accompany surgery, infection and other severe illnesses. Hazzard describes a cecal diameter of **10 cm or more** in this presentation. The measurement helps judge distension and follow progression. A single diameter cannot rule out a mechanical lesion, ischemia or perforation (pp1310–1311, 1361–1362).

Check potassium and magnesium. **Hypokalemia impairs neuromuscular function** and reduces the effective cholinergic stimulus to intestinal smooth muscle. The June 2023 question asked which chemistry abnormality to expect. The September 2024 question used the same postoperative radiographic pattern to ask for the diagnosis. A volvulus twists the bowel around its mesentery and can require urgent detorsion. A stercoral ulcer is pressure necrosis beneath a hard fecaloma. Sudden pain and peritonitis from perforation require urgent surgical treatment (pp1361–1362).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-811bbe2e4f19b983c1f44c2d" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Treat uncomplicated acute colonic dilatation in stages.** Hazzard starts with bowel rest, flatus-tube decompression, correction of potassium and magnesium disturbances, and treatment of the precipitant. Persistent dilatation may warrant neostigmine if cardiac contraindications are absent, then endoscopic decompression if necessary. PEG after resolution can reduce recurrence (p1362).
> Current practice (not the book): ASGE advises monitored neostigmine with atropine available, and endoscopic decompression when pharmacologic treatment is contraindicated or unsuccessful. [ASGE guideline, pp230–232 and Figure 1](https://www.asge.org/docs/default-source/guidelines/american-society-for-gastrointestinal-endoscopy-guideline-on-the-role-of-endoscopy-in-the-management-of-acute-colonic-pseudo-obstruction-and-colonic-volvulus-2020-february-gie.pdf).

June 2022 tested the order of treatment. June 2025 supplied severe hypokalemia and a rectal tube, then asked for the next drug. The accepted option names **neostigmine** followed by the brand name **Mestinon**. In Israel the Mestinon tablet contains pyridostigmine. These are two different active ingredients. [Israeli Mestinon leaflet, October 2024, section 1](https://mohpublic.z6.web.core.windows.net/IsraelDrugs/Rishum01_19_681206424.pdf).

## When watery leakage means the rectum is full

In an older adult with dementia, several small episodes of watery soiling, poor appetite and abdominal discomfort can be the presenting signs of impaction. Ask the caregiver about the last satisfactory evacuation and the use of laxatives or antidiarrheals. Examine the abdomen and rectum. Liquid stool or mucus can pass around a hard mass, and even soft stool can accumulate when the outlet will not empty. **The presence of stool on digital examination can establish distal retention, but an empty rectum leaves the proximal colon unexamined** (pp1361–1365).

A large firm rectal plug calls for gentle manual evacuation before inserting an enema or suppository, with local anesthetic gel if needed. Assess the extent of remaining loading and whether obstruction is present before prescribing oral clearance. Renal impairment makes repeated phosphate enemas hazardous. After clearance, a regular regimen and reliable assistance to the toilet help prevent another episode. A nursing-home intervention that cleared the rectum and then maintained evacuation with lactulose resolved overflow in 94% of adherent participants, a result tied to that population and regimen (pp1361, 1376, 1378–1379).

## Which laxative fits the problem?

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0b3a1e91eb40ee076419243b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Evidence quality and treatment sequence answer different questions.** The comparison of trials on p1372 assigned level one to PEG in adults, meaning consistent findings from well-conducted studies, and level two to psyllium and lactulose. Evidence specific to frail older residents was less extensive. For an ambulant person who drinks well and has uncomplicated constipation, Table 87-11 begins with psyllium and fluids. For a bedridden patient at risk of recurrent impaction it starts with a stimulant and an osmotic agent, then uses PEG if symptoms persist (pp1372, 1378).

> Current practice (not the book): For adults with chronic idiopathic constipation, AGA/ACG strongly recommends polyethylene glycol and conditionally suggests fiber, particularly psyllium. Fluid tolerance and the risk of impaction still guide selection in frail older patients. [AGA/ACG constipation guideline, 2023](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/pharmacological-management-of-chronic-idiopathic-constipation-cic/).

**Match the agent to its mechanism.** Lactulose is a nonabsorbed disaccharide. Colonic bacteria ferment it, leaving an osmotic load that retains water and may cause gas and cramps. PEG retains water without depending on bacterial activation. Senna stimulates myenteric activity and typically acts in eight to twelve hours, making bedtime administration practical. Bisacodyl is activated by intestinal brush-border enzymes. Excessive softening can aggravate leakage in a patient with a weak anal sphincter (pp1372–1376).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-0b0784b75b4373eb612a632d mcq-9ae66d685bd288df1e1dae42" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Match the agent to its harm.** Aspiration of oral mineral oil may cause lipoid pneumonia, especially when swallowing or airway protection is impaired. Mineral oil can also reduce absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. Senna and related anthraquinones can cause melanosis coli. A phosphate enema can cause **high serum phosphate**, especially in kidney disease. Psyllium can cause gas and bloating. Fermentable sugars may taste sweet and produce cramps. Bisacodyl may cause cramps, and a daily suppository can cause rectal burning (Table 87-9, pp1373–1374). The May 2024 paper gives “Table 87-4, p1373.” The senna row is on p1374 in the continuation of Table 87-9.

When an opioid is necessary, assess for impaction, start an effective laxative and reassess. Enteric mu-receptor activation slows contraction, increases colonic fluid absorption and raises anal sphincter tone. Refractory opioid-induced constipation can respond to peripheral mu-receptor antagonism. Neurologic disease and reduced mobility warrant active follow-up after clearance. Docusate has little laxative action, and magnesium hydroxide can accumulate in renal impairment (pp1376–1378).

## Distinguishing risk factors from normal aging

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-da57058ee45d9336a52750e4 mcq-c35e64bb9f1ba0dc91fc878d" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>A medication review is part of the examination. Antipsychotics with antimuscarinic effects and tricyclic antidepressants interfere with cholinergic contraction. Verapamil inhibits lower-bowel smooth-muscle activity. Calcium supplements and oral iron can also aggravate constipation. In a bedbound patient with poor intake, high TSH, low ferritin and fecal loading, review the indication for quetiapine and taper it if appropriate. Iron deficiency and thyroid under-replacement require attention in their own right. Adding oral iron or reducing thyroid replacement would not relieve the current stool burden (Table 87-5, p1359).

The coffee question asked about a physiologic effect. In healthy young volunteers, black coffee increased rectosigmoid activity within four minutes. This observation cannot predict whether coffee will relieve constipation in a frail older patient. The rapid response differs from the sustained inhibitory effects of calcium-channel blockade or antimuscarinic drugs (pp1359–1360).

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-4e388b7f6e0adb26bcb83894 mcq-6830feff40ac5bbc0903e834" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Healthy aging generally preserves weekly bowel frequency. In symptomatic individuals, increased left-colonic collagen deposition may alter compliance, and reduced inhibitory input to circular muscle can impair coordinated propulsion. Loss of enteric neurons producing acetylcholine or nitric oxide is a proposed contributor to slow transit. Longer transit removes water, shrinks the stool and reduces the distending stimulus to further propulsion. Do not infer from these mechanisms that constipation is inevitable with aging. The frequency statement on p1356 differs from the explicit epidemiology on p1355 (pp1355–1357).

## A patient strains for an hour but does not leak

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-a106c2e93b7ced5d12e16004" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>**Rectal dysmotility** can produce a compliant, dilated rectum that retains hard or soft stool. Reduced sensation blunts the urge to empty, and reduced sacral parasympathetic output may reduce rectal contraction. **Pelvic floor dyssynergia** impairs the push itself. Normally the abdomen generates pressure as the external sphincter and puborectalis relax. An inadequate push or paradoxical outlet contraction leaves a feeling of incomplete evacuation despite prolonged straining (pp1357–1358, Figure 87-2).

June 2026 accepted both reduced parasympathetic influence and failure of coordinated abdominal, rectoanal and pelvic-floor activity. The absence of urgency, stains and leakage supports an evacuation complaint, but the history alone cannot identify a single physiological cause. Examine tone and relaxation during an attempted push. When persistent symptoms warrant it, balloon expulsion and manometry help define the problem. Biofeedback trains coordination when the patient can understand, retain and practice the maneuvers (pp1364–1365, 1368, 1370–1371).

## Summary

### Definition & Epidemiology
- **Functional constipation.** Table 87-1 prints at least two features on **>25% of occasions for ≥12 weeks in the last 12 months**, including **≤2 bowel movements/week**, straining, hard stool, incomplete evacuation, blockage or manual maneuvers (p1354).
  > Current practice (not the book): Rome IV specifies **<3 spontaneous bowel movements/week** for the frequency item, criteria met over the **last 3 months** with onset **≥6 months** earlier, rare loose stools without laxatives and insufficient IBS criteria. [Rome Foundation, C2](https://theromefoundation.org/rome-iv/rome-iv-criteria/).
- North American prevalence **12-19%**; an estimated **63 million** people in North America meet Rome II criteria for constipation (p1354).
- Self-reported constipation in adults 65+: **26%** women, **16%** men; rises to **34%/26%** in the 84+ group (p1354).
- Normal aging does NOT reduce bowel movement frequency: only **1-7%** of any age group report ≤2 BM/wk. Among older adults who self-report "constipation," **<10%** actually have ≤2 BM/wk and **>50%** move bowels daily — straining (**65%**) and hard stool (**39%**) drive the complaint, not frequency; **21%** of community-dwelling people 65+ have rectal outlet delay (p1355).
- New-onset constipation incidence in nursing homes screened every 3 months: **7%** (p1355).
- LTC prevalence is far higher than community: a Finnish study found chronic constipation/rectal outlet delay in **79%/81%** (women/men) of nursing home residents; **50-74%** of LTC residents use daily laxatives (p1355).

### Pathophysiology
- Normal total gut transit <**72 h**; older adults with chronic constipation average **4-9 days**, extending to **6 days to >14 days** if bedridden/institutionalized (p1356).
- Slowed transit reduces stool water content (normally **75%**), shrinking fecal bulk and further blunting propulsive activity — a self-worsening cycle (p1356).
- **37%** of non-constipated older adults were unable to evacuate a small solid sphere in simulated defecation studies (p1357).
- Table 87-4 age-related mechanisms of chronic constipation: increased colonic collagen deposition, reduced inhibitory nerve input to circular muscle, and increased plasma-endorphin binding to gut receptors (p1356).

### Risk Factors
- Polypharmacy (≥**5 medications**) is an established risk factor (p1359); nursing home residents average **6-9 meds/day** (p1358). Anticholinergic drugs are the only medication class with level [1] evidence in Table 87-5 (p1359).
- **Iron sulfate** >**325 mg/day**: does not substantially increase iron absorption in older people and may significantly increase GI side effects (p1359).
- Calcium supplementation has a real but modest effect: **13%** vs **9.1%** placebo over a 5-year trial (p1359).
- Parkinson disease: **59%** meet Rome criteria (vs **21%** in age-matched controls); pelvic dyssynergia affects **60%** of PD patients (p1360).
- Stroke: **60%** of patients are constipated while undergoing rehabilitation (p1360).
- Diabetes: colonic transit prolongs to **200 ± 144 h** in immobile diabetics. Metoclopramide may treat diabetic gastroparesis. The book advises avoiding **continuous metoclopramide for >12 weeks** and encourages a **two-week drug holiday** or a tolerated dose reduction, with tight glucose control when clinically feasible (p1361).
  > Current practice (not the book): Tardive dyskinesia risk rises with total exposure. For diabetic gastroparesis, US prescribing information advises avoiding more than **12 weeks of total treatment**. A drug holiday does not reset cumulative exposure. [Reglan prescribing information, Boxed Warning](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=de55c133-eb08-4a35-91a2-5dc093027397)
- In one adjusted study, reporting <3 BM/week was associated with >**2-fold** colon cancer risk — constipation itself is not an indication for colonoscopy, but a genuine change in bowel habit should prompt workup (p1361).

### Complications
- Overflow fecal incontinence in frail nursing home residents: enemas-until-clear then lactulose achieved **94%** resolution — yet only **4%** of these residents are referred for further assessment (p1361).
- Fecal impaction was the primary reason for **27%** of admissions in one UK acute geriatric unit survey (p1362).
- Constipation → urinary retention: constipation raised the risk of retention (PVR >**100 mL**) **4-fold** in hospitalized women 65+; treating the constipation dropped mean PVR from **85→30 mL** over 4 months (p1362).
- Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome): cecal diameter ≥**10 cm** on x-ray (p1362).
- Diverticular disease affects **30-60%** of adults over 60 (p1363).

### Evaluation
- Digital rectal exam is required in everyone reporting constipation; an empty rectum on exam does NOT exclude the diagnosis (p1364).
- Normal perineal descent is <**4 cm** (p1365). Plain x-ray: colon diameter >**6.5 cm** without acute obstruction points toward megacolon; rectal dilatation >**4 cm** implies dysmotility (p1365).
- Colonic transit marker study: normal clears to ≤**5 of 24** markers by day **5**; balloon expulsion normal range **1-5 min** (p1368).
- Colonoscopy is NOT indicated for constipation alone — in 400 colonoscopies in octogenarians, cancer detection was only **2%** for symptoms other than bleeding (eg, constipation, abdominal pain) vs **12%** for bleeding (p1368).

### Nonpharmacologic Management
- Fiber target **20-35 g/day**; start low at **3-4 g/day** and titrate up to limit bloating (p1370).
- Fluid **1.5-2 L/day** significantly enhanced the effect of added fiber in an RCT in adults aged 18-50; often not practical or acceptable for frail older patients (p1370).
- For dyssynergic defecation, biofeedback improved symptoms in **70% vs 23%** with diazepam at 3 months; in a separate comparison, **80% vs 22%** improved with biofeedback versus laxatives at both 6 and 12 months (p1371).

### Drugs (name — dose — key cautions)
- **Senna** — 1-3 tabs at bedtime if symptoms persist after bulk laxatives in ambulant older people (Table 87-11, p1378); onset **8-12 h** (p1372); cheap, safe first-line stimulant.
- **Bisacodyl** — **5–10 mg** by mouth up to **3 times/wk** (Table 87-9, p1373); **10 mg daily** was the trial regimen in outpatients with a mean age of **62** (p1375); caution: bisacodyl used for colonoscopy prep may raise ischemic colitis risk in patients with cardiovascular disease (p1368).
- **Psyllium/bulk agents** — 1-3×/day with fluids; adds **1.4** BM/wk vs placebo (p1375); caution: impaction risk if fluid intake is poor.
- **Lactulose/sorbitol** — 15–30 mL once or twice daily (Table 87-9, p1373); adds **1.9** BM/wk (p1376); sorbitol equally effective and cheaper.
- **PEG** — more potent hyperosmolar laxative than lactulose; a trial of PEG plus electrolytes for disimpaction at **1 L (8 sachets)/day × 3 days** achieved **89%** success (p1376); caution: liquid stools.
- **Magnesium salts** — avoid magnesium hydroxide in CKD; magnesium citrate not recommended in older adults at all (pseudo-obstruction risk) (p1376).
- **Docusate** — avoid: little or no laxative action, even at **300 mg/day** (p1376), despite being the predominantly prescribed agent in US nursing homes (p1372).
- **Glycerin suppository** — first-line for rectal outlet delay; **bisacodyl suppository** second-line; onset **5-45 min** (p1377).
- **Lubiprostone 24 mcg BID**; **linaclotide**; **plecanatide** (placebo-controlled trials showed efficacy at 3 and 6 mg — trial doses) — secretagogues; effective but costly, with limited older-adult trial data (p1377).
- **Prucalopride** — 5-HT4 agonist; unlike older 5-HT4 agents (cisapride, tegaserod), not associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (p1377).
- **PAMORAs for opioid-induced constipation.** Hazzard reports **>3 complete spontaneous bowel movements (CSBMs)** for naldemedine (**52% vs 35%**) and **≥3 CSBMs/week** for naloxegol (**42% vs 29%**) (p1378).
  > Current practice (not the book): The pivotal trials measured spontaneous bowel movements, without requiring a sense of complete evacuation. Responders had at least three per week and at least one more than baseline for nine of twelve weeks, including three of the last four. [COMPOSE trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28576452/) · [KODIAC trials](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1310246)
- Table 87-9 laxative mechanisms: sodium picosulfate is hydrolyzed by colonic bacteria, bisacodyl by intestinal/colonic brush-border enzymes — both to the same active metabolite (antiabsorptive/secretory, prokinetic); the table describes lactulose and sorbitol together as “Nonabsorbable disaccharides” fermented by colonic bacteria *[sic — lactulose is a disaccharide; sorbitol is a sugar alcohol]*; PEG osmotically increases intraluminal fluids; senna/anthraquinones alter electrolyte transport via increased intraluminal fluids, stimulate the myenteric plexus, and increase motility (p1373–1374).

*The June 2025 pseudo-obstruction option pairs neostigmine with the brand name of a different drug (see Israeli context above).*
