## Localizing acute kidney injury and deciding when dialysis is urgent

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7f71419177e54d446b41de5d mcq-37ef49b08af5012046c10790" data-practice-ids="mcq-c204d57269eb58c03fc2a81d mcq-6c8fdecc2d703bfc5a14daf7 mcq-a0d3ff23f69690ed51088758 mcq-274b9aac5f85ca97943ab06b" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

An older adult who has taken a diuretic during several days of diarrhea may have a filtration problem before there is structural kidney injury. Ask separately how much fluid the patient has lost and how much blood is actually reaching the kidneys. Severe liver disease can redistribute fluid and lower effective circulating volume. Low cardiac output can do the same even in someone with leg edema and wet lungs. These are the reasons the cirrhosis vignette points to a prerenal mechanism (p1281–1282).

For an intrinsic lesion, look for evidence of damage within the parenchyma. Hemolytic–uremic syndrome injures small vessels, prolonged seizures can release myoglobin that injures tubules, and pyelonephritis involves the renal interstitium. For postrenal AKI, look for a distended bladder, prostatic obstruction or severe fecal impaction. Unilateral obstruction seldom markedly elevates creatinine when the other kidney works normally. **In May 2024 Q22 both acute heart failure from malignant hypertension and HUS receive credit.** Table 83-1 actually lists the former under prerenal cardiac failure and HUS under intrinsic injury (p1281–1282).

Check the previous creatinine, course of urine output, medications, urine sediment and the possibility of obstruction. NSAIDs reduce the kidney's ability to maintain inflow during volume depletion. ACE inhibitors and ARBs reduce pressure on the outflow side of the glomerulus. With poor intake, an SGLT2 inhibitor's osmotic diuresis may add to fluid loss despite its long-term kidney benefits. Escitalopram, the exception in June 2022 Q3, more characteristically raises concern for hyponatremia (p1279–1283 and empagliflozin and escitalopram prescribing information).

Give isotonic fluid for a demonstrable volume deficit and reassess respiration, circulation and urine output. Drain bladder outlet obstruction with a catheter. In December 2021 Q21, pressure improved after fluids, but oliguria persisted, oxygen saturation fell to 88% and pulmonary edema appeared. The worsening complication was fluid overload. Potassium remained dangerously high and required treatment even though its trajectory and the acidosis had improved. Life-threatening, medically refractory fluid, potassium or acid–base abnormalities and symptomatic uremia are reasons for urgent dialysis. Creatinine alone cannot make that decision (p1283).

## Nephrotic-range protein loss and diabetic kidney disease

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-8d91e921a4f3e6b38cc30ba0" data-practice-ids="mcq-90507ce1829dae10f6d7ca4a" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Think of heavy proteinuria when an older patient's edema is accompanied by low albumin. Urinalysis can redirect an assessment initially focused on heart failure. In the chapter, nephrotic syndrome includes more than 3 g of urinary protein per day, low albumin, edema and dyslipidemia. Renal vein thrombosis can be a presenting complication. Measure renal function and protein loss and consider nephrology assessment for the underlying glomerular disorder (p1285).

For May 2024 Q49, **diabetic nephropathy** is the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in the general US adult population. In older adults who actually undergo biopsy for isolated nephrotic syndrome, membranous disease accounts for about 30–40% and minimal change disease for about 15%. Typical diabetic nephropathy is often diagnosed without biopsy. A rapid fall in filtration, unusually early heavy proteinuria or absence of expected diabetic microvascular disease should prompt consideration of another disorder. This selection explains why the most common clinical cause and most common biopsy finding differ (p1285–1286).

## Itch, mineral disease and nutrition during dialysis

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-eb4176cd70d5d177aafe8cc6 mcq-34b4e39adec6e653936a4380 mcq-5cc3bcb961bbfb9897e8af66 mcq-56a930970c2bcf04fdd39deb" data-practice-ids="mcq-19200b160f41788c44c397e6" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

An older patient may scratch through the night despite regular hemodialysis. Examine for dry skin or a drug eruption, review dialysis adequacy and phosphate control, correct iron deficiency when present, and protect and moisturize the skin. Low-dose gabapentin can help persistent uremic itch. Reassess for sedation and impaired balance in someone already at risk of falling. Sedating anticholinergic antihistamines add cognitive and functional burden. Reducing dialysis frequency or increasing an analgesic opioid does not address the renal mechanism (p1296).

Phosphate retention and reduced production of active vitamin D stimulate PTH release. Older dialysis patients experience more bone pain and pathological fractures, and older women may have less bone mineral than younger women with similar dialysis duration. These observations do not imply greater hyperphosphatemia or more severe renal osteodystrophy at older ages. Active vitamin D concentrations showed no correlation with age in the group described. Osteoporosis can coexist with renal osteodystrophy. A history of falls alone does not identify the bone disorder or establish an indication for alendronate (p1295).

For new loss of function, ask who buys food, who prepares it, whether the patient can eat it, and whether nausea followed a change in dialysis. A list of sodium, phosphate, potassium and fluid restrictions may leave very little the person will actually eat. Follow weight trends, dietary intake and albumin together. Low albumin by itself is insufficient to diagnose malnutrition. A renal dietitian can find acceptable substitutions while protecting energy and protein intake. Look for weakness, falls, sarcopenia, cognitive change and depression as contributors to declining independence (p1291, 1295–1296).

## Starting, trialing or withdrawing dialysis

<span data-exam-ids="mcq-7bd5c333fb742a1192c41f33" data-practice-ids="mcq-e817bdda20dbd2e4a2124856 mcq-40b47298ce126cf107bce372 mcq-a06aada7d4008649524d7b5e" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

In a patient with advanced kidney disease, anorexia, fatigue and nausea are familiar manifestations of uremia. Behavioral change, new confusion, unexplained heart failure or loss of function may be the presenting clue in an older adult. Establish the previous cognitive and functional baseline and assess other reversible contributors. A practice case pairs new aggression with an eGFR of 22: stop metformin below 30 mL/min/1.73 m² and evaluate the behavior on its own merits (p1291 and Chapter 99 p1577, Table 99-11).

Kidney failure is defined by a filtration threshold, while the decision to start dialysis also depends on symptoms, difficult-to-control complications and the patient's goals. When advanced dementia and uremic delirium are hard to distinguish, agree on the purpose and duration of a trial. The chapter allows 3–4 weeks for uremic cognitive symptoms to clear and describes a planned 4–6 week treatment trial with explicit goals. Decide how the team will assess comfort, cognition or a hoped-for activity before treatment starts (p1291, 1293–1294).

In June 2023 Q39, a cognitively intact man already receiving hemodialysis asks to stop. Begin by asking what has become intolerable. Explore depression, pain, transport burden or loss of independence and assess understanding of the likely consequences. Document decision-making capacity, involve people the patient wants present and arrange symptom and end-of-life care if he chooses withdrawal. Frailty, nursing-home residence, albumin and the clinician's estimate of survival can inform this discussion, but they cannot tell the patient how to value treatment time, function or independence (p1296–1297).

## Recognizing CKD and slowing progression

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-dbca0ec187cc977ffcb45a5e" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

A single abnormal creatinine cannot establish chronicity. Compare previous values and look for albuminuria, blood or casts on urine testing and structural changes on imaging persisting for more than three months. Albuminuria and reduced filtration each convey prognostic information. In the US estimate quoted, 47% of adults over 70 met CKD criteria, compared with about 11.5% in all adults. Most were identified by filtration or albuminuria, long before symptoms of kidney failure (p1277–1279).

Review blood pressure, cardiovascular risk, diabetes control, medication doses, nephrotoxic exposures and episodes of AKI. In proteinuric disease, a single ACE inhibitor or ARB can lower pressure and protein loss. Combining them increases complications. In diabetes, consider kidney-protective therapy alongside glucose control. Weigh dietary protein restriction against frailty and unintentional weight loss. If phosphate remains elevated despite dietary changes, use a binder with meals. Correct low vitamin D when present and assess persistent secondary hyperparathyroidism separately. Progressive decline, significant albuminuria, red-cell casts, persistent potassium abnormalities or difficult hypertension are reasons for earlier nephrology involvement and planning (p1279–1280, 1286, 1290).

## Glomerular, interstitial and renovascular disease

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-aa7281edda3f5115f5e9031d mcq-7c9f2acf34dd73d71de69a94" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Hematuria and proteinuria with rapidly rising creatinine should raise concern for rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Ask about recent infection, systemic symptoms, rash and hemoptysis. In older adults, postinfectious GN and small-vessel vasculitis can present with nonspecific fatigue or malaise. Urgent nephrology assessment can guide serology and biopsy. Frailty, bleeding tendency and small echogenic kidneys affect the risk and likely benefit of biopsy. Treatment depends on the underlying lesion, the urgency of preserving kidney function and the risks of immunosuppression (p1283–1285).

Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis may follow a new antibiotic, proton pump inhibitor, NSAID or diuretic. A thorough exposure history matters when the presentation is simply a rise in creatinine. Withdraw the suspected drug promptly and follow recovery. For persistent or uncertain cases, biopsy and corticosteroid treatment require judgment about diagnostic certainty, severity and harms (p1287).

Suspect renovascular disease when hypertension is difficult to control or renal function deteriorates unexpectedly. Duplex ultrasound can identify a stenosis, but finding an anatomic lesion does not prove it determines the patient's pressure. A renal vein renin ratio of at least 1.5 favors a pressure response to intervention, with substantial uncertainty for an individual. Medical therapy is first-line for stable disease. CORAL found no overall benefit from adding revascularization. Rapid deterioration with bilateral disease or a solitary functioning kidney warrants a different assessment from stable unilateral narrowing (p1287–1289).

## Choosing a modality and managing complications

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-0ceadf0d4044aa67db827f93 mcq-c20bfd8f3c78d3b8b8c85f02" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>

Conventional center hemodialysis requires repeated travel and reliable vascular access. A fistula needs time to mature, while a catheter permits rapid access at the cost of greater bacteremia risk. Peritoneal dialysis allows home treatment and more control over the schedule, but the patient or caregiver must manage exchanges and recognize complications such as peritonitis. Nocturnal or cyclic treatment can accommodate rehabilitation in trained facilities. Assess frailty, function, cognition and infection risk when evaluating transplantation. Comprehensive conservative care includes active treatment of fluid, electrolyte and anemia problems alongside relief of pain, itch, poor sleep and other symptoms (p1291–1294).

Renal anemia typically reflects inadequate erythropoietin and a hypoproliferative marrow. Check for iron deficiency and bleeding, especially when indices differ from the usual normocytic pattern. During erythropoietin treatment, increasing red-cell production can exhaust previously sufficient iron stores. Cardiovascular mortality remains high on dialysis, so assess for active disease and address conventional vascular risks, anemia and fluid overload in the context of the patient's goals (p1294). ## Calcium oxalate arthritis and red-cell morphology in kidney failure

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-39fad91c5b4510f05efdb072" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>When a dialysis patient develops crystal arthritis, ask about vitamin C supplements. The body converts ascorbic acid to oxalate, and dialysis clears it incompletely. Synovial calcium oxalate deposits can therefore provoke arthritis. Sevelamer and lanthanum bind dietary phosphate and do not supply oxalate. [Related Harrison teaching, ch372 p2867](?chapter=83#ch83-harrison-oxalate).

<span data-practice-ids="mcq-28b6d2af7cf87462379ebe90" data-reader-metadata="exam"></span>Separate marrow production from smear appearance. Lack of erythropoietin can produce a hypoproliferative anemia. Uremia can also change the membrane of circulating red cells, producing echinocytes with evenly spaced projections. Howell–Jolly bodies are nuclear remnants within a cell, while target cells have a central area of hemoglobin. [Related Harrison teaching, ch63 p434, Figure 63-10](?chapter=83#ch83-harrison-burr).

## Summary

### CKD — Definition & Diagnosis
- CKD criteria: eGFR **<60 mL/min/1.73 m²** (normal young-adult GFR **~125**; **<15** = kidney failure), albuminuria (ACR **>30 mg/g**), hematuria (any degree), urinary casts, or imaging abnormality — any persisting **>3 months** (p1278).
- CKD is usually asymptomatic until GFR falls to roughly **15–20 mL/min/1.73 m²** (p1278).
- US CKD prevalence **~11.5%** overall (4.8% stages 1–2, 6.7% stages 3–5) but **47%** in adults **>70y** (p1278).

### CKD — Management
- BP target in CKD: **SBP 120–130**, **DBP <90** (p1279); goal of **<130/80** for diabetes + CKD (p1286).
- ACEI/ARB (RAS blockade) is the mainstay in diabetes when DN is suspected; **do not combine ACEI+ARB** (↑ complications) (p1279).
- SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce CKD-progression risk in diabetics; benefit in nondiabetics is still emerging (p1279–1280).
- Statins (± ezetimibe) recommended once eGFR **<60** (p1280).
- Low-protein diet (0.5–0.6 g/kg/d) or very-low (0.3–0.4 g/kg/d) — some evidence it may reduce progression to ESKD in **CKD stage 4–5**; in nondiabetic CKD stage ≤3 it does not appear to reduce progression (p1280).
- Discuss dialysis preferences once eGFR falls **<~20** (p1280).
- Nephrology referral triggers (KCP list, p1278): AKI, red cell casts, refractory HTN, K⁺ abnormalities, recurrent/extensive stones, hereditary kidney disease, **CKD stage 4–5**, severely increased albuminuria. K/DIGO's own restated list (p1290) phrases the GFR trigger as **GFR <30** instead.

### AKI
- Classified by urine output: nonoliguric **>400 mL/day**, oliguric **100–400**, anuric **<100** — nonoliguric has the better prognosis (p1280).
- Categorized prerenal / intrarenal / postrenal (Table 83-1, p1281–1282).
- NSAID use **doubles** AKI risk in adults **>65** (p1282).
- CI-AKI (contrast-induced) risk factors: old age, CKD, diabetes, heart failure, volume depletion, other nephrotoxins (p1282).

### Glomerulonephritis / RPGN
- IgA nephropathy is the most common primary GN worldwide; **30–40%** progress to ESKD within 20 years; ESKD incidence is almost **2×** higher in patients **older than 50** (p1283).
- Postinfectious GN (PIGN): hematuria + proteinuria **10–14 days** post-infection; hypocomplementemia in **>70%**; **~50%** need acute dialysis; only **~20%** achieve complete recovery, **~30%** progress to ESKD (p1283).
- RPGN classification — Table 83-2 (p1284).
- Corticosteroids are the mainstay; cyclophosphamide for crescentic/proliferative disease or life-threatening complications (p1284–1285); rituximab is roughly **equivalent potency** to cyclophosphamide (p1285).

### Nephrotic Syndrome
- Definition: proteinuria **>3 g/day** + hypoalbuminemia + edema + dyslipidemia (p1285).
- Most common biopsy findings in older adults with NS: membranous nephropathy (MN) + minimal change disease (MCD) + amyloidosis = **~60%** combined — MCD **~15%**, MN **~30–40%** of biopsies (p1285).
- Amyloidosis found on **~10%** of biopsies done for presumed idiopathic NS (p1285).
- Treatment: RAS blockade for proteinuria; sodium restriction **<2 g/day** + loop diuretics for edema (p1286).

### Diabetic Nephropathy (DN)
- **~30%** of diabetics develop DN in their lifetime; DN is the leading cause of NS and dialysis-ESKD in the US (p1286).
- Accelerated kidney-function loss once eGFR falls **<45 mL/min/1.73 m²** (p1286).
- ACEI/ARB, SGLT2i, and GLP-1 RA all delay CKD→ESKD progression; BP goal **<130/80** (p1286).

### Acute Interstitial Nephritis (AIN)
- **~80%** of older patients show partial/complete recovery within 6 months (p1287).
- Most cases in older adults are drug-induced (antibiotics, PPIs, NSAIDs, diuretics); stopping the offending drug is the key intervention (p1287).
- Corticosteroids improve outcomes if started within **14 days** of first symptoms (p1287, main text).
- Rare: airway involvement can cause bronchospasm (p1287).

### Renovascular Disease
- Prevalence **2–3%** of general hypertensive population; up to **40%** of refractory hypertension (p1287).
- Atherosclerosis accounts for almost **90%** of renovascular cases in older adults (p1287), and a recognized nonatherosclerotic cause is **[fibromuscular dysplasia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30648921/)**; Hazzard prints “fibrous dysplasia” in the paragraph and in Table 83-3 (p1288).
- Duplex ultrasound sensitivity/specificity: low-to-mid **90%** range (p1288).
- Renal vein renin ratio **≥1.5** between kidneys predicts a beneficial BP response to revascularization; failure to lateralize does *not* predict a negative response; overall BP response cannot be predicted confidently with renal vein renin measurements (p1289).
- CORAL trial (**947** patients): no benefit of revascularization vs. medical management (p1289).
- Up to **50%** of stenoses worsen over time, at up to **5%/year** (p1289).

### ESKD — Epidemiology & Referral
- ESKD/kidney failure = GFR **<15 mL/min/1.73 m²** (p1289).
- **~10–13%** of adults in North America, Europe, and Asia are estimated to have some form of CKD (p1290).
- Historic untreated CKD decline rate **~7–10 mL/min/year** (p1290).
- **~60%** of US ESKD patients have nausea/vomiting at the time dialysis is initiated (p1291).

### Renal Replacement Therapy Choices
- Conventional hemodialysis: **3×/week**, **~4 h**/session; access options are AV fistula (best outcomes), AV graft, or temporary catheter (high bacteremia rates) (p1291–1292).
- AV fistula matures over **~3 months** before it's usable (p1292).
- Peritoneal dialysis: continuous cyclic or nocturnal PD preferred in long-term care (p1293).
- Among patients **>75** placed on a kidney-transplant waitlist, **<40%** receive a transplant within **5 years** (p1293).
- A time-limited dialysis trial is typically predetermined at **4–6 weeks** (p1293).

### ESKD Complications
- Anemia: typically develops around GFR **~35 mL/min**; normochromic, normocytic (p1294). The chapter gives a conservative hemoglobin level near **11.5 g/dL** and misprints the unit as mg/dL (p1294); [KDIGO 2026, recommendation 3.3.1](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline.pdf) advises an ESA treatment target below 11.5 g/dL in adults with CKD.
- Hazzard describes ferritin **<500 ng/mL** or transferrin saturation **<30%** under its historical “functional iron deficiency” wording (p1294). In anemic patients receiving hemodialysis, [KDIGO 2026 recommendation 2.1](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline.pdf) suggests starting iron when **both** ferritin is **≤500 ng/mL** and transferrin saturation is **≤30%**.
- Cardiovascular death risk on dialysis: **10–100×** the general-population risk (p1294).
- Secondary hyperparathyroidism: PTH begins rising by GFR **~40 mL/min** (p1295).
- Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) supplementation for malnutrition: **50 mg/day** (p1295).
- Pruritus affects up to **40%** of ESKD patients; low-dose gabapentin reduces uremic pruritus; **avoid** diphenhydramine/hydroxyzine (anticholinergic burden) (p1296).

### Prognosis & End of Life
- **1-year survival** for octogenarians/nonagenarians after starting dialysis: **50%** per the chapter's own Key Clinical Points (p1278) — see book contradiction below.
- **87%** of nursing-home residents died or had decreased functional status within 1 year of starting hemodialysis (p1296).
- Hospice use is **5-fold lower** among hemodialysis patients than among older non-dialysis patients (p1297).
- The "surprise question" (would you be surprised if this patient died in 6 months?), combined with albumin, age, dementia, and peripheral vascular disease, is a validated mortality predictor (p1296).

### Drugs (name — dose/target — key cautions)
- **Phosphate binders** (calcium acetate, sevelamer, lanthanum carbonate) — for hyperphosphatemia not controlled by diet alone (p1280).
- **Calcitriol** (active vitamin D analog) — for PTH unresponsive to plain vitamin D supplementation; caution: hypercalcemia and hyperphosphatemia (p1280).
- **Pyridoxine** — 50 mg/day for malnutrition (p1295); **gabapentin** — low-dose, for uremic pruritus (p1296); **avoid** diphenhydramine/hydroxyzine in ESKD pruritus (anticholinergic burden) (p1296).

### Book contradiction (exam trap)
- **1-year dialysis survival for octogenarians/nonagenarians is given as two different numbers in the same chapter**: the opening Key Clinical Points box says **50%** (p1278), but the "Prognosis and Survival" section later states **54%** (p1296) for the identical statistic. Both are printed as-is; know which figure a question is quoting.

**3. What blood pressure target is recommended for older adults with CKD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Systolic 120–130 mm Hg, diastolic under 90 mm Hg (p1279).

</details>

**4. At what eGFR does statin therapy become specifically recommended in CKD, and is combining ACEI with ARB advised?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

eGFR under 60 mL/min/1.73 m². Combining ACEI and ARB is generally not advised — it increases complications (p1279–1280).

</details>

**5. How is AKI classified by urine output, and which category has the best prognosis?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Nonoliguric (>400 mL/day), oliguric (100–400 mL/day), anuric (<100 mL/day); nonoliguric AKI has the better prognosis (p1280).

</details>

**7. What proportion of IgA nephropathy patients progress to ESKD within 20 years, and how does age at onset affect ESKD risk?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

30–40% progress to ESKD within 20 years; ESKD incidence is almost twice as high in patients older than 50 (p1283).

</details>

**8. What three diagnoses account for about 60% of kidney-biopsy findings in older adults with nephrotic syndrome?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Membranous nephropathy, minimal change disease, and amyloidosis (p1285).

</details>

**9. At what eGFR does diabetic nephropathy show accelerated loss of kidney function, and what is the BP goal in diabetes plus CKD?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

eGFR below 45 mL/min/1.73 m²; BP goal under 130/80 mm Hg (p1286).

</details>

**10. What proportion of older patients with acute interstitial nephritis recover partially or completely within 6 months, and what is the single most important management step?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 80%; the most important step is stopping the offending drug (p1287).

</details>

**11. What proportion of renovascular disease in older adults is due to atherosclerosis, and what did the CORAL trial show about revascularization?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

About 90%; CORAL (947 patients) showed no benefit of revascularization over medical management (p1287, p1289).

</details>

**13. Around what GFR does renal anemia typically develop, and what type of anemia is it?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Around GFR 35 mL/min; a normochromic, normocytic anemia due to inadequate erythropoietin production (p1294).

</details>

**15. The chapter states two different 1-year survival rates for octogenarians/nonagenarians starting dialysis — what are they, and where does each appear?**

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

50% (Key Clinical Points, p1278) and 54% (Prognosis and Survival section, p1296) — the same statistic printed differently in two places in the same chapter.

</details>
