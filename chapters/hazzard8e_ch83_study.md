# Kidney Diseases

Distinguish a new kidney injury from chronic loss of filtration. Urgency depends on the complication, while mechanism and goals guide longer-term treatment.

## Chronic kidney disease

A single abnormal creatinine cannot establish chronicity. Compare previous values and look for albuminuria, blood or casts on urine testing and structural changes on imaging persisting for more than three months. Albuminuria and reduced filtration each convey prognostic information. In the US estimate quoted, 47% of adults over 70 met CKD criteria, compared with about 11.5% in all adults. Most were identified by filtration or albuminuria, long before symptoms of kidney failure (<a href="?chapter=83#p1277">p1277</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">1279</a>).

CKD criteria: eGFR **<60 mL/min/1.73 m²** (normal young-adult GFR **~125**), albuminuria (ACR **>30 mg/g**), hematuria (any degree), urinary casts, or imaging abnormality — any persisting **>3 months** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>).

Review blood pressure, cardiovascular risk, diabetes control, medication doses, nephrotoxic exposures and episodes of AKI. In proteinuric disease, a single ACE inhibitor or ARB can lower pressure and protein loss. Combining them increases complications. In diabetes, consider kidney-protective therapy alongside glucose control. Weigh dietary protein restriction against frailty and unintentional weight loss. If phosphate remains elevated despite dietary changes, use a binder with meals. Correct low vitamin D when present and assess persistent secondary hyperparathyroidism separately. Progressive decline, significant albuminuria, red-cell casts, persistent potassium abnormalities or difficult hypertension are reasons for earlier nephrology involvement and planning (<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">p1279</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">1280</a>, <a href="?chapter=83#p1286">1286</a>, <a href="?chapter=83#p1290">1290</a>).

CKD is usually asymptomatic until GFR falls to roughly **15–20 mL/min/1.73 m²** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>).

US CKD prevalence **~11.5%** overall (4.8% stages 1–2, 6.7% stages 3–5) but **47%** in adults **>70y** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>).

BP target in CKD: **SBP 120–130**, **DBP <90** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">p1279</a>); goal of **<130/80** for diabetes + CKD (<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">p1286</a>).

> **Current practice: Blood pressure targets in CKD.** KDIGO suggests a standardized office systolic BP below 120 mm Hg if tolerated in adults with hypertension and CKD. A routine unstandardized clinic reading is not equivalent. Choose a less intensive target when symptomatic orthostasis, frailty or limited life expectancy warrants it. [KDIGO CKD guideline, 2024](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf).

ACEI/ARB (RAS blockade) is the mainstay in diabetes when DN is suspected; **do not combine ACEI+ARB** (↑ complications) (<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">p1279</a>).

SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce CKD-progression risk in diabetics; benefit in nondiabetics is still emerging (<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">p1279</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">1280</a>).

> **Current practice: SGLT2 inhibitors in CKD.** KDIGO recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor in CKD with T2D and eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73 m². For adults with CKD, including those without diabetes, strong indications include eGFR ≥20 with urine ACR ≥200 mg/g, or heart failure irrespective of albuminuria. Once started, continue below eGFR 20 if tolerated until kidney replacement therapy. [KDIGO CKD guideline, 2024](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf).

Statins (± ezetimibe) recommended once eGFR **<60** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

Low-protein diet (0.5–0.6 g/kg/d) or very-low (0.3–0.4 g/kg/d) — some evidence it may reduce progression to ESKD in **CKD stage 4–5**; in nondiabetic CKD stage ≤3 it does not appear to reduce progression (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

> **Current practice: Protein targets in frail patients with CKD.** Protein restriction must be individualized in older adults with CKD. KDIGO generally suggests 0.8 g/kg/day in CKD G3–G5, but frailty or sarcopenia can favor higher protein and energy targets. [KDIGO CKD guideline, 2024](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/KDIGO-2024-CKD-Guideline.pdf).

Discuss dialysis preferences once eGFR falls **<~20** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

Nephrology referral triggers (KCP list, <a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>): AKI, red cell casts, refractory HTN, K⁺ abnormalities, recurrent/extensive stones, hereditary kidney disease, **CKD stage 4–5**, severely increased albuminuria. K/DIGO's own restated list (<a href="?chapter=83#p1290">p1290</a>) phrases the GFR trigger as **GFR <30** instead.

## Acute kidney injury

### Localizing acute kidney injury and deciding when dialysis is urgent

An older adult who has taken a diuretic during several days of diarrhea may have a filtration problem before there is structural kidney injury. Ask separately how much fluid the patient has lost and how much blood is actually reaching the kidneys. Severe liver disease can redistribute fluid and lower effective circulating volume. Low cardiac output can do the same even in someone with leg edema and wet lungs. These are the reasons the cirrhosis vignette points to a prerenal mechanism (<a href="?chapter=83#p1281">p1281</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1282">1282</a>).

For an intrinsic lesion, look for evidence of damage within the parenchyma. Hemolytic–uremic syndrome injures small vessels, prolonged seizures can release myoglobin that injures tubules, and pyelonephritis involves the renal interstitium. For postrenal AKI, look for a distended bladder, prostatic obstruction or severe fecal impaction. Unilateral obstruction seldom markedly elevates creatinine when the other kidney works normally. **Both acute heart failure from malignant hypertension and HUS receive credit.** <a href="?chapter=83#book-table-83-1">Table 83-1</a> actually lists the former under prerenal cardiac failure and HUS under intrinsic injury (<a href="?chapter=83#p1281">p1281</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1282">1282</a>).

Categorized prerenal / intrarenal / postrenal (<a href="?chapter=83#book-table-83-1">Table 83-1</a>, <a href="?chapter=83#p1281">p1281</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1282">1282</a>).

CI-AKI (contrast-induced) risk factors: old age, CKD, diabetes, heart failure, volume depletion, other nephrotoxins (<a href="?chapter=83#p1282">p1282</a>).

Check the previous creatinine, course of urine output, medications, urine sediment and the possibility of obstruction. NSAIDs reduce the kidney's ability to maintain inflow during volume depletion. ACE inhibitors and ARBs reduce pressure on the outflow side of the glomerulus. With poor intake, an SGLT2 inhibitor's osmotic diuresis may add to fluid loss despite its long-term kidney benefits. Escitalopram, more characteristically raises concern for hyponatremia (<a href="?chapter=83#p1279">p1279</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1283">1283</a> and empagliflozin and escitalopram prescribing information).

Give isotonic fluid for a demonstrable volume deficit and reassess respiration, circulation and urine output. Drain bladder outlet obstruction with a catheter. In an acutely deteriorating patient, pressure improved after fluids, but oliguria persisted, oxygen saturation fell to 88% and pulmonary edema appeared. The worsening complication was fluid overload. Potassium remained dangerously high and required treatment even though its trajectory and the acidosis had improved. Life-threatening, medically refractory fluid, potassium or acid–base abnormalities and symptomatic uremia are reasons for urgent dialysis. Creatinine alone cannot make that decision (<a href="?chapter=83#p1283">p1283</a>).

Classified by urine output: nonoliguric **>400 mL/day**, oliguric **100–400**, anuric **<100** — nonoliguric has the better prognosis (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

> **Current practice: Diagnostic thresholds for acute kidney injury.** KDIGO defines AKI by a creatinine increase ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, a rise to ≥1.5 times baseline within seven days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for at least six hours. Interpret these changes against the patient’s actual baseline. [KDIGO AKI criteria, 2012](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/KDIGO-2012-AKI-Guideline-English.pdf).

**Phosphate binders** (calcium acetate, sevelamer, lanthanum carbonate) — for hyperphosphatemia not controlled by diet alone (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

**Calcitriol** (active vitamin D analog) — for PTH unresponsive to plain vitamin D supplementation; caution: hypercalcemia and hyperphosphatemia (<a href="?chapter=83#p1280">p1280</a>).

## Glomerular and diabetic disease

Think of heavy proteinuria when an older patient's edema is accompanied by low albumin. Urinalysis can redirect an assessment initially focused on heart failure. In the chapter, nephrotic syndrome includes more than 3 g of urinary protein per day, low albumin, edema and dyslipidemia. Renal vein thrombosis can be a presenting complication. Measure renal function and protein loss and consider nephrology assessment for the underlying glomerular disorder (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>).

Definition: proteinuria **>3 g/day** + hypoalbuminemia + edema + dyslipidemia (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>).

**Diabetic nephropathy** is the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in the general US adult population. In older adults who actually undergo biopsy for isolated nephrotic syndrome, membranous disease accounts for about 30–40% and minimal change disease for about 15%. Typical diabetic nephropathy is often diagnosed without biopsy. A rapid fall in filtration, unusually early heavy proteinuria or absence of expected diabetic microvascular disease should prompt consideration of another disorder. This selection explains why the most common clinical cause and most common biopsy finding differ (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">1286</a>).

IgA nephropathy is the most common primary GN worldwide; **30–40%** progress to ESKD within 20 years; ESKD incidence is almost **2×** higher in patients **older than 50** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1283">p1283</a>).

Postinfectious GN (PIGN): hematuria + proteinuria **10–14 days** post-infection; hypocomplementemia in **>70%**; **~50%** need acute dialysis; only **~20%** achieve complete recovery, **~30%** progress to ESKD (<a href="?chapter=83#p1283">p1283</a>).

RPGN classification — <a href="?chapter=83#book-table-83-2">Table 83-2</a> (<a href="?chapter=83#p1284">p1284</a>).

Corticosteroids are the mainstay; cyclophosphamide for crescentic/proliferative disease or life-threatening complications (<a href="?chapter=83#p1284">p1284</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">1285</a>); rituximab is roughly **equivalent potency** to cyclophosphamide (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>).

Most common biopsy findings in older adults with NS: membranous nephropathy (MN) + minimal change disease (MCD) + amyloidosis = **~60%** combined — MCD **~15%**, MN **~30–40%** of biopsies (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>).

Amyloidosis found on **~10%** of biopsies done for presumed idiopathic NS (<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">p1285</a>).

Treatment: RAS blockade for proteinuria; sodium restriction **<2 g/day** + loop diuretics for edema (<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">p1286</a>).

**~30%** of diabetics develop DN in their lifetime; DN is the leading cause of NS and dialysis-ESKD in the US (<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">p1286</a>).

Accelerated kidney-function loss once eGFR falls **<45 mL/min/1.73 m²** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">p1286</a>).

ACEI/ARB, SGLT2i, and GLP-1 RA all delay CKD→ESKD progression; BP goal **<130/80** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1286">p1286</a>).

## Interstitial and renovascular disease

Hematuria and proteinuria with rapidly rising creatinine should raise concern for rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Ask about recent infection, systemic symptoms, rash and hemoptysis. In older adults, postinfectious GN and small-vessel vasculitis can present with nonspecific fatigue or malaise. Urgent nephrology assessment can guide serology and biopsy. Frailty, bleeding tendency and small echogenic kidneys affect the risk and likely benefit of biopsy. Treatment depends on the underlying lesion, the urgency of preserving kidney function and the risks of immunosuppression (<a href="?chapter=83#p1283">p1283</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1285">1285</a>).

Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis may follow a new antibiotic, proton pump inhibitor, NSAID or diuretic. A thorough exposure history matters when the presentation is simply a rise in creatinine. Withdraw the suspected drug promptly and follow recovery. For persistent or uncertain cases, biopsy and corticosteroid treatment require judgment about diagnostic certainty, severity and harms (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>).

**~80%** of older patients show partial/complete recovery within 6 months (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>).

Most cases in older adults are drug-induced (antibiotics, PPIs, NSAIDs, diuretics); stopping the offending drug is the key intervention (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>).

Suspect renovascular disease when hypertension is difficult to control or renal function deteriorates unexpectedly. Duplex ultrasound can identify a stenosis, but finding an anatomic lesion does not prove it determines the patient's pressure. A renal vein renin ratio of at least 1.5 favors a pressure response to intervention, with substantial uncertainty for an individual. Medical therapy is first-line for stable disease. CORAL found no overall benefit from adding revascularization. Rapid deterioration with bilateral disease or a solitary functioning kidney warrants a different assessment from stable unilateral narrowing (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1289">1289</a>).

Corticosteroids improve outcomes if started within **14 days** of first symptoms (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>, main text).

Rare: airway involvement can cause bronchospasm (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>).

Prevalence **2–3%** of general hypertensive population; up to **40%** of refractory hypertension (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>).

Atherosclerosis accounts for almost **90%** of renovascular disease in the geriatric population. Hazzard calls the other cause **“fibrous dysplasia”** in the prose (<a href="?chapter=83#p1287">p1287</a>) and <a href="?chapter=83#book-table-83-3">Table 83-3</a> (<a href="?chapter=83#p1288">p1288</a>).

> **Current practice: Fibromuscular dysplasia.** the accepted vascular diagnosis is **fibromuscular dysplasia** — [International consensus, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30648921/).

Duplex ultrasound sensitivity/specificity: low-to-mid **90%** range (<a href="?chapter=83#p1288">p1288</a>).

Renal vein renin ratio **≥1.5** between kidneys predicts a beneficial BP response to revascularization; failure to lateralize does *not* predict a negative response; overall BP response cannot be predicted confidently with renal vein renin measurements (<a href="?chapter=83#p1289">p1289</a>).

CORAL trial (**947** patients): no benefit of revascularization vs. medical management (<a href="?chapter=83#p1289">p1289</a>).

Up to **50%** of stenoses worsen over time, at up to **5%/year** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1289">p1289</a>).

## Dialysis, complications and goals of care

### Itch, mineral disease and nutrition during dialysis

An older patient may scratch through the night despite regular hemodialysis. Examine for dry skin or a drug eruption, review dialysis adequacy and phosphate control, correct iron deficiency when present, and protect and moisturize the skin. Low-dose gabapentin can help persistent uremic itch. Reassess for sedation and impaired balance in someone already at risk of falling. Sedating anticholinergic antihistamines add cognitive and functional burden. Reducing dialysis frequency or increasing an analgesic opioid does not address the renal mechanism (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>).

Pruritus affects up to **40%** of ESKD patients; low-dose gabapentin reduces uremic pruritus; **avoid** diphenhydramine/hydroxyzine (anticholinergic burden) (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>).

Phosphate retention and reduced production of active vitamin D stimulate PTH release. Older dialysis patients experience more bone pain and pathological fractures, and older women may have less bone mineral than younger women with similar dialysis duration. These observations do not imply greater hyperphosphatemia or more severe renal osteodystrophy at older ages. Active vitamin D concentrations showed no correlation with age in the group described. Osteoporosis can coexist with renal osteodystrophy. A history of falls alone does not identify the bone disorder or establish an indication for alendronate (<a href="?chapter=83#p1295">p1295</a>).

Secondary hyperparathyroidism: PTH begins rising by GFR **~40 mL/min** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1295">p1295</a>).

Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) supplementation for malnutrition: **50 mg/day** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1295">p1295</a>).

For new loss of function, ask who buys food, who prepares it, whether the patient can eat it, and whether nausea followed a change in dialysis. A list of sodium, phosphate, potassium and fluid restrictions may leave very little the person will actually eat. Follow weight trends, dietary intake and albumin together. Low albumin by itself is insufficient to diagnose malnutrition. A renal dietitian can find acceptable substitutions while protecting energy and protein intake. Look for weakness, falls, sarcopenia, cognitive change and depression as contributors to declining independence (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a>, <a href="?chapter=83#p1295">1295</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">1296</a>).

### Starting, trialing or withdrawing dialysis

In a patient with advanced kidney disease, anorexia, fatigue and nausea are familiar manifestations of uremia. Behavioral change, new confusion, unexplained heart failure or loss of function may be the presenting clue in an older adult. Establish the previous cognitive and functional baseline and assess other reversible contributors. A practice case pairs new aggression with an eGFR of 22: stop metformin below 30 mL/min/1.73 m² and evaluate the behavior on its own merits (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a> and Chapter 99 <a href="?chapter=99#p1577">p1577</a>, <a href="?chapter=99#book-table-99-11">Table 99-11</a>).

ESKD/kidney failure = GFR **<15 mL/min/1.73 m²** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1289">p1289</a>).

Kidney failure is defined by a filtration threshold, while the decision to start dialysis also depends on symptoms, difficult-to-control complications and the patient's goals. When advanced dementia and uremic delirium are hard to distinguish, agree on the purpose and duration of a trial. The chapter allows 3–4 weeks for uremic cognitive symptoms to clear and describes a planned 4–6 week treatment trial with explicit goals. Decide how the team will assess comfort, cognition or a hoped-for activity before treatment starts (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a>, <a href="?chapter=83#p1293">1293</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">1294</a>).

Among patients **>75** placed on a kidney-transplant waitlist, **<40%** receive a transplant within **5 years** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1293">p1293</a>).

A time-limited dialysis trial is typically predetermined at **4–6 weeks** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1293">p1293</a>).

A cognitively intact patient may ask to stop hemodialysis. Begin by asking what has become intolerable. Explore depression, pain, transport burden or loss of independence and assess understanding of the likely consequences. Document decision-making capacity, involve people the patient wants present and arrange symptom and end-of-life care if he chooses withdrawal. Frailty, nursing-home residence, albumin and the clinician's estimate of survival can inform this discussion, but they cannot tell the patient how to value treatment time, function or independence (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1297">1297</a>).

**87%** of nursing-home residents died or had decreased functional status within 1 year of starting hemodialysis (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>).

Hospice use is **5-fold lower** among hemodialysis patients than among older non-dialysis patients (<a href="?chapter=83#p1297">p1297</a>).

The "surprise question" (would you be surprised if this patient died in 6 months?), combined with albumin, age, dementia, and peripheral vascular disease, is a validated mortality predictor (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>).

**1-year dialysis survival for octogenarians/nonagenarians is given as two different numbers in the same chapter**: the opening Key Clinical Points box says **50%** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>), but the "Prognosis and Survival" section later states **54%** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1296">p1296</a>) for the identical statistic. Both are printed as-is; know which figure a question is quoting.

### Choosing a modality and managing complications

Conventional center hemodialysis requires repeated travel and reliable vascular access. A fistula needs time to mature, while a catheter permits rapid access at the cost of greater bacteremia risk. Peritoneal dialysis allows home treatment and more control over the schedule, but the patient or caregiver must manage exchanges and recognize complications such as peritonitis. Nocturnal or cyclic treatment can accommodate rehabilitation in trained facilities. Assess frailty, function, cognition and infection risk when evaluating transplantation. Comprehensive conservative care includes active treatment of fluid, electrolyte and anemia problems alongside relief of pain, itch, poor sleep and other symptoms (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">1294</a>).

**~60%** of US ESKD patients have nausea/vomiting at the time dialysis is initiated (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a>).

Conventional hemodialysis: **3×/week**, **~4 h**/session; access options are AV fistula (best outcomes), AV graft, or temporary catheter (high bacteremia rates) (<a href="?chapter=83#p1291">p1291</a>–<a href="?chapter=83#p1292">1292</a>).

Peritoneal dialysis: continuous cyclic or nocturnal PD preferred in long-term care (<a href="?chapter=83#p1293">p1293</a>).

Renal anemia typically reflects inadequate erythropoietin and a hypoproliferative marrow. Check for iron deficiency and bleeding, especially when indices differ from the usual normocytic pattern. During erythropoietin treatment, increasing red-cell production can exhaust previously sufficient iron stores. Cardiovascular mortality remains high on dialysis, so assess for active disease and address conventional vascular risks, anemia and fluid overload in the context of the patient's goals (<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">p1294</a>). Iron status matters when interpreting the hemoglobin response to erythropoietin.

Anemia in CKD is typically normochromic and normocytic and develops around GFR **35 mL/min**. Hazzard prints a conservative hemoglobin target “near **11.5 mg/dL**” (<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">p1294</a>). *[sic — hemoglobin is measured in g/dL here.]*

> **Current practice: Hemoglobin target with an ESA.** in adults receiving an ESA, target hemoglobin **below 11.5 g/dL** — [KDIGO 2026, recommendation 3.3.1](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline.pdf).

Hazzard describes ferritin **<500 ng/mL** or transferrin saturation **<30%** under its historical “functional iron deficiency” wording (<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">p1294</a>).

> **Current practice: Iron treatment during hemodialysis.** for anemic patients receiving hemodialysis, [KDIGO 2026 recommendation 2.1](https://kdigo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/KDIGO-2026-Anemia-in-CKD-Guideline.pdf) suggests starting iron when **both** ferritin is **≤500 ng/mL** and transferrin saturation is **≤30%**.

Cardiovascular death risk on dialysis: **10–100×** the general-population risk (<a href="?chapter=83#p1294">p1294</a>).

### Calcium oxalate arthritis and red-cell morphology in kidney failure

When a dialysis patient develops crystal arthritis, ask about vitamin C supplements. The body converts ascorbic acid to oxalate, and dialysis clears it incompletely. Synovial calcium oxalate deposits can therefore provoke arthritis. Sevelamer and lanthanum bind dietary phosphate and do not supply oxalate. [Related Harrison teaching, ch372 p2867](?chapter=83#ch83-harrison-oxalate).

Separate marrow production from smear appearance. Lack of erythropoietin can produce a hypoproliferative anemia. Uremia can also change the membrane of circulating red cells, producing echinocytes with evenly spaced projections. Howell–Jolly bodies are nuclear remnants within a cell, while target cells have a central area of hemoglobin. [Related Harrison teaching, ch63 p434, Figure 63-10](?chapter=83#ch83-harrison-burr).

**~10–13%** of adults in North America, Europe, and Asia are estimated to have some form of CKD (<a href="?chapter=83#p1290">p1290</a>).

AV fistula matures over **~3 months** before it's usable (<a href="?chapter=83#p1292">p1292</a>).

**1-year survival** for octogenarians/nonagenarians after starting dialysis: **50%** per the chapter's own Key Clinical Points (<a href="?chapter=83#p1278">p1278</a>) — the text and table differ.

## Key numbers

- NSAID use **doubles** AKI risk in adults **>65** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1282">p1282</a>).

- Historic untreated CKD decline rate **~7–10 mL/min/year** (<a href="?chapter=83#p1290">p1290</a>).

בהסבר משרד הבריאות על חוק החולה הנוטה למות מובחנים שני מצבים בסוף החיים. כאשר מתקיימים תנאי החוק, אפשר להימנע מחידוש טיפול מחזורי שיש לו נקודת סיום ברורה. אסור להפסיק טיפול רציף אם ההפסקה עלולה להביא למות החולה. כשאדם יכול להביע את רצונו, מבררים אותו ישירות. אצל מטופל המבקש לוותר על דיאליזה חשוב לברר גם את סיבת הבקשה ואת הסבל שאפשר להקל, כפי שמתואר בפרק בעמ׳ 1296–1297. [משרד הבריאות, המתת חסד, 25.05.2026, הסעיפים ״מה החוק לגבי המתת חסד בישראל״ ו״מה מותר ואסור לעשות במקרה של מחלה סופנית״](https://me.health.gov.il/older-adult/services-rights/before-saying-goodbye/euthanasia/)
